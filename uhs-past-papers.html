Unit I: Introduction to Pharmacology
Q1. Name the factors affecting the rate of absorption and distribution. (2021 Annual)

Factors Affecting Absorption:

  1. Area of absorptive surface: A larger surface area (such as the microvilli of the small intestine) greatly increases the rate of drug absorption.
  2. Route of administration: Intravenous administration bypasses absorption entirely, whereas oral or topical routes depend on membrane barriers.
  3. Vascularity (Blood flow): High blood flow at the site of administration maintains a steep concentration gradient, enhancing absorption.
  4. pH environment: Non-ionized (lipophilic) drugs pass through lipid cell membranes much faster than ionized drugs depending on the local pH and drug pKa.
  5. Presence of other substances: Food, heavy metals, or chelating agents in the GI tract can bind drugs and alter their absorption rate.
  6. Gastrointestinal motility: Rapid peristalsis may decrease absorption time, whereas delayed gastric emptying generally slows absorption.
  7. Functional integrity of absorptive surface: Damage or disease of the mucosal lining impairs absorption.
  8. Disease states: Conditions like malabsorption syndromes alter drug uptake.

Factors Affecting Distribution:

  1. Cardiac output and local blood flow: Organs with high perfusion (brain, liver, kidneys) receive drugs much faster than poorly perfused tissues (adipose tissue, skin).
  2. Capillary permeability: Structural differences in capillary endothelial junctions (e.g., blood-brain barrier vs. liver sinusoids) affect drug exit from circulation.
  3. Size of the organ: Larger tissue mass impacts the extent of distribution.
  4. Binding of drugs to plasma proteins: Only free (unbound) drugs are pharmacologically active and able to cross membranes; albumin binding restricts distribution.
  5. Lipid solubility: Highly lipophilic drugs easily cross cell membranes and accumulate in adipose tissue.
Q2. Define bioavailability. Discuss the factors that influence bioavailability. What do you mean by the term zero-order kinetics of elimination? (2020 Supplementary & 2019 Supplementary)

Definition of Bioavailability:

Bioavailability is defined as the rate and extent to which an administered drug reaches the systemic circulation in an unchanged form.

Factors Influencing Bioavailability:

  1. First-pass hepatic metabolism: Drugs absorbed from the GI tract pass through the portal circulation to the liver, where a significant fraction may be metabolized before reaching systemic circulation.
  2. Solubility of drugs: Highly hydrophilic drugs cannot cross lipid cell membranes easily, while extremely hydrophobic drugs are insoluble in aqueous body fluids; both limit absorption.
  3. Chemical instability: Certain drugs (such as insulin or penicillin G) are unstable in the acidic pH of the stomach or degraded by digestive enzymes.
  4. Nature of the drug formulation: Particle size, crystal form, binders, and coatings (enteric or sustained release) greatly influence dissolution and absorption rates.

Zero-Order Kinetics of Elimination:

If a constant amount of drug is metabolized and eliminated per unit of time, the rate of elimination is constant and does not depend on the drug concentration. This is called zero-order kinetics (also called nonlinear kinetics). The elimination rate is independent of plasma concentration. Examples include ethanol, phenytoin, and high-dose aspirin.

Q3. a) Define the following terms as per "Drug Act 1976 of Pakistan": i. Spurious drugs ii. Counterfeit drug iii. Generic name.
b) Enumerate four factors influencing bioavailability. (2023 Fall / 2025 Final Term)

a) Definitions as per "Drug Act 1976 of Pakistan":

  1. Spurious Drug: A drug that mimics another drug in appearance, label, or packaging, but contains no active pharmaceutical ingredient, contains a different drug, or is manufactured under a false name to deceive the public.
  2. Counterfeit Drug: A drug whose container, label, or manufacturing details deliberately misrepresent its true identity, origin, or manufacturer without authorization.
  3. Generic Name: The official, non-proprietary scientific name of a drug substance accepted internationally, distinct from any brand or trade name given by a specific pharmaceutical manufacturer.

b) Four Factors Influencing Bioavailability:

  1. First-pass hepatic metabolism: Metabolism of an orally administered drug in the liver before it reaches systemic circulation reduces its active fraction.
  2. Solubility and dissolution rate: Extremely hydrophilic or hydrophobic drug formulations absorb poorly compared to balanced lipophilic compounds.
  3. Chemical stability in the GI tract: Degradation by stomach acid or digestive enzymes reduces active drug availability.
  4. Nature of drug formulation: Particle size, disintegration rate, binders, and special coatings alter how fast a drug dissolves for uptake.
Q4. Enlist three merits and demerits of oral route of drug administration. Define volume of distribution. How is it calculated? (2019 Annual)

Merits of Oral Route:

  • Safest route of drug administration.
  • Most common and convenient route for patients.
  • Economical and painless, requiring no special equipment.

Demerits of Oral Route:

  • Limited absorption of certain drugs due to food or chemical characteristics.
  • First-pass metabolism in the liver can significantly reduce active drug levels.
  • Patient compliance is necessary; cannot be used in unconscious or vomiting patients.

Volume of Distribution (Vd):

The apparent volume of distribution is defined as the fluid volume that is required to contain the entire drug in the body at the same concentration measured in the plasma, or a hypothetical volume required to contain the drug homogeneously at the same concentration measured in blood or plasma.

Calculation:

It is calculated by dividing the total amount of drug in the body by the plasma concentration at time zero.

Formula: Vd = Amount of Drug in the Body / Plasma Concentration at Time Zero

Q5. Enlist four names of routes of drug administration. Define receptor and enlist different receptor families or signaling mechanisms. (2018 Supplementary)

Routes of Drug Administration:

  1. Oral route (given by mouth)
  2. Subcutaneous route (into the subcutaneous tissue just below the skin)
  3. Intramuscular (IM) route (into muscle)
  4. Intravenous (IV) route (directly into a vein)

Definition of Receptor:

Receptors are specific macromolecular components in a biologic system with which drugs interact to produce changes in the function of the system.

Major Receptor Families / Signaling Mechanisms:

  1. Transmembrane ligand-gated ion channels: Increase transmembrane conductance of ions and mediate rapid synaptic transmission (e.g., Nicotinic receptors).
  2. Transmembrane G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs): Activate G proteins (Gs, Gi, Gq), which in turn regulate intracellular second messengers like cAMP or IP3.
  3. Enzyme-linked receptors: Possess intracellular enzymatic activity linked to extracellular binding domains (e.g., Receptor tyrosine kinases for insulin and growth factors).
  4. Intracellular receptors: Lipid-soluble ligands cross the cell membrane to bind intracellular receptors that regulate gene transcription (e.g., Steroid and thyroid hormone receptors).

Q7. What is the First Pass Effect? Give its relationship with bioavailability. Differentiate first order kinetics with zero order kinetics. (2016 Annual)

First Pass Effect:

When a drug is absorbed from the gastrointestinal tract, it enters the portal circulation before entering the systemic circulation. If the drug is rapidly metabolized in the liver or gut wall during this initial passage, the amount of unchanged drug entering the systemic circulation is substantially decreased. This is referred to as first-pass metabolism.

Relationship with Bioavailability:

Extensive first-pass metabolism extracts a large portion of the active drug before it reaches systemic targets, directly reducing bioavailability and potentially resulting in subtherapeutic clinical action.

Difference between First-Order Kinetics and Zero-Order Kinetics:

Feature First-Order Kinetics Zero-Order Kinetics
Rate of Elimination Proportional to drug concentration (varies with concentration). Constant amount eliminated per unit time (independent of concentration).
Graph Representation Curved exponential graph on linear scale. Linear straight-line graph.
Saturation Metabolizing enzymes are not saturated. Metabolizing enzymes are saturated.
Q8. Discuss major drug receptor families. (2014 Annual)

Receptors are categorized into four major superfamilies based on their molecular structure and transduction mechanisms:

  1. G-Protein Coupled Receptors (GPCRs): Comprise an extracellular ligand-binding domain coupled to intracellular G-proteins (Gs, Gi, Gq). Agonist binding causes GDP-GTP exchange on the alpha subunit, triggering downstream second messenger cascades.
  2. Transmembrane Receptor Tyrosine Kinases: Feature extracellular ligand-binding domains linked via a peptide chain to an intracellular kinase domain. Ligand binding induces dimerization and autophosphorylation of tyrosine residues, stimulating growth and metabolic pathways (e.g., Insulin receptor).
  3. Cytokine Receptors: Receptors lacking intrinsic enzymatic activity that recruit separate intracellular tyrosine kinase enzymes like JAK (Janus Kinase) to phosphorylate STAT proteins for transcriptional regulation.
  4. Ligand-Gated Ion Channels: Channel proteins that open upon agonist binding, rapidly altering transmembrane ion conductance and cellular polarization (e.g., Nicotinic acetylcholine receptors).
  5. Intracellular Receptors: Located within the cytoplasm or nucleus, activated by lipophilic ligands that diffuse across membranes to modify gene expression with a characteristic delayed onset.
Q9. Write advantages, disadvantages, indications, and risks of the parenteral route. (2014 Annual & 2008 Annual)

Advantages of Parenteral Route:

  • Immediate physiological response can be achieved.
  • Essential for drugs that are poorly absorbed orally or destroyed by digestive secretions (e.g., insulin).
  • Ideal for uncooperative, nauseated, or unconscious patients.
  • Allows precise control over circulating drug concentrations.

Disadvantages of Parenteral Route:

  • Requires trained personnel and aseptic technique.
  • Injections can be painful and cause local tissue irritation.
  • Once administered, drug action is difficult to reverse in cases of toxicity.
  • Manufacturing and packaging requirements make parenteral formulations more expensive.

Indications:

  • Emergency situations requiring rapid onset of action.
  • Patients unable to take oral medications.
  • Drugs with very low oral bioavailability.

Risks of Intravenous Administration:

  • Infection and sepsis at the injection site.
  • Direct damage to veins and surrounding vascular tissue.
  • Risk of air embolism.
  • Thrombophlebitis and blood clot formation.
Q10. Define antagonist and discuss different types of antagonism with examples. (2007 Annual & 2017 Supplementary)

Definition of Antagonist:

An antagonist is a drug that has affinity for a receptor but possesses no intrinsic activity. It produces no biological effect in the absence of an agonist, but decreases or prevents the action of an agonist.

Types of Antagonism:

  1. Physical Antagonism: Based on the physical property of a drug (e.g., activated charcoal binding to ingested alkaloids in the GI tract to prevent systemic absorption).
  2. Chemical Antagonism: A chemical reaction between two compounds that neutralizes the active drug without receptor involvement (e.g., chelating agents binding toxic heavy metals).
  3. Physiological / Functional Antagonism: Two agonist drugs act on completely independent receptors and physiological systems to produce opposing effects (e.g., adrenaline causing bronchodilation via beta receptors versus histamine causing bronchoconstriction via H1 receptors).
  4. Pharmacological Antagonism: Competes for or blocks the same receptor site as the agonist.
    • Competitive (Reversible) Antagonism: Binds reversibly to the receptor site; can be overcome by increasing the concentration of the agonist.
    • Non-competitive (Irreversible) Antagonism: Binds covalently or to an allosteric site on the receptor, permanently preventing agonist binding regardless of concentration.
Q11. Define Therapeutic Index, its clinical importance, and antibiotics avoided in older adults. (2009 Annual)

Therapeutic Index (TI):

The therapeutic index is the ratio of the toxic dose (TD50) to the effective dose (ED50), serving as a quantitative indicator of a drug's safety margin in achieving desired therapeutic effects without toxicity.

Formula: Therapeutic Index = TD50 / ED50

Clinical Importance of Therapeutic Index:

A large therapeutic index indicates a wide margin of safety between therapeutic and toxic levels. Conversely, drugs with a narrow therapeutic index (such as digoxin) require strict patient monitoring and blood level testing to prevent severe toxicity.

Antibiotics Avoided in Older Adults and Reasons:

  1. Ciprofloxacin (Fluoroquinolones): Avoided or used with extreme caution in older adults due to elevated risks of tendinitis, tendon rupture, and central nervous system disturbances.
  2. Augmentin (Amoxicillin/Clavulanic acid): Increased risk of cholestatic jaundice and hepatic dysfunction in older patients, alongside prolonged gastrointestinal adverse effects.
Unit II: Anti-Infective & Antimicrobial Drugs (Antibacterial, Antiviral, Antifungal, Antimalarial)
Q1. a) Resistance to antibiotics is a very serious concern among microbiologists and physicians. Write down three mechanisms for the resistance of penicillin along with their brief description.
b) Acyclovir is prescribed to treat herpes virus infection. Write down its recognised adverse effects (one for Topical & one for IV use) and mechanism of resistance. (2023 Fall / 2025 Final Term)

a) Three Mechanisms of Penicillin Resistance:

  1. Enzymatic Inactivation (Beta-lactamase production): Bacteria produce beta-lactamase (penicillinase) enzymes that cleave the essential beta-lactam ring of penicillin, rendering the antibiotic inactive before it reaches its cellular target.
  2. Altered Target Site (Modified Penicillin-Binding Proteins - PBPs): Mutations lead to structural alterations in penicillin-binding proteins (PBPs), significantly reducing penicillin's binding affinity (e.g., Methicillin-Resistant Staphylococcus aureus - MRSA).
  3. Decreased Permeability / Efflux Pumps: Gram-negative bacteria reduce outer membrane porin channel expression to block penicillin entry or use active efflux pumps to pump the antibiotic out of the bacterial cell.

b) Acyclovir - Adverse Effects & Mechanism of Resistance:

  • Recognised Adverse Effects:
    • Topical Use: Localized mucosal burning, stinging sensation, or transient mild irritation.
    • Intravenous (IV) Use: Crystalline nephrotoxicity (renal impairment due to drug crystallization in renal tubules) or neurotoxicity (confusion, tremors).
  • Mechanism of Resistance:
    • Primary resistance arises from mutations causing deficiency or structural modification of viral Thymidine Kinase (TK), preventing initial phosphorylation of acyclovir into its active monophosphate form.
    • Less commonly, mutations alter viral DNA polymerase, reducing acyclovir triphosphate binding affinity.
Q2. a) Write down the mechanism of action of Amphotericin-B and Flucytosine.
b) Prescribe a drug that is effective against gametocytic form of malarial parasite. Also write down its mechanism of action along with its two adverse effects. (2023 Fall / 2025 Final Term)

a) Mechanism of Action of Antifungal Agents:

  • Amphotericin-B: Binds specifically to ergosterol in the fungal cell membrane, forming transmembrane artificial pores. This leakage of intracellular ions (especially K+ and Mg2+) causes cell disruption and fungal cell death.
  • Flucytosine (5-FC): Transported into fungal cells by cytosine permease and converted into 5-fluorouracil (5-FU) by cytosine deaminase. It inhibits thymidylate synthase and incorporates into fungal RNA, selectively disrupting fungal DNA and protein synthesis.

b) Gametocidal Antimalarial Drug - Primaquine:

  • Prescribed Drug: Primaquine (Highly effective against the gametocytic stage of all Plasmodium species, preventing malaria transmission).
  • Mechanism of Action: Generates reactive oxygen species (ROS) and interferes with electron transport in parasite mitochondria, destroying intracellular respiratory structures.
  • Two Adverse Effects:
    1. Acute hemolytic anemia in patients with Glucose-6-Phosphate Dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency.
    2. Gastrointestinal distress (abdominal cramps, nausea, and epigastric distress).
Q3. Classify antimalarial drugs based on their clinical use and life cycle stages. (2021 Annual)

Antimalarial drugs are classified according to their activity against specific stages of the Plasmodium parasite life cycle:

  1. Tissue Schizonticides (Prophylactic agents): Act on pre-erythrocytic liver stages to prevent infection (e.g., Primaquine, Atovaquone).
  2. Blood Schizonticides (Suppressive / Curative agents): Eliminate asexual parasites in red blood cells to treat clinical malaria attacks (e.g., Chloroquine, Artemisinin derivatives, Quinine).
  3. Gametocides: Destroy sexual stage parasites in the blood to prevent transmission to mosquitoes (e.g., Primaquine).
  4. Radical Cure Agents: Eradicate dormant liver-stage hypnozoites of Plasmodium vivax and ovale (e.g., Primaquine).
Q4. Discuss the mechanism of action, therapeutic uses, and adverse effects of Chloroquine. (2020 Annual)

Mechanism of Action:

Chloroquine concentrates within the acidic food vacuoles of the malaria parasite. It inhibits the enzyme heme polymerase, preventing the conversion of toxic free heme into insoluble hemozoin. The accumulation of free heme results in parasite lysis and death.

Therapeutic Uses:

  • Treatment of acute uncomplicated malaria caused by susceptible strains of Plasmodium species.
  • Chemoprophylaxis in sensitive malarial endemic regions.
  • Anti-inflammatory properties utilized in autoimmune disorders like rheumatoid arthritis and systemic lupus erythematosus.

Adverse Effects:

  • Gastrointestinal distress, nausea, and abdominal cramps.
  • Headache, dizziness, and mild blurred vision.
  • Long-term use can cause retinopathy and corneal deposits.
  • Pruritus, especially in darker-skinned patient populations.
Q5. Write a note on Artemisinin-based combination therapies (ACTs). (2021 Supplementary)

Artemisinin-based combination therapies are currently recommended by health organizations as first-line treatment for uncomplicated Plasmodium falciparum malaria.

  • Combination Rationale: Combines a fast-acting artemisinin derivative (e.g., Artemether, Artesunate) with a longer-acting partner drug (e.g., Lumefantrine, Amodiaquine) to ensure complete parasite clearance and prevent drug resistance.
  • Mechanism: Artemisinins contain an endoperoxide bridge that interacts with intracellular iron to generate reactive free radicals that damage parasite proteins and membranes.
  • Benefits: Rapid symptom relief, high cure rates, and reduced risk of treatment failure.
Unit III: Drugs Affecting the Gastrointestinal System
Q1. a) Describe briefly the mechanism of action and two clinical uses of H2 receptor blockers.
b) Enumerate the drugs used in quadruple regimen for treatment of H.pylori infection. (2023 Fall / 2025 Final Term)

a) H2-Receptor Blockers (e.g., Ranitidine, Famotidine):

  • Mechanism of Action: Competitively block histamine H2-receptors located on parietal cells of the gastric mucosa. This inhibits histamine-stimulated basal and nocturnal gastric acid secretion, as well as reducing acid output triggered by food or gastrin.
  • Two Clinical Uses:
    1. Treatment and prevention of Peptic Ulcer Disease (Gastric and Duodenal ulcers).
    2. Symptomatic relief of Gastroesophageal Reflux Disease (GERD) and erosive esophagitis.

b) Quadruple Regimen for Helicobacter pylori Infection:

Bismuth-based Quadruple Therapy administered for 10–14 days consists of:

  1. Proton Pump Inhibitor (PPI): Omeprazole or Pantoprazole (twice daily).
  2. Bismuth Subsalicylate / Subcitrate: Mucosal protective agent.
  3. Metronidazole: Antimicrobial coverage against anaerobes/H. pylori.
  4. Tetracycline: Protein synthesis inhibitor antibiotic.
Q2. Classify anti-ulcer drugs used in Peptic Ulcer Disease (PUD). (2021 Annual)

Anti-ulcer drugs are categorized based on their mechanism of reducing gastric acidity or protecting the mucosal lining:

  1. Proton Pump Inhibitors (PPIs): Omeprazole, Esomeprazole, Pantoprazole.
  2. H2-Receptor Antagonists: Ranitidine, Famotidine.
  3. Antacids: Aluminum hydroxide, Magnesium hydroxide, Calcium carbonate.
  4. Mucosal Protective Agents: Sucralfate, Bismuth subsalicylate, Misoprostol.
  5. Anti-Helicobacter pylori Regimens: Combinations of antibiotics (Amoxicillin, Clarithromycin, Metronidazole) with PPIs.
Q3. Discuss the mechanism of action and clinical uses of Proton Pump Inhibitors (PPIs). (2020 Annual)

Mechanism of Action:

Proton pump inhibitors are prodrugs that diffuse into the secretory canaliculi of parietal cells, where they are converted into active sulfenamide forms in an acidic environment. This active form binds irreversibly to the hydrogen-potassium ATPase pump enzyme, halting gastric acid secretion until new enzyme pumps are synthesized.

Clinical Uses:

  • Treatment and prevention of gastric and duodenal ulcers.
  • Gastroesophageal Reflux Disease (GERD) and erosive esophagitis.
  • Zollinger-Ellison syndrome and pathological hypersecretory states.
  • Eradication protocols for Helicobacter pylori infections.

  2. Dopamine Receptor Antagonists: Metoclopramide, Domperidone, Prochlorperazine.
  3. H1 Antihistamines and Anticholinergics: Dimenhydrinate, Promethazine, Scopolamine.
  4. Neurokinin-1 (NK1) Receptor Antagonists: Aprepitant.
  5. Corticosteroids: Dexamethasone (used as an adjuvant).

Clinical Indications:

Q5. Write a note on laxatives and cathartics with their classification. (2018 Annual)

Laxatives and cathartics are agents used to facilitate defecation and relieve constipation by softening stool or stimulating bowel motility.

Classification:

  1. Bulk-Forming Laxatives: Dietary fiber, Psyllium, Methylcellulose (absorb water to form a gel, stimulating peristalsis).
  2. Osmotic Laxatives: Magnesium hydroxide, Polyethylene glycol, Lactulose (draw water into the intestinal lumen via osmotic pressure).
  3. Stimulant Laxatives: Senna, Bisacodyl (directly stimulate enteric nerves and intestinal mucosal secretion).
  4. Stool Softeners (Emollients): Docusate sodium (lower surface tension to allow water and lipids to penetrate stool).
  5. Lubricant Laxatives: Mineral oil (lubricates stool and intestinal mucosa).

Q2. Classify anticoagulants and discuss the mechanism of action of Heparin. (2021 Annual)

Classification of Anticoagulants:

  1. Parenteral Anticoagulants: Unfractionated Heparin (UFH), Low Molecular Weight Heparins (Enoxaparin), Fondaparinux.
  2. Oral Anticoagulants:
    • Vitamin K Antagonists: Warfarin.
    • Direct Oral Anticoagulants (DOACs): Dabigatran, Rivaroxaban, Apixaban.

Mechanism of Action of Heparin:

Heparin binds to plasma antithrombin III via a specific pentasaccharide sequence. This binding induces a conformational change in antithrombin III, accelerating its ability to inactive clotting factors, primarily thrombin (Factor IIa) and Factor Xa, thereby preventing fibrin clot formation.

Q5. Write a note on thrombolytics (fibrinolytics) and their role in acute myocardial infarction. (2018 Annual)

Thrombolytic agents are powerful medications designed to dissolve existing blood clots rapidly and restore blood flow through occluded vessels.

  • Mechanism of Action: They act directly or indirectly as serine proteases to convert inactive plasminogen into active plasmin, which then cleaves fibrin matrices within thrombi.
  • Common Agents: Alteplase (tPA), Streptokinase, Reteplase.
  • Role in Acute Myocardial Infarction (AMI): Administered during acute coronary occlusion to achieve prompt coronary reperfusion, salvage ischemic myocardial tissue, and reduce mortality, especially when primary percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) is unavailable.
  • Major Risk: Bleeding complications and intracranial hemorrhage.

Q3. Discuss the mechanism of action and adverse effects of Alkylating Agents. (2020 Annual)

Mechanism of Action:

Alkylating agents work by transferring alkyl groups to DNA nucleotide bases (specifically the N7 position of guanine). This causes cross-linking of DNA strands, abnormal DNA base pairing, and single- or double-strand breaks, which inhibit DNA replication, transcription, and trigger cellular apoptosis in rapidly dividing cancer cells.

Adverse Effects:

  • Bone marrow suppression leading to neutropenia, thrombocytopenia, and anemia.
  • Gastrointestinal mucosal damage resulting in severe nausea, vomiting, and stomatitis.
  • Alopecia (hair loss) due to damage to hair follicle cells.
  • Gonadal dysfunction, infertility, and secondary malignancies.
  • Cystitis and hemorrhagic cystitis (specifically associated with cyclophosphamide).
Q4. Write a comprehensive note on Antimetabolites used in cancer chemotherapy. (2019 Supplementary)

Antimetabolites are structural analogs of normal cellular metabolites involved in DNA and RNA synthesis. They disrupt cell division during the S-phase of the cell cycle.

Key Subclasses and Examples:

  1. Folic Acid Analogs (Methotrexate): Inhibit dihydrofolate reductase, preventing the synthesis of reduced folates required for purine and thymidine synthesis.
  2. Pyrimidine Analogs (5-Fluorouracil, Cytarabine): Inhibit thymidylate synthase or incorporate into RNA and DNA to halt nucleic acid elongation.
  3. Purine Analogs (6-Mercaptopurine): Block purine nucleotide synthesis pathways.

Clinical Applications:

Widely utilized in treating leukemias, lymphomas, breast cancer, colorectal cancer, and choriocarcinoma.

Q5. Discuss the general principles of cancer chemotherapy and management of chemotherapy-induced toxicities. (2018 Annual)

General Principles of Cancer Chemotherapy:

  • Combination Therapy: Utilizing drugs with different mechanisms of action and non-overlapping toxicities to maximize tumor cell destruction and prevent drug resistance.
  • Cyclic Administration: Administered in scheduled cycles with rest intervals to allow normal healthy tissues (bone marrow, GI mucosa) time to recover.
  • Cell Cycle Specificity: Agents are targeted to specific phases of cell replication (e.g., S-phase or M-phase) or act in a cell-cycle nonspecific manner.

Management of Chemotherapy-Induced Toxicities:

  • Nausea and Vomiting: Prophylactic administration of 5-HT3 antagonists and corticosteroids.
  • Myelosuppression and Neutropenia: Use of colony-stimulating factors (G-CSF) and strict infection control precautions.
  • Anemia: Blood transfusion support or erythropoiesis-stimulating agents when indicated.
  • Urothelial Protection: Hydration and protective agents like mesna to prevent hemorrhagic cystitis during alkylating therapy.
