Unit I: Introduction to Pharmacology
Q1. Give Advantages & Disadvantages Of IV Route. (2022 Annual)

Advantages of Intravenous (IV) Route:

  • Immediate bioavailability and rapid onset of action because absorption is completely bypassed.
  • Allows precise titration of serum drug concentrations for critical care medications.
  • Suitable for large volume fluids and drugs that cause severe pain or necrosis if given via subcutaneous/intramuscular routes.
  • Ideal for unconscious, emergency, or hemodynamically unstable patients.

Disadvantages of Intravenous (IV) Route:

  • High risk of systemic toxicity due to immediate peak concentrations if injected too rapidly.
  • Strictly irreversible once administered; cannot recall or purge the drug from circulation.
  • Requires professional skill, aseptic technique, and patent venous access.
  • Risk of phlebitis, local thrombosis, air embolism, and blood-borne infections.
Q2. Define Bioavailability, Bioequivalence, Efficacy, Potency, Agonist, First Pass Effect. (2024 Annual)
  • Bioavailability: The fraction and rate of an administered drug that reaches the systemic circulation unchanged.
  • Bioequivalence: Two drug products whose rate and extent of systemic absorption do not statistically differ under similar experimental conditions.
  • Efficacy: The maximum biological response obtainable from a drug when all receptors are occupied (intrinsic activity).
  • Potency: The comparative measure of drug dose required to produce a specific magnitude of pharmacological effect.
  • Agonist: A ligand that binds to a receptor and triggers a cellular response mimicking endogenous signaling molecules.
  • First Pass Effect: The initial metabolic degradation of an oral drug in the gut wall and liver before reaching systemic circulation.
Q3. Define Enteric Coating, Enlist Two Reasons Of Its Use With One Examples Each. Describe Factors Determine Routes Of Administration. Enlist Common Routes Of Drugs. (2025 Annual)

Definition of Enteric Coating:

A chemical polymer barrier applied to oral tablets or capsules designed to resist gastric acid and dissolve only when reaching the higher alkaline pH of the small intestine.

Two Reasons for Use with Examples:

  1. Protection of stomach mucosa from irritating drugs: Prevents direct chemical ulceration of gastric lining (Example: Enteric-coated Aspirin).
  2. Protection of acid-sensitive drugs from gastric degradation: Prevents destruction of active compounds by stomach acid (Example: Enteric-coated Omeprazole).

Factors Determining Routes of Administration:

  • Physical and chemical properties of the drug (lipid solubility, stability, molecular size).
  • Rate and speed of onset required (emergency vs. maintenance therapy).
  • Patient condition, compliance, and consciousness level.
  • Presence of first-pass hepatic metabolism.

Common Routes of Drugs:

  1. Oral route
  2. Intravenous route
  3. Intramuscular route
  4. Subcutaneous route
  5. Topical and Inhalation routes
Q4. What Information Can Get From Graded, Quantal Dose Response And Area Under The Curve. What Are Spare Receptors, How To Show That They Are Present In Any Tissue. (2025 Annual)

Information from Dose-Response Curves & AUC:

  • Graded Dose-Response Curve: Provides data on drug potency, maximal efficacy, and individual pharmacological sensitivity across varying doses.
  • Quantal Dose-Response Curve: Indicates population responsiveness frequency, median effective dose (ED50), median lethal dose (LD50), and therapeutic index safety margins.
  • Area Under the Curve (AUC): Measures total systemic drug exposure over time, reflecting overall bioavailability and clearance rate.

Spare Receptors and Detection:

Spare Receptors: Receptors that exist in excess of the number required to elicit a maximal biological response. They can be demonstrated experimentally by showing that a maximal pharmacological response occurs at agonist concentrations lower than those required to occupy 100% of available receptors, or by observing that irreversible receptor blockers do not decrease maximum response until a critical receptor threshold is depleted.

Q2. Enlist Mechanism Of Action, Uses, Side Effects Of Pencillin. (2018, 2019, 2020)

Mechanism of Action:

Penicillins bind to penicillin-binding proteins (PBPs) in the bacterial cell wall, inhibiting transpeptidase cross-linking of peptidoglycan chains, which weakens cell wall integrity and leads to osmotic lysis.

Therapeutic Uses:

Treatment of streptococcal infections, pneumococcal pneumonia, syphilis, meningococcal meningitis, and gonorrhea.

Side Effects:

  • Hypersensitivity allergic reactions ranging from skin rashes to life-threatening anaphylaxis.
  • Gastrointestinal disturbances, diarrhea, and pseudomembranous colitis risk.
  • Electrolyte imbalances (if administering high doses of potassium penicillin).

Q4. Can You Administer Cephalosporin Without Sensitivity Testing To A Patinet With Pencillin Sensitivity? (2009 Annual)

No. Cephalosporins share a similar beta-lactam ring structure with penicillins. Patients with a known history of severe IgE-mediated penicillin allergy carry a cross-reactivity risk (approximately 1% to 10%) of developing allergic reactions or anaphylaxis. Skin sensitivity testing and physician consultation are mandatory before administration.

Q5. Write Down Adverse Effects Of Tetracycline. (2013, 2016, 2019)
  • Permanent tooth discoloration, enamel hypoplasia, and bone growth suppression in pediatric patients.
  • Phototoxicity and severe skin sunburn reactions upon sun exposure.
  • Gastrointestinal distress, esophageal ulceration, nausea, and superinfections (candidiasis).
  • Hepatotoxicity and nephrotoxicity, especially when using expired formulations.
Q6. Write Down Adverse Effects Of Aminoglycosides And Chloramphenicol. (2019 Annual)

Adverse Effects of Aminoglycosides:

  • Nephrotoxicity (acute tubular necrosis) and Ototoxicity (irreversible auditory and vestibular damage).
  • Neuromuscular blockade resulting in skeletal muscle weakness.

Adverse Effects of Chloramphenicol:

  • Bone marrow suppression, including dose-dependent reversible anemia and life-threatening aplastic anemia.
  • Gray Baby Syndrome in neonates due to immature liver glucuronidation capacity.
Q7. Enlist 1st Line Anti-Mycobacterial Drugs. Write MOA & Adverse Effects Of Isoniazid. (2019, 2020)

First-Line Anti-TB Drugs (RIPE):

  1. Rifampicin
  2. Isoniazid (INH)
  3. Pyrazinamide
  4. Ethambutol

Mechanism of Action of Isoniazid:

Inhibits the synthesis of mycolic acids, which are essential components of the unique mycobacterial cell wall.

Adverse Effects:

  • Peripheral neuropathy (prevented by concurrent pyridoxine/Vitamin B6 supplementation).
  • Hepatotoxicity, elevated liver enzymes, and drug-induced hepatitis.
Q8. Write Classification, MOA, Adverse Effects, Resistance, Pharmacokinetics Of Aminoglycosides. (2007, 2018, 2022)

Classification:

Gentamicin, Tobramycin, Amikacin, Streptomycin, Neomycin.

Mechanism of Action:

Irreversibly binds to the 30S ribosomal subunit, causing mRNA misreading and halting bacterial protein synthesis (bactericidal action).

Adverse Effects:

Nephrotoxicity, ototoxicity, and neuromuscular blockade.

Mechanisms of Resistance:

Bacterial plasmid-mediated synthesis of modifying enzymes (acetyltransferases, phosphorylases) that inactivate the drug; ribosomal mutations.

Pharmacokinetics:

Poorly absorbed orally (administered parenterally), does not cross the blood-brain barrier well, and eliminated unchanged by glomerular filtration in kidneys.

Q9. Briefly Discuss Pulmonary Tuberculosis & It's Management. (2020 Annual)

Pulmonary Tuberculosis: A contagious chronic bacterial infection caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis affecting lung tissue, characterized by chronic cough, fever, night sweats, and weight loss.

Management (DOTS Protocol):

  • Intensive Phase (2 months): Four-drug regimen (Rifampicin, Isoniazid, Pyrazinamide, Ethambutol) to rapidly reduce bacterial load.
  • Continuation Phase (4 months): Two-drug regimen (Rifampicin and Isoniazid) to eliminate remaining dormant bacilli and prevent relapse.
Q10. Which Anti TB Drug Causes Visual Disturbance. Write It's Mechanism Of Action. (2019 Annual)

Ethambutol causes optic neuritis, resulting in visual disturbances such as reduced visual acuity and red-green color blindness.

Mechanism of Action:

Inhibits the enzyme arabinosyl transferase, which is critical for the polymerization of mycobacterial cell wall arabinogalactan.

Q11. Enumerate Drugs Diagrammatically In Intensive And Continuous Phase Of TB Therapy And Write Mechanism Of Action Of Continuous Phase Drugs. (2025 Annual)

TB Treatment Phases & Drugs:

  • Intensive Phase (2 Months): Rifampicin + Isoniazid + Pyrazinamide + Ethambutol
  • Continuation Phase (4 Months): Rifampicin + Isoniazid

Mechanism of Action of Continuation Phase Drugs:

  • Rifampicin: Inhibits DNA-dependent RNA polymerase enzyme in bacteria, suppressing RNA synthesis.
  • Isoniazid: Inhibits mycolic acid synthesis, disrupting cell wall assembly.
Q12. Which Drug Is Used For Prophylaxis Of Chloroquine Resistant Malaria. (2025 Annual)

Atovaquone-Proguanil or Doxycycline are standard prophylactic agents prescribed for travelers visiting regions with chloroquine-resistant Plasmodium falciparum strains.

Q13. Name Drug Given Orally For Cryptococcal Meningitis With AIDS And Enlist Its MOA. (2025 Annual)

Fluconazole is administered orally for maintenance therapy and step-down treatment of cryptococcal meningitis in patients with AIDS.

Mechanism of Action:

Inhibits the fungal cytochrome P450 enzyme 14-alpha-demethylase, preventing the conversion of lanosterol into ergosterol and disrupting fungal cell membrane stability.

Unit III: Drugs Affecting the Gastrointestinal System
Q1. Write Mechanism Of Action Of Metoclopramide. (2018 Annual)

Metoclopramide acts as a dopamine D2 receptor antagonist in the central chemoreceptor trigger zone and functions as a peripheral 5-HT4 receptor agonist in the gastrointestinal tract. This enhances acetylcholine release, increasing lower esophageal sphincter tone and accelerating gastric emptying.

Q2. Define IBD And Enlist Major Drugs Used To Treat IBS. (2022 Annual)

Inflammatory Bowel Disease (IBD): A chronic inflammatory condition of the gastrointestinal tract encompassing Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis.

Major Drugs Used for Irritable Bowel Syndrome (IBS):

  • Antispasmodics (Dicyclomine, Hyoscine)
  • Serotonin modulators (Alosetron, Eluxadoline)
  • Laxatives or anti-diarrheal agents (Loperamide) depending on subtype (IBS-C or IBS-D).
Q3. Describe Therapeutic Uses Of Antacids. (2022 Annual)
  • Symptomatic relief of acute heartburn, acid indigestion, and sour stomach.
  • Adjunctive therapy in healing peptic ulcers and GERD by neutralizing gastric hydrochloric acid.
  • Prevention of stress-related mucosal bleeding in critically ill hospital patients.
Unit IV: Drugs Affecting Hematology System
Q1. Write Down Mechanism, Clinical Uses And Adverse Effects Of Aspirin. What Are Important Precautions Related To Aspirin. (2008, 2013, 2018)

Mechanism of Action:

Irreversibly acetylates and inhibits cyclooxygenase (COX-1 and COX-2) enzymes, preventing the synthesis of thromboxane A2 in platelets to block aggregation.

Clinical Uses:

Secondary prevention of myocardial infarction and ischemic stroke, acute coronary syndrome, and mild-to-moderate analgesia/antipyresis.

Adverse Effects:

Gastric mucosal irritation, ulceration, gastrointestinal bleeding, and tinnitus in high doses.

Important Precautions:

  • Avoid in children with viral infections due to the risk of Reye's syndrome.
  • Discontinue 7 days prior to elective surgery to prevent bleeding complications.
Unit V: Anti-Neoplastic Drugs
Q1. Write Mechanism Of Action Of Methotrexate With Uses & Side Effects. (2013, 2022)

Mechanism of Action:

A folic acid antagonist that competitively binds to dihydrofolate reductase (DHFR), inhibiting the synthesis of active reduced folates required for purine and thymidylate synthesis.

Therapeutic Uses:

Acute lymphoblastic leukemia, choriocarcinoma, non-Hodgkin lymphoma, and severe autoimmune diseases like rheumatoid arthritis and psoriasis.

Side Effects:

Bone marrow suppression, severe oral mucositis, hepatotoxicity, and pulmonary fibrosis.

Q4. Describe Principles Of Chemotherapy With Characteristics Of An Ideal Anticancer Drug And Its Ultimate Goal. (2025 Annual)

Principles of Chemotherapy:

  • Combination regimens: Utilizing multi-agent protocols with distinct mechanisms to prevent resistance.
  • Cyclic dosing: Providing intermittent rest intervals to allow normal bone marrow and mucosal tissues to recover.

Characteristics of an Ideal Anticancer Drug:

Selectively targets and destroys malignant cells while sparing all normal host tissues, exhibits no cumulative toxicity, and does not induce drug resistance.

Ultimate Goal:

Achieving total eradication of malignant cells to produce a permanent cure or long-term remission, alongside effective palliative symptom control in advanced stages.
