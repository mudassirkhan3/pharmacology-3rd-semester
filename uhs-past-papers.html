Unit I: Introduction to Pharmacology & General Pharmacology

Q1. Name the factors affecting the rate of absorption and distribution. 2021 Annual

Factors Affecting Absorption:

  • Area of Absorptive Surface: A larger surface area (such as the microvilli of the small intestine) significantly increases the rate of drug absorption.
  • Route of Administration: Intravenous administration bypasses absorption entirely, whereas oral, subcutaneous, and intramuscular routes depend heavily on local blood flow and membrane barriers.
  • Vascularity: High blood flow at the site of administration maintains a steep concentration gradient, accelerating absorption.
  • pH and Ionization State: Weak acids are better absorbed in the acidic environment of the stomach, whereas weak bases are absorbed primarily in the alkaline environment of the intestine. Non-ionized drugs cross lipid bilayers much faster than ionized drugs.
  • Presence of Other Substances: Food, concurrent medications, or chelating agents can bind to drugs or alter gastric emptying time, thereby decreasing or delaying absorption.
  • Gastrointestinal (GI) Motility: Extremely rapid motility reduces contact time with absorptive surfaces, whereas slowed motility can enhance or alter absorption extent.
  • Functional Integrity of Absorptive Surface: Pathological conditions affecting mucosal integrity can impair absorption.
  • Disease States: Conditions affecting splanchnic blood flow or absorptive epithelium alter overall drug uptake.

Factors Affecting Distribution:

  • Cardiac Output and Local Blood Flow: Well-perfused organs (brain, liver, kidneys) receive drugs much faster than poorly perfused tissues (adipose tissue, resting skeletal muscle).
  • Capillary Permeability: Differences in capillary structure (e.g., tight junctions in the blood-brain barrier versus fenestrated capillaries in hepatic sinusoids) dictate how easily drugs leave circulation.
  • Size and Mass of the Organ: Larger tissue mass increases the distribution capacity for lipophilic drugs.
  • Binding of Drugs to Plasma Proteins: Drugs extensively bound to plasma proteins (such as albumin) have a restricted volume of distribution because only the free, unbound fraction can diffuse across membranes to exert pharmacological effects.
  • Lipid Solubility: Highly lipid-soluble drugs easily cross cell membranes and accumulate readily in adipose tissue and the central nervous system.
Q2. Define bioavailability. Discuss the factors that influence bioavailability, and define zero-order kinetics. 2020 Supplementary & 2019 Supplementary

Bioavailability Definition: Bioavailability is defined as the rate and extent to which an administered drug reaches the systemic circulation in an unchanged active form. For intravenous administration, bioavailability is absolute (100 percent), whereas oral administration is subject to incomplete absorption and first-pass metabolism.

Methods to Improve Bioavailability:

  1. Utilizing specialized prodrug formulations that enhance lipophilicity and membrane permeability.
  2. Employing micronization or nanoparticle technology to increase dissolution rates for poorly water-soluble drugs.
  3. Using enteric coatings or absorption enhancers to prevent degradation in the gastrointestinal tract.

Factors Influencing Bioavailability:

  • First-pass hepatic metabolism.
  • Physicochemical solubility and chemical stability of the drug.
  • Nature of the drug formulation and excipients used.

Zero-Order Kinetics of Elimination:

If a constant amount of drug is metabolized per unit of time, the rate of elimination is constant and does not depend on the drug concentration. This is called zero-order kinetics (also referred to as nonlinear kinetics). Clearance saturates at high concentrations. Classic examples of drugs following zero-order kinetics include ethanol, phenytoin, and high-dose aspirin.

Q3. Enlist merits and demerits of oral route of drug administration, and define volume of distribution. 2019 Annual

Merits of Oral Route: Safest route, most common route, convenient for patients, and highly economical.

Demerits of Oral Route: Limited absorption of certain drugs, food interactions can alter efficacy, patient compliance is required, and drugs may undergo significant metabolism before reaching systemic circulation.

Volume of Distribution (Vd): It is defined as the hypothetical fluid volume that is required to contain the entire drug in the body at the same concentration measured in the plasma. It reflects the extent of drug distribution into body tissues and fluids.

Calculation Formula:

Vd = (Amount of Drug in the Body) / (Plasma Concentration at Time Zero)

Q4. Enlist routes of drug administration and define receptors with major receptor families. 2018 Supplementary & 2014 Annual

Routes of Drug Administration:

  1. Oral Route: Administration by mouth.
  2. Subcutaneous (Hypodermic): Injection into the subcutaneous tissue just below the skin.
  3. Intramuscular (IM): Injection directly into deep skeletal muscle tissue.
  4. Intradermal (ID): Injection into the dermal layer just beneath the epidermis.
  5. Intravenous (IV): Direct injection into the venous bloodstream for immediate systemic effect.

Receptors: Receptors are specialized macromolecular biological molecules with which active drugs interact to initiate a chain of biochemical events producing a functional change in the system.

Major Receptor Families & Signaling Mechanisms:

  • Transmembrane Ligand-Gated Ion Channels: Regulate ion conductance across cell membranes, operating in milliseconds (e.g., Nicotinic acetylcholine receptors).
  • Transmembrane G Protein-Coupled Receptors (GPCRs): Coupled to intracellular G proteins (Gs, Gi, Gq) that activate effector enzymes or second messengers.
  • Enzyme-Linked Receptors: Possess intrinsic enzymatic activity or associate directly with kinases (e.g., Insulin and growth factor receptors).
  • Intracellular Receptors: Located within the cytoplasm or nucleus, activated by lipid-soluble ligands (e.g., steroid and thyroid hormones) to regulate gene transcription.
Q5. Enumerate phase 2 drug metabolizing reactions with typical drug substrate examples and characteristics. 2018 Annual

Phase 2 reactions primarily involve synthetic conjugation processes that attach polar groups to drug molecules or phase 1 metabolites to render them water-soluble and easily excretable.

Phase II Reaction Typical Drug Substrate Example
Glucuronidation Acetaminophen, Diazepam, Morphine
Acetylation Clonazepam, Isoniazid, Sulfonamides
Glutathione Conjugation Ethacrynic Acid, Reactive Phase 1 metabolites of acetaminophen
Glycine Conjugation Nicotinic Acid (Niacin), Salicylic Acid

Characteristics of Phase 2 Reactions:

  • Catalyze the conjugation of drug substrates with endogenous hydrophilic moieties.
  • Consistently produce water-soluble metabolites.
  • Facilitate rapid renal or biliary excretion from the body.
  • Inactivate toxic intermediate metabolites produced during phase 1 oxidation reactions.
Q6. Explain the First Pass Effect and differentiate between First-Order Kinetics and Zero-Order Kinetics (with separate graphs). 2016 Annual

First-Pass Effect: When a drug is absorbed from the gastrointestinal tract, it enters the portal circulation and passes through the liver before reaching the systemic circulation. If the liver rapidly metabolizes the drug during this initial passage, the amount of unchanged drug entering systemic circulation is drastically reduced, leading to subtherapeutic action unless adjusted.

Kinetics of Reaction Comparison:

Parameter First-Order Kinetics Zero-Order Kinetics
Rate Dependency Rate of reaction is directly proportional to the concentration of reactants. Rate of reaction is constant and independent of reactant concentration.

Q7. Discuss major drug receptor families and signaling mechanisms. 2014 Annual

Receptor families mediate cellular signaling through distinct molecular architectures:

  • G Protein-Coupled Receptors (GPCRs): Comprise extracellular ligand-binding domains linked to heterotrimeric G proteins (alpha, beta, gamma subunits). Agonist binding induces GTP binding and dissociation of subunits to regulate downstream effectors.
  • Transmembrane Receptor Tyrosine Kinases: Feature extracellular ligand-binding domains linked via peptide chains to intracellular protein kinase domains.
  • Cytokine Receptors: Agonist-induced dimerization alters intracellular conformation, recruiting and activating JAK kinases and STAT transcription factors.
  • Ligand and Voltage-Gated Channels: Increase transmembrane ionic conductance across synapses, eliciting rapid cellular depolarization or hyperpolarization within milliseconds.
  • Intracellular Receptors: Stimulate gene transcription by binding directly to specific DNA sequences following prolonged lag periods.

Indications for Parenteral Administration: Used for drugs poorly absorbed from the GI tract (e.g., heparin) or unstable in digestive acids, and for patients requiring rapid onset of action.

Risks of Intravenous Drug Administration:

  • Infection and contamination at the injection site.
  • Vascular damage, phlebitis, or infiltration.
  • Air embolism.
  • Thrombosis and blood clot formation.
Q9. What is an antagonist? Discuss different types of antagonism with examples. 2007 Annual & 2017 Supplementary

Antagonist Definition: A drug that possesses affinity for a specific receptor but lacks intrinsic activity. It produces no biological effect in the absence of an agonist, but decreases or blocks the effect of an agonist when co-administered.

Types of Antagonism:

  1. Physical Antagonism: Based on physical properties; for example, medicinal charcoal binds alkaloids physically in the GI tract to prevent systemic absorption.
  2. Chemical Antagonism: Chemical interaction between two substances without receptor involvement; for example, chelating agents binding heavy metal ions.
  3. Physiological / Functional Antagonism: Occurs when two agonists act on separate independent receptors to produce opposite physiological effects (e.g., histamine causing bronchoconstriction versus adrenaline causing bronchodilation).
  4. Pharmacological Antagonism: Competes for the same receptor site:
    • Competitive (Reversible) Antagonism: Binds reversibly to the receptor site and can be overcome by increasing agonist concentration.
    • Non-Competitive (Irreversible) Antagonism: Binds to allosteric or receptor sites permanently, preventing activation regardless of agonist concentration.

Unit II: Drugs Used to Prevent and Treat Infection & Malaria

Core Overview & Clinical Management UHS Curriculum

Antimicrobial & Antimalarial Categories: Anti-infective therapy encompasses bactericidal and bacteriostatic agents designed to eliminate pathogenic microorganisms without causing undue host toxicity.

  • Antibacterial Agents: Target bacterial cell wall synthesis, protein synthesis, or nucleic acid replication (e.g., penicillins, cephalosporins, macrolides).
  • Antimalarial Drugs: Designed to eradicate plasmodium parasites within erythrocytes and hepatic stages (e.g., chloroquine, artemisinin-based combination therapies).
  • Antifungal & Antiviral Agents: Disrupt fungal cell membrane ergosterol synthesis or viral polymerase enzymes.

Nursing Measures & Patient Education:

  • Instruct patients to complete the full prescribed course of anti-infective therapy to prevent microbial resistance.
  • Monitor for hypersensitivity reactions, gastrointestinal distress, and secondary superinfections.
  • Ensure proper hydration and baseline renal/hepatic function laboratory monitoring.

Unit III: Drugs Affecting the Gastrointestinal System

Gastrointestinal Pharmacotherapy & Nursing Care UHS Curriculum

Common Gastrointestinal Disorders & Symptoms: Peptic ulcer disease, gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD), nausea, vomiting, constipation, and diarrhea.

Classification of GI Drugs:

  • Antacids & Acid Suppressants: Neutralize gastric acid or inhibit acid secretion (Proton Pump Inhibitors, H2 receptor antagonists).
  • Antiemetics: Prevent or relieve nausea and vomiting by acting on central and peripheral emetic pathways.
  • Laxatives & Cathartics: Promote bowel evacuation for constipation or diagnostic preparation.
  • Antidiarrheals: Reduce intestinal motility and fluid secretion.

Nursing Responsibilities: Monitor electrolyte balance, assess bowel sounds and abdominal pain, evaluate therapeutic response, and educate patients regarding medication timing relative to meals.

Unit IV: Drugs Affecting Hematology System

Hematological Agents & Therapeutic Uses UHS Curriculum

Uses and Effects: Drugs affecting the hematological system regulate coagulation, platelet aggregation, and erythropoiesis to manage thromboembolic disorders and anemias.

Classification & Mechanisms:

  • Anticoagulants: Prevent thrombus formation by inhibiting clotting factor cascades (e.g., heparin, warfarin).
  • Antiplatelet Agents: Inhibit platelet aggregation and plug formation (e.g., aspirin).
  • Thrombolytics: Dissolve existing blood clots by converting plasminogen to plasmin.
  • Hematinics: Provide essential building blocks like iron, vitamin B12, and folic acid for red blood cell synthesis.

Expected & Adverse Reactions: Primary adverse reactions include bleeding tendencies, bruising, and thrombocytopenia. Rigorous nursing monitoring of coagulation profiles (PT/INR, aPTT) and vital signs is essential.

Unit V: Anti-Neoplastic Drugs

Oncological Pharmacology & Cytotoxic Agents UHS Curriculum

Description & Therapeutic Uses: Anti-neoplastic agents are cytotoxic drugs utilized in cancer chemotherapy to inhibit rapid cellular proliferation and tumor progression.

Classification of Anti-Neoplastic Drugs:

  • Alkylating Agents: Cross-link DNA strands to prevent cellular replication.
  • Antimetabolites: Mimic cellular metabolites to disrupt DNA synthesis pathways.
  • Plant Alkaloids & Mitotic Inhibitors: Arrest cell division by interfering with microtubule assembly during mitosis.
  • Antitumor Antibiotics: Intercalate into DNA and generate free radicals to damage malignant cells.

Adverse Toxicities & Nursing Responsibilities:

  • Bone marrow suppression leading to neutropenia, anemia, and thrombocytopenia.
  • Gastrointestinal mucosal inflammation (mucositis, nausea).
  • Alopecia and organ-specific toxicities (cardiotoxicity, nephrotoxicity).
  • Rigorous aseptic nursing care, monitoring complete blood counts (CBC), and providing supportive emetogenic prophylaxis.
