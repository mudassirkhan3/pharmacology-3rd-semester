UNIT 1: INTRODUCTION TO PHARMACOLOGY

Welcome to foundational nursing pharmacology! This module breaks down essential definitions, historical milestones, why we give medications, how they are classified, and how to use clinical tools safely.

1. Important Terms to Know

  • Pharmacology: The complete science of medications, including how they are made, how they work in the body, and their medical uses.
  • Drug: Any chemical substance that changes how the body functions when it is introduced.
  • Medication: A drug given specifically to diagnose, cure, treat, or prevent a disease.
  • Generic Name: The official, universal name of a drug (for example, paracetamol).
  • Trade / Brand Name: The commercial name given by the pharmaceutical company making the drug (for example, Panadol or Tylenol).
  • Adverse Drug Reaction (Side Effect): An unintended or uncomfortable reaction that happens even when taking a normal, prescribed dose.

2. A Brief History of Pharmacology

Pharmacology grew from ancient healing traditions into a precise medical science:

  • Ancient Era: People relied on plants, roots, and minerals, recorded in early medical texts.
  • 19th Century: Scientists learned how to extract pure active ingredients from plants (such as isolating morphine from opium).
  • Modern Era: Advanced chemistry, laboratory testing, and strict clinical trials ensure every medicine we give today is safe and effective.

3. Why Do We Give Medications?

Medications serve five main purposes in healthcare:

  • Diagnostic: To help find out what is wrong (like special dyes used in X-rays or CT scans).
  • Curative: To eliminate an illness entirely (like antibiotics curing an infection).
  • Palliative: To keep a patient comfortable and ease symptoms when a cure isn't possible (like pain medicine in palliative care).
  • Prophylactic (Preventive): To stop an illness before it starts (like vaccines or preventive antibiotics before surgery).
  • Replacement: To supply chemicals the body cannot make on its own (like insulin for diabetes).

4. Where Do Medications Come From?

Source Category Description & Examples
Plant Sources Derived from flowers, barks, or leaves (e.g., digoxin from foxglove, morphine from poppies).
Animal Sources Extracted from animal tissues (e.g., heparin from porcine tissue).
Mineral Sources Natural earth elements (e.g., iron supplements, potassium chloride).
Synthetic Sources Created in a laboratory by scientists (e.g., lab-made insulin, aspirin).

5. How Drugs Are Classified

Drugs are grouped in different ways to make learning easier:

  • Therapeutic Use: Grouped by what disease they treat (e.g., blood pressure medications).
  • Pharmacological Action: Grouped by how they work in the body (e.g., beta-blockers).
  • Body System: Grouped by which organ system they affect (e.g., respiratory drugs).

6. Three Drug Supply Systems in Hospitals

Hospitals use structured systems to manage medications securely:

  • Floor Stock System: General supplies kept right on the ward. Convenient, but carries a higher risk of inventory mistakes.
  • Individual Prescription Order System: Medications are sent from the pharmacy specifically labeled for an individual patient for a set time.
  • Unit-Dose System: The safest and most modern standard. Medications arrive pre-packaged in exact, single-use doses ready to give directly to the patient, greatly reducing errors.

7. Medication Standards and Laws

Government rules protect patients by making sure drugs are pure, safe, and effective:

  • Official Books (Pharmacopeias): Official guidelines detailing exact drug recipes and quality tests.
  • Regulatory Agencies: Government watchdogs (like the FDA or national health authorities) that approve new medications.
  • Controlled Substance Rules: Strict laws for addictive narcotics requiring double-locked storage and careful witness sign-offs.

8. Reliable Drug Information Resources

As a nurse, always verify medication facts using trusted sources:

  • Standard nursing drug handbooks and official hospital formularies.
  • Direct consultation with clinical pharmacists.
  • Trusted digital medical databases and mobile apps.

9. Creating Clinical Drug Cards

Drug cards are wonderful study tools for nursing students. A great drug card includes:

  • Generic and brand names.
  • Drug category and how it works in the body.
  • Why the patient is prescribed this medication.
  • Safe dose ranges and crucial vital sign checkpoints (e.g., holding heart medication if pulse is too low).
  • Important side effects and nursing actions.

PHARMACOKINETICS (What the Body Does to the Drug)

Pharmacokinetics looks at how a drug travels through the body. We remember the four core steps using the friendly acronym ADME:

  • Absorption: How the drug enters the bloodstream from where it was given.
  • Distribution: How the drug travels through the blood to reach tissues and organs.
  • Metabolism: How the body breaks down and alters the drug (mostly happening in the liver).
  • Excretion: How the body filters and removes the drug (mostly through the kidneys in urine).

Therapeutic Index and Dose-Response Curve

Understanding the relationship between the dose of a drug and its effect is crucial for patient safety. The Dose-Response Curve (DRC) shows how much biological effect a drug produces as the dose increases.

The Therapeutic Index (TI) is a safety ratio that compares the dose that produces toxicity (TD50) to the dose that produces the desired therapeutic effect (ED50). A wider margin between these two curves means a safer medication!

Nursing Insight on Safety
