Pharmacokinetics (PK) vs. Pharmacodynamics (PD) Master Table

High-Yield Exam Focus

Understanding the exact core difference between Pharmacokinetics and Pharmacodynamics is the single most essential foundation of nursing pharmacology.

Comparison Criteria Pharmacokinetics (PK) Pharmacodynamics (PD)
Core Definition What the BODY does to the drug. What the DRUG does to the body.
Primary Focus Drug movement, concentrations, onset time, half-life ($t_{1/2}$), and duration of action in body fluids. Biochemical mechanisms, receptor binding, systemic responses, and toxicological outcomes.
Key Stages / Mechanisms ADME Model:
  • Absorption (Enteral/Parenteral)
  • Distribution (Plasma protein binding)
  • Metabolism (Liver CYP450 enzymes)
  • Excretion (Renal clearance)
 Receptor & Enzyme Interactions:
  • Agonist vs. Antagonist binding
  • Enzyme Inhibition / Activation
  • Ion Channel Opening/Closing
  • Dose-Response Relationships ($EC_{50}$)
Influencing Patient Factors Renal clearance ($GFR$), Hepatic function, Gastric emptying rate, Age, Blood circulation, Hydration status. Receptor density, Genetic receptor polymorphism, Target tissue sensitivity, Tolerance, Desensitization.
Clinical Nursing Application Determining dose intervals, IV infusion rates, monitoring peak/trough levels, and adjusting for renal failure. Evaluating drug efficacy, therapeutic response, side effect profile, antidote administration, and toxic limits.
Prototypical Clinical Example Oral Paracetamol is absorbed in small intestine, metabolized in liver into $NAPQI$, excreted in urine. Paracetamol inhibits central $COX-2$ enzymes to block prostaglandin synthesis, reducing pain & fever.

1 Essential Pharmacology Terminologies

UHS SLO #1

Building a professional nursing foundation requires absolute precision in medical terminology:

Pharmacology (Pharmacon + Logos)

The science of drugs, including their origin, chemical composition, biological effects, mechanism of action, and therapeutic applications in living systems.

Drug / Medication

Any active chemical substance that modifies biological function to diagnose, treat, mitigate, cure, or prevent disease.

Pharmacokinetics (PK)

Study of drug movement throughout the body over time (Absorption, Distribution, Metabolism, and Excretion - ADME).

Pharmacodynamics (PD)

Study of the biochemical and physiological effects of drugs and their specific mechanisms of action at target receptors.

Pharmacotherapeutics

The clinical execution of pharmacological principles for disease prevention, management, and treatment in human patients.

Toxicology

The study of adverse, harmful, or poisonous effects of chemical agents and toxic drug overdoses on living tissues.

Pharmacy

The health science branch responsible for collecting, compounding, preparing, dispensing, and safeguarding proper medication usage.

Posology

The branch of medical science devoted to determining accurate drug dosages tailored to age, weight, and renal function.

2 Brief History & Milestones of Pharmacology

UHS SLO #2
Ancient Era (3000 BCE)

Ebers Papyrus & Empirical Herbalism

Ancient Egyptian scrolls documented hundreds of natural plant, mineral, and animal remedies used empirically prior to modern physiological understanding.

Middle Ages (800–1200 CE)

Islamic Golden Age & Ibn Sina (Avicenna)

Ibn Sina authored The Canon of Medicine, establishing initial principles of controlled clinical trial observations, drug purity standards, and apothecary compounding.

16th Century

Paracelsus — Father of Toxicology

Formulated the law of toxicology: "The dose makes the poison." Established that chemical dosage determines therapeutic benefit versus toxicity.

19th Century (1805)

Morphine Isolation & Modern Pure Chemistry

Friedrich Sertürner isolated pure morphine from opium poppy, launching laboratory-based experimental pharmacology and standardized chemical dosages.

20th Century to Present

Antibiotics, Recombinant DNA & Personalized Medicine

Alexander Fleming discovered Penicillin (1928). Genetic engineering enabled synthetic human insulin, monoclonal antibodies, and pharmacogenomic therapeutics.

3 Purposes of Medication Administration

UHS SLO #3
🔍

1. Diagnostic Purpose

Used to confirm or identify pathological conditions (e.g., Radiopaque contrast dyes for CT scans, Tuberculin PPD skin tests).

🦠

2. Curative Purpose

Completely eradicates the underlying disease or pathogen (e.g., Antibiotics like Penicillin curing bacterial infections).

🩺

3. Symptomatic / Palliative

Relieves pain or distressing symptoms without curing the root pathology (e.g., Paracetamol for fever, Morphine in advanced oncology).

🛡️

4. Prophylactic / Preventive

Prevents onset of illness or surgical site complications (e.g., Vaccines, Pre-operative IV prophylactic antibiotics).

🧪

5. Replacement / Substitutive

Replaces essential endogenous molecules absent due to disease (e.g., Insulin in Type 1 Diabetes, Levothyroxine in hypothyroidism).

⚙️

6. Health Maintenance / Supportive

Sustains optimal body function during recovery (e.g., Antihypertensives, Multivitamins, IV electrolyte maintenance fluids).

5 Classification Systems of Drugs

UHS SLO #5
1. By Body System Target

Groups drugs based on the principal anatomical body system affected.

  • Cardiovascular Drugs (Antihypertensives)
  • Central Nervous System Drugs (Sedatives)
  • Gastrointestinal Drugs (Antacids)
2. Therapeutic Classification

Groups drugs based on clinical intent or therapeutic usefulness in disease management.

  • Antihypertensives (Lowers Blood Pressure)
  • Analgesics (Relieves Pain)
  • Antipyretics (Reduces Fever)
3. Pharmacological Classification

Groups drugs based on precise biochemical mechanisms of action at cellular/receptor levels.

  • Beta-Blockers (Propranolol)
  • ACE Inhibitors (Captopril)
  • Calcium Channel Blockers (Amlodipine)

Understanding the 3 Types of Drug Nomenclature

1. Chemical Name: Describes exact atomic and molecular structure (e.g., N-(4-hydroxyphenyl)acetamide).
2. Generic (Non-Proprietary) Name: Official universal name assigned by USAN/WHO (e.g., Paracetamol / Acetaminophen).
3. Brand (Trade/Proprietary) Name: Trademarked name created by manufacturing company (e.g., Panadol®, Calpol®).

6 The 3 Types of Hospital Drug Supply Systems

UHS SLO #6
System 1

Stock Supply System (Floor Stock)

Medications are stocked in bulk directly on hospital nursing units. Nurses pull doses from ward stock cabinets for assigned patients.

✔ Advantage: Immediate availability for emergency situations.

✖ Disadvantage: Increased medication error rates, inventory loss, and drug waste.

System 2

Individual Prescription Supply

The hospital pharmacy dispenses a multi-day (3 to 7 days) drug supply for each individual patient upon receiving physician orders.

✔ Advantage: Pharmacist reviews prescription prior to dispensing.

✖ Disadvantage: Potential delay in initial dose delivery; returned unused meds.

System 3

Unit-Dose Supply System (Gold Standard)

Pharmacy dispenses individually packaged, pre-labeled single doses for a 24-hour period into patient-specific drawer carts.

✔ Advantage: Lowest medication error rates (<1%), maximum safety!

✖ Disadvantage: High pharmacy personnel requirement and specialized equipment.

7 Drug Standards & Legislation Safety

UHS SLO #7

1. Official Pharmacopeia Standards

Legal compendiums establishing official purity, potency, quality, identity, and strength standards:

  • USP (United States Pharmacopeia): Official US pharmaceutical standard.
  • BP (British Pharmacopoeia): Official United Kingdom standard.
  • IP (International Pharmacopoeia): Published by World Health Organization (WHO).

2. Drug Legislation & Controlled Substances

Statutory laws governing drug manufacturing, distribution, prescribing rights, and abuse prevention:

  • Controlled Substances Act: Classifies addictive narcotics into Schedules (Schedules I to V) according to abuse potential.
  • DRAP (Drug Regulatory Authority of Pakistan): Enforces drug registration, pricing, quality control, and safety laws under the Drugs Act 1976.

9 Preparing Clinical Nursing Drug Cards

UHS SLO #9 & Clinical Skill

Drug cards are concise bedside quick-reference tools mandatory for clinical ward rotations. Standardized card layout required by nursing faculty:

DRUG CARD: METOPROLOL TARTRATE Classification: Therapeutic: Antihypertensive | Pharmacological: Beta-1 Selective Blocker
Trade: Lopressor®
1. Route & Dose Range

50–100 mg PO daily in 1 or 2 divided doses. Emergency IV: 5 mg every 2 mins (Max 15 mg).

2. Action / Mechanism

Selectively blocks Beta-1 adrenergic receptors in cardiac tissue, decreasing heart rate & cardiac output.

3. Indications

Hypertension, Angina Pectoris, Post-Myocardial Infarction cardioprotection.

4. Contraindications

Sinus Bradycardia (<50 bpm), Cardiogenic shock, 2nd/3rd-degree AV block, Severe asthma.

5. Key Adverse Effects

Bradycardia, Hypotension, Fatigue, Dizziness, Erectile dysfunction, Masked hypoglycemia symptoms.

6. High-Yield Nursing Responsibilities
  • Pre-Assessment: Check apical pulse & BP prior to administration. Hold dose if HR < 60 bpm or Systolic BP < 90 mmHg.
  • Patient Teaching: Warn patient against abrupt withdrawal (prevents rebound angina/hypertension). Teach orthostatic blood pressure precautions.
