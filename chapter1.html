Unit I: Introduction to Pharmacology

Complete BSN Curriculum: Kinetics, Dynamics, Receptor Mechanisms & Clinical Administration

Detailed study notes featuring animated visual simulators for ADME and Intrinsic Activity, comprehensive tables on full/partial/inverse agonists and reversible/irreversible antagonists, potency/efficacy graphs, and standardized clinical drug card preparation.

1. Discuss the terminologies related to pharmacology

Essential nursing pharmacology terminology required for clinical practice:

• Pharmacology: The comprehensive study of the properties, actions, and biochemical effects of drugs on living biological systems.

• Pharmacokinetics (PK): What the body does to the drug spanning absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion (ADME).

• Pharmacodynamics (PD): What the drug does to the body through receptor binding, signal transduction, and physiological response.

• Bioavailability ($F$): The fraction of administered drug that enters systemic circulation in an active, unchanged form.

• Half-Life ($t_{1/2}$): The time required for the plasma concentration of a drug to decrease by 50%.

💡 Nursing Memory Mnemonic: PK vs. PD

PK = Processing by Kidneys/Body (ADME path).
PD = Power Delivered at the target cell receptor.

2. Discuss the history of pharmacology briefly

Pharmacology evolved from ancient traditional folklore and empirical plant remedies into a precise biomedical science. Early civilizations utilized crude herbal extracts (e.g., opium poppy, cinchona bark, digitalis). The 19th-century birth of modern chemistry allowed active pharmaceutical compounds to be isolated (e.g., morphine in 1805). The 20th century transformed the field with synthetic drug creation, antibiotic discoveries, and molecular receptor pharmacology.

3. Identify the purposes of medication

Medications are prescribed and administered in clinical settings for four main purposes:

Medication Purpose Clinical Definition & Objective Nursing Example
Diagnostic Used alongside imaging or tests to diagnose disorders. Radiopaque contrast dyes, radioactive diagnostic tracers.
Prophylactic Administered to prevent disease or infection before onset. Pre-surgical prophylactic antibiotics, immunizations.
Therapeutic To cure, manage, or reduce symptoms of active illness. Antihypertensive agents, insulin for diabetes mellitus.
Replacement To substitute or supplement missing natural body chemicals. Levothyroxine for hypothyroidism, insulin replacement.

4. Identify the source of medication

Pharmaceutical agents are derived from four principal sources in nature and laboratories:

1. Plant Sources: Alkaloids and glycosides extracted from plants (e.g., Morphine from opium, Digoxin from foxglove).

2. Animal Sources: Biological extracts from animal tissues (e.g., Heparin from porcine intestinal mucosa).

3. Mineral Sources: Inorganic elements and mineral compounds (e.g., Ferrous sulfate for anemia, magnesium sulfate).

4. Synthetic & Recombinant Sources: Laboratory-synthesized chemical compounds and recombinant DNA biotechnology (e.g., synthetic antibiotics, human insulin).

5. Discuss the classification of drugs & Advanced Pharmacokinetics/Dynamics

Drugs are classified based on therapeutic utility or chemical mechanism. Below is the rigorous exploration of Pharmacokinetic ADME factors, Pharmacodynamic receptor dynamics & intrinsic activity, and Potency/Efficacy with therapeutic index calculations.

A. Pharmacokinetics (ADME) & Influencing Factors

Pharmacokinetics determines drug concentration levels over time. Each phase is governed by specific physiological variables:

Phase Core Mechanism Key Factors Affecting the Process
Absorption Movement of drug from administration site into bloodstream. Route (IV is 100% instant), surface area, blood flow, lipid solubility, pH, and food interactions.
Distribution Reversible transfer of drug from blood to interstitial tissues. Cardiac output, regional blood flow, capillary permeability, and Plasma Protein Binding (Albumin) (free unbound drug is active).
Metabolism Biotransformation (mainly in liver via CYP450 enzyme systems). Age (neonates/elderly), genetic polymorphisms, liver disease, first-pass effect, and enzyme induction/inhibition.
Excretion Elimination of drug waste from body (primarily via kidneys). Renal function (measured by Glomerular Filtration Rate / Creatinine Clearance), urine pH, and urinary flow.

B. Pharmacodynamics: Intrinsic Activity & Receptor Binding Types

Intrinsic Activity ($\alpha$): The mathematical measure of the capacity of a bound drug-receptor complex to trigger a cellular signal response (ranging from 0.0 to 1.0).

Agent / Binding Type Affinity Intrinsic Activity ($\alpha$) Reversible / Irreversible Clinical Description & Explanation
Full Agonist High 1.0 (Maximum) Reversible Binds and activates receptor fully, producing maximum biological effect (e.g., Morphine).
Partial Agonist High Intermediate (>0 & <1) Reversible Binds receptor but elicits sub-maximal cellular response even at 100% receptor occupancy.
Inverse Agonist High Negative (< 0) Reversible Stabilizes receptor in an inactive state, suppressing basal constitutive activity below zero.
Competitive Antagonist High 0.0 (Zero) Reversible Competes for the active site. Can be overcome by increasing local agonist concentration.
Non-Competitive Antagonist High 0.0 (Zero) Irreversible (or allosteric) Binds permanently (covalent bond) or alters receptor conformation. Cannot be overcome by adding agonist.

C. Potency, Efficacy & Therapeutic Index (TI) with Graph

• Efficacy ($E_{max}$): The maximum therapeutic response a drug can produce regardless of dosage.

• Potency: The dose required to produce 50% of the drug's maximal effect ($ED_{50}$).

• Therapeutic Index (TI): The ratio measuring drug safety margin:

Therapeutic Index (TI) = $\frac{TD_{50}}{ED_{50}}$    (Toxic Dose 50 / Effective Dose 50)

6. Describe the three types of drug supply system

Hospitals and nursing units utilize three primary medication distribution models:

• Stock Supply System: Bulk quantities of non-controlled medications maintained on wards. High risk of medication errors and pilferage.

• Individual Prescription System: Pharmacy dispenses individual patient prescriptions labeled specifically for each client.

• Unit-Dose Distribution System: The safest, gold-standard hospital system. Pre-packaged, single-dose medications organized by administration schedule in patient-specific drawers.

7. Discuss the drugs standards and legislation

Drug standards and statutory legislation ensure pharmaceutical products maintain rigorous criteria for purity, strength, quality, and efficacy. Regulatory bodies (such as drug inspection authorities and pharmacopeia commissions) enforce strict scheduling of controlled substances to prevent drug diversion and protect public health.

8. Identify resource to collect and utilize drug information

Nurses must rely on authorized, evidence-based pharmacological references:

• Nursing Drug Handbooks: Focused explicitly on nursing assessments, lab monitoring, and patient education.

• Physicians' Desk Reference (PDR): Compilation of manufacturer prescribing data and package inserts.

• Clinical Databases: Lexicomp, UpToDate, and Micromedex for real-time drug interaction checks.
