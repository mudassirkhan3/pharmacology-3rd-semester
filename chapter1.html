Advanced Pharmacodynamics

Drug Action, Receptor Families & Dose-Response Relationships

Simplified clinical explanations of how drugs interact with cellular receptors, initiate signal transduction cascades, and produce therapeutic or toxic responses.

1. Introduction to Pharmacodynamics

Pharmacodynamics (PD) describes what the drug does to the body and how drug concentrations influence the magnitude of the response. Most drugs exert their beneficial or harmful effects by binding to specialized target macromolecules called receptors located on the cell surface or inside the cell.

🎯 The Drug-Receptor Complex

When a drug binds to its receptor, it forms a drug-receptor complex that triggers changes in the cell's biochemical or molecular activity—a process known as signal transduction.

Protein Targets & Exceptions

Most drug receptors are proteins. Even general anesthetics, once thought to dissolve in cell membrane fats, are now known to interact with membrane proteins. However, all rules have exceptions: many antimicrobials, antitumor drugs, and mutagenic agents interact directly with DNA rather than proteins.

2. The Four Major Receptor Families

Pharmacology defines a receptor as any biologic molecule to which a drug binds to produce a measurable response (including enzymes, nucleic acids, and structural proteins). However, the richest sources of therapeutically relevant receptors are proteins that translate extracellular signals into intracellular responses, divided into four core families:

  • 1) Ligand-Gated Ion Channels: Fast-acting channels that open or close instantly upon binding.
  • 2) G Protein-Coupled Receptors (GPCRs): Membrane receptors that activate intracellular G proteins and second messengers.
  • 3) Enzyme-Linked Receptors: Receptors with intrinsic enzyme activity (such as tyrosine kinase) that phosphorylate proteins.
  • 4) Intracellular Receptors: Receptors located inside the cell (nucleus/cytoplasm) that regulate gene transcription.

💡 Chemical Nature Determines Receptor Location

Hydrophilic (water-soluble) ligands cannot cross the lipid cell membrane, so they bind to surface receptors. Hydrophobic (fat-soluble) ligands easily cross the lipid bilayer to bind with intracellular receptors inside the cell.

3. Transmembrane Signaling Mechanisms

1. Ligand-Gated Ion Channels

The extracellular portion contains the binding site. When an agonist binds, the channel opens briefly (milliseconds) to let specific ions flow across the membrane:

  • Example (Acetylcholine / Nicotinic Receptor): Opens to allow sodium ($Na^+$) influx and potassium ($K^+$) outflux, generating an action potential in neurons or muscle contraction.
  • Example (GABA Receptor): Increases chloride ($Cl^-$) influx, hyperpolarizing and calming neurons.
  • Voltage-Gated Channels: Can also be targeted by drugs (e.g., local anesthetics bind voltage-gated sodium channels to block pain signals).

2. G Protein-Coupled Receptors (GPCRs)

GPCRs feature three subunits ($\alpha, \beta, \gamma$). The $\alpha$ subunit binds GTP when activated, splitting from the $\beta\gamma$ complex to stimulate or inhibit cellular enzymes:

  • Gs & Gi: Stimulate or inhibit adenylyl cyclase, altering cyclic AMP ($cAMP$) levels.
  • Gq: Activates phospholipase C, generating $IP_3$ and $DAG$ second messengers to regulate calcium ($Ca^{2+}$) and protein kinases. Responses last seconds to minutes.

3. Enzyme-Linked Receptors

These receptors form dimers or complexes. When activated, they undergo structural changes increasing cytosolic enzyme activity (lasting minutes to hours). Most possess tyrosine kinase activity (e.g., insulin, epidermal growth factor). Binding causes self-phosphorylation (autophosphorylation), triggering a cascade of intracellular signals.

4. Intracellular Receptors

Located entirely inside the cell, requiring lipid-soluble ligands to diffuse inward. Their primary targets are nuclear transcription factors. The ligand-receptor complex enters the nucleus to regulate gene expression (transcription into RNA and translation into proteins). This process takes hours to days (e.g., steroid hormones).

Other intracellular targets include: Tubulin (paclitaxel target), dihydrofolate reductase (trimethoprim target), and bacterial 50S ribosomal subunits (macrolide antibiotics like erythromycin).

4. Dose-Response & Binding Curves

Drug binding and activation are two distinct steps. Binding is governed by affinity, while activation is governed by efficacy. Antagonists have affinity but zero efficacy.

Graded Dose-Response Relations

Plotting response against increasing doses produces a rectangular hyperbola (linear graph). Because doses span orders of magnitude, semilogarithmic plots are used to create smooth, easy-to-read sigmoidal curves.

5. Potency, Efficacy & Spare Receptors

  • Potency: The amount of drug needed to produce a given effect, measured by the EC50 (concentration causing 50% of maximal response). Lower EC50 = higher potency.
  • Efficacy (Emax): The maximum response a drug can produce when all receptors are occupied. Efficacy is clinically much more important than potency because a higher-efficacy drug provides superior therapeutic benefit.
  • Spare Receptors (Receptor Reserve): Many full agonists can elicit maximal responses at very low receptor occupancy (e.g., <1%), meaning tissue reserve capacity is substantial.

6. Full, Partial & Inverse Agonists

Agonist Type Intrinsic Activity Clinical Function & Examples
Full Agonist 1.0 (Maximum) Binds and stabilizes the active receptor state, producing maximal biologic response (e.g., phenylephrine at $\alpha_1$ receptors increases blood pressure).
Partial Agonist Between 0 and 1.0 Produces submaximal response even at 100% occupancy. Can act as an antagonist in the presence of a full agonist (e.g., aripiprazole stabilizes dopamine pathways).
Inverse Agonist Less than 0 (Negative) Stabilizes the inactive receptor form ($R$), reversing constitutive activation and producing the opposite effect of an agonist.

7. Competitive & Noncompetitive Antagonists

Antagonists bind receptors with high affinity but have zero intrinsic activity, reducing agonist effects.

Competitive Antagonism

  • Binds reversibly to the active site.
  • Shifts the agonist dose-response curve to the right (increases apparent EC50).
  • Can be completely overcome by adding higher concentrations of the agonist (Maximal efficacy $E_{max}$ remains unchanged).

Noncompetitive Antagonism

  • Binds irreversibly (often covalently) or to an allosteric site.
  • Agonists cannot surmount the inhibitory effect regardless of concentration.
    • Reduces total maximal efficacy ($E_{max}$) unless spare receptors are present.

8. Allosteric & Functional Antagonism

  • Allosteric Modulators (NAMs & PAMs): Bind to a separate site away from the active orthosteric site. Negative allosteric modulators inhibit activity, while positive allosteric modulators (like benzodiazepines binding GABA channels) enhance the net activating effect.
  • Functional / Physiological Antagonism: Occurs when two drugs act at completely separate receptors to produce opposite physiological effects.
    Classic Example: Histamine causes bronchoconstriction via $H_1$ receptors, while epinephrine causes bronchodilation via $\beta_2$ receptors.
