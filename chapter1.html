Pharmacokinetics vs. Pharmacodynamics: ADME, Receptors, Potency, Efficacy & Therapeutic Index

An intuitive, simple-to-understand breakdown tailored for BSN nursing students, featuring interactive visual animations for drug absorption, liver metabolism, renal excretion, and cellular receptor binding kinetics.

1. Pharmacokinetics: What the Body Does to the Drug

In simple terms, Pharmacokinetics (PK) tracks the journey of a medication from the moment it enters your body until it completely leaves. It is summarized by the acronym ADME: Absorption, Distribution, Metabolism, and Excretion.

💡 Nursing Memory Mnemonic: ADME

Absorption (Entering blood) → Distribution (Traveling to tissues) → Metabolism (Liver processing) → Excretion (Kidney removal).

A. Absorption & Bioavailability

Absorption is how a drug moves from the administration site (e.g., mouth, muscle, skin) into the bloodstream (systemic circulation). Before oral drugs can absorb, they must undergo liberation (dissolving in the stomach). Low stomach pH and digestive enzymes can break down some drugs before they even enter circulation.

Bioavailability ($F$): The exact percentage of the administered drug that successfully reaches your bloodstream in an active form. When a medication is given intravenously (IV), 100% of the drug enters the blood instantly, making IV the gold standard for 100% bioavailability.

First-Pass Metabolism: When you swallow an oral pill, it travels through the gut wall and directly to the liver via the portal vein. The liver metabolizes a large portion of the drug *before* it ever reaches the rest of the body, which significantly lowers oral bioavailability compared to IV.

B. Distribution & Volume of Distribution ($V_d$)

Distribution describes how the drug spreads from the bloodstream into body tissues. This is heavily influenced by Protein Binding. In the blood, drugs often bind to plasma proteins like albumin. Only free (unbound) drug can leave the blood vessels to bind to cell receptors and produce an effect or be eliminated. If a patient has low albumin (e.g., in kidney or liver failure), more free drug floats around, drastically raising the risk of toxicity!

Volume of Distribution ($V_d$): A mathematical concept describing how widely a drug spreads in body fluids. Large lipophilic (fat-loving) drugs like chloroquine have a massive $V_d$ because they hide deep inside body fat and tissues.

C. Metabolism & Excretion

Metabolism (Biotransformation): Mostly handled by the liver using CYP450 enzymes (Phase I oxidation) and glucuronidation (Phase II conjugation). The goal is to turn active, fat-soluble drugs into water-soluble compounds so the body can eliminate them.

Excretion: The permanent removal of drug waste from the body, primarily performed by the kidneys via urine (through glomerular filtration and tubular secretion), or occasionally via bile, lungs, or sweat.

2. Pharmacodynamics: What the Drug Does to the Body

Pharmacodynamics (PD) explores the drug's mechanism of action (MOA) at the cellular and receptor level. Drugs act as ligands that lock into specific cellular receptors.

A. Ligands & Receptor Binding Types

Receptor Ligand Type Intrinsic Activity ($\alpha$) Clinical Description & Action
Full Agonist 1.0 (Maximum) Binds the receptor and triggers maximum biological response (e.g., Morphine).
Partial Agonist Intermediate (>0 & <1) Binds the receptor but produces sub-maximal response, even if all receptors are occupied.
Antagonist 0.0 (Zero) Occupies the receptor without triggering activation, blocking agonists from binding (e.g., Flumazenil).
Inverse Agonist Negative (<0) Forces the receptor into an inactive state, suppressing basal resting activity.

B. Potency, Efficacy & Therapeutic Index (TI)

• Potency: The amount of drug (dose) required to produce 50% of its maximal effect ($ED_{50}$). A more potent drug requires a smaller dose.

• Efficacy ($E_{max}$): The absolute maximum therapeutic response a drug can produce, regardless of how high you raise the dose.

• Therapeutic Index (TI): A critical safety metric measuring the margin between a drug's therapeutic benefit and its toxicity:

Therapeutic Index (TI) = $\frac{TD_{50}}{ED_{50}}$    (Toxic Dose for 50% / Effective Dose for 50%)

A narrow therapeutic index (e.g., Warfarin, Digoxin, Vancomycin) means there is a very small safety margin between an effective dose and a toxic, lethal dose, requiring strict laboratory blood level monitoring.

3. Unit I: Complete Introduction to Pharmacology Syllabus Topics (1 to 9)

1. Terminologies in Pharmacology

Key terms: Pharmacology, Pharmacokinetics, Pharmacodynamics, Bioavailability, Half-life ($t_{1/2}$), Therapeutic Index, and Steady State.

2. Brief History of Pharmacology

Evolution from herbal folklore and ancient plant extracts (opium, foxglove, cinchona) to 19th-century chemical isolation and 20th-century synthetic/recombinant drug discovery.

3. Purposes of Medication

Classified into Diagnostic (contrast dyes), Prophylactic (preventative vaccines/antibiotics), Therapeutic (treating active disease), and Replacement (hormone/insulin substitution).

4. Sources of Medication

Derived from Plants (morphine, digoxin), Animals (heparin, insulin), Minerals (iron, magnesium sulfate), and Synthetic/Recombinant Laboratories.

5. Classification of Drugs

Categorized by pharmacological action or clinical therapeutic use (e.g., antihypertensives, antibiotics, diuretics).

6. Three Types of Drug Supply Systems

1. Stock Supply System (bulk ward storage).
2. Individual Prescription System (pharmacy dispenses per patient).
3. Unit-Dose Distribution System (gold-standard single-dose secure patient drawers).

7. Drug Standards and Legislation

Statutory laws and pharmacopeia standards ensuring pharmaceutical purity, strength, safety, and efficacy while regulating controlled substances.

8. Resources for Drug Information

Authorized references: Nursing Drug Handbooks, Physicians' Desk Reference (PDR), and clinical databases like Lexicomp and UpToDate.
