UNIT 1: INTRODUCTION TO PHARMACOLOGY

Comprehensive coverage of Unit I core nursing pharmacology foundations, advanced pharmacokinetics, intrinsic activity, agonist types, functional antagonism, therapeutic index, drug-response curves, potency, and efficacy.

UNIT 1: INTRODUCTION TO PHARMACOLOGY

Welcome to foundational nursing pharmacology. This module covers core definitions, historical milestones, medication purposes, classifications, supply systems, legal standards, and practical toolkits for safe nursing administration.

1. Discuss the terminologies related to pharmacology

  • Pharmacology: The comprehensive science of drugs, covering their origin, chemical composition, pharmacokinetics, pharmacodynamics, therapeutic uses, and toxicology.
  • Drug: Any chemical substance that alters biological function through molecular interactions when introduced into a living organism.
  • Medication: A substance administered for the diagnosis, cure, mitigation, treatment, or prevention of disease.
  • Generic Name: The official, non-proprietary international name of a drug assigned by the USAN (e.g., paracetamol).
  • Trade / Brand Name: The proprietary trademarked name given by the pharmaceutical manufacturer (e.g., Tylenol, Panadol).
  • Adverse Drug Reaction (ADR) / Side Effect: An unintended, noxious, or hazardous response occurring at normal therapeutic doses.

2. Discuss the history of pharmacology briefly

Pharmacology transitioned from ancient folklore and herbal remedies into an empirical molecular science:

  • Ancient Era: Reliance on plants, minerals, and animal products documented in historical texts like the Ebers Papyrus (Egypt) and ancient Ayurvedic treatises.
  • 19th Century Emergence: The isolation of active chemical compounds from crude drugs (e.g., isolation of morphine from opium by Friedrich Sertürner in 1805, digitalis from foxglove).
  • Modern Era: Breakthroughs in synthetic chemistry, molecular biology, genomics, and monoclonal antibodies, establishing rigorous clinical trials and regulatory frameworks.

3. Identify the purposes of medication

Medications serve several core healthcare functions:

  • Diagnostic: Agents used to identify diseases (e.g., radiopaque contrast media for CT scans, barium sulfate).
  • Curative: Agents that eliminate pathogens or cure disease states (e.g., antibiotics curing bacterial pneumonia).
  • Palliative: Agents that relieve symptoms and improve quality of life without curing the underlying disease (e.g., analgesics in terminal cancer care).
  • Prophylactic (Preventive): Agents given to prevent illnesses before they occur (e.g., vaccines, prophylactic antibiotics prior to surgical incision).
  • Replacement: Agents that supplement missing physiological body chemicals (e.g., insulin for type 1 diabetes mellitus, levothyroxine for hypothyroidism).

4. Identify the source of medication

Source Category Description & Clinical Examples
Plant Sources Derived from roots, leaves, barks, or seeds (e.g., digoxin from foxglove, morphine from opium poppy, atropine).
Animal Sources Extracted from animal tissues or organs (e.g., animal insulin, heparin extracted from porcine intestinal mucosa).
Mineral Sources Inorganic elements and minerals from the earth (e.g., magnesium sulfate, ferrous sulfate, potassium chloride).
Synthetic & Biosynthetic Created in laboratories via organic chemistry or recombinant DNA technology (e.g., synthetic human insulin, sulfonamides, aspirin).

5. Discuss the classification of drugs

Drugs are categorized based on multiple standardized systems:

  • Therapeutic Classification: Categorized by what clinical condition they treat (e.g., antihypertensives, antidepressants, antihyperglycemics).
  • Pharmacological Classification: Categorized by how they act at the molecular or systemic level (e.g., beta-blockers, ACE inhibitors, calcium channel blockers).
  • Body System Classification: Grouped according to the physiological system they affect (e.g., cardiovascular drugs, respiratory agents, central nervous system drugs).

6. Describe the three types of drug supply system

Hospitals and healthcare facilities utilize structured systems to store, manage, and distribute medications safely:

  • Floor Stock System: Bulk supplies of common medications are kept on nursing units. Nurses dispense directly from floor stock; highly vulnerable to medication errors and inventory discrepancies.
  • Individual Prescription Order System: Medications are dispensed from the pharmacy specifically for an individual patient in a designated supply drawer for a 24-to-72-hour period.
  • Unit-Dose System: The gold standard safety system. Medications are dispensed by the pharmacy in single-unit, pre-packaged, pre-labeled doses ready to administer directly to the specific patient, reducing preparation errors.

7. Discuss the drugs standards and legislation

National and international laws protect public health by establishing strict standards for drug purity, strength, quality, safety, and efficacy:

  • Pharmacopeias & Formularies: Official public standards books (e.g., United States Pharmacopeia - USP, British Pharmacopoeia) defining medication composition and purity tests.
  • Regulatory Agencies: Government bodies (e.g., FDA in the United States, DRAP in Pakistan) responsible for approving new drugs, monitoring adverse events, and enforcing manufacturing compliance.
  • Controlled Substance Acts: Legislation regulating narcotics and habit-forming drugs, requiring secure double-locked storage, exact inventory counts, and witnessed waste disposal.

8. Identify resource to collect and utilize drug information

Nurses must consult verified, evidence-based literature before administering medications:

  • Standard Nursing Drug Handbooks: (e.g., Nursing Central, Mosby's Drug Guide).
  • Pharmacist Consultations & Hospital Formularies: Institutional references outlining approved drug lists, dosages, and IV compatibility.
  • Electronic Databases & Apps: UpToDate, Lexicomp, Micromedex, and official manufacturer prescribing information sheets.

9. Learn to prepare drugs cards

Drug cards are essential clinical learning tools used by nursing students to master pharmacology safely. A standard clinical drug card includes:

  • Generic & Brand Name: Proper spelling and pronunciation.
  • Pharmacological Classification & Mechanism of Action: Drug category and how it works.
  • Indication: Approved clinical uses and reasons the patient is taking it.
  • Dosage, Route & Safe Parameters: Standard clinical doses, routes, and baseline vital sign cutoffs (e.g., hold if heart rate under 60 bpm for digoxin).
  • Adverse Effects & Nursing Interventions: Common side effects, toxicities, lab monitoring, and patient education.

PHARMACOKINETICS (What the Body Does to the Drug)

Pharmacokinetics is the quantitative study of how a drug moves through the body over time. It encompasses four core stages remembered by the acronym ADME, along with liberation kinetics and comprehensive factors affecting each phase.

Absorption & Factors Affecting Absorption

Liberation is the release of a drug from its pharmaceutical dosage form. Absorption is the net movement of active drug molecules from the site of administration across biological membranes into systemic circulation.

Factors Affecting Drug Absorption:

  • Route of Administration: Intravenous (IV) bypasses absorption entirely (100% bioavailability). Oral (PO) drugs must traverse gastrointestinal mucosal barriers.
  • Surface Area & Blood Flow: Larger absorbing surfaces (e.g., small intestinal microvilli) and high regional blood perfusion accelerate absorption rates.
  • Lipid Solubility & Ionization (pH): Un-ionized, lipophilic drugs diffuse across cell membranes easily. Gastric pH affects weak acids/bases ionization states.
  • First-Pass Metabolism: High hepatic clearance of orally administered drugs significantly reduces systemic bioavailability before reaching general circulation.
  • Food and Drug Interactions: Presence of food, gastric emptying rate, and concurrent administration of binding agents (e.g., antacids) can alter drug absorption.

Distribution & Factors Affecting Distribution

Distribution refers to the reversible transfer of drug from systemic circulation into interstitial and intracellular tissue fluids.

The Volume of Distribution (Vd) is a theoretical pharmacokinetic parameter relating the total amount of drug in the body to the plasma drug concentration.

Factors Affecting Drug Distribution:

  • Plasma Protein Binding: Drugs reversibly bind to plasma proteins (primarily albumin). Only the free, unbound fraction is pharmacologically active and able to diffuse to target receptors.
  • Tissue Perfusion & Blood Flow: Highly vascularized organs (brain, heart, liver, kidneys) receive rapid initial distribution, whereas adipose tissue and bone receive slower distribution.
  • Membrane Permeability: Specialized barriers like the blood-brain barrier restrict passage of hydrophilic compounds, requiring high lipophilicity to enter the central nervous system.

Metabolism & Factors Affecting Metabolism

Metabolism (Biotransformation) is the chemical alteration of a drug within the body, primarily occurring in the liver via enzymatic systems (e.g., Cytochrome P450 enzymes) to convert active lipophilic drugs into inactive, water-soluble metabolites for renal elimination.

Factors Affecting Drug Metabolism:

  • Enzyme Induction & Inhibition: Certain drugs induce hepatic enzymes (increasing breakdown rates of other drugs), while others inhibit enzymes (causing drug accumulation and toxicity).
  • Age & Organ Function: Infants have immature hepatic enzyme systems, and older adults have reduced liver mass and blood flow, slowing metabolic rates.
  • Hepatic Disease: Cirrhosis or hepatitis significantly reduces metabolic capacity, requiring lower drug dosages to avoid toxicity.
  • Genetics (Pharmacogenomics): Genetic polymorphisms can create "rapid metabolizers" or "poor metabolizers" for certain drug pathways.

Excretion & Factors Affecting Excretion

Excretion is the irreversible removal of drugs and their metabolites from the body, primarily carried out by the kidneys into urine, but also via bile, feces, sweat, and exhaled air.

Factors Affecting Drug Excretion:

  • Renal Function: Measured by Glomerular Filtration Rate (GFR) and Creatinine Clearance. Impaired renal function (e.g., chronic kidney disease) severely delays excretion, risking toxicity.
  • Urine pH: Alters the ionization state of drugs in the renal tubules. For example, alkalinizing urine with sodium bicarbonate accelerates the excretion of weak acids like aspirin during an overdose.
  • Active Tubular Secretion: Transport pumps in the kidney move drugs into urine; multiple drugs competing for these pumps can delay excretion.

PHARMACODYNAMICS (What the Drug Does to the Body)

Pharmacodynamics evaluates the biochemical and physiological effects of drugs and their mechanisms of action at cellular receptors.

Intrinsic Activity & Agonist Types

Drugs exert effects based on their affinity (ability to bind to a receptor) and intrinsic activity (efficacy) — the ability to initiate a biological response after binding.

  • Full Agonist: Binds to the receptor and produces a maximal biological response (Intrinsic activity = 1).
  • Partial Agonist: Binds to the receptor but produces a submaximal response, even if all receptors are occupied. It can act as a competitive antagonist in the presence of a full agonist (Intrinsic activity between 0 and 1).
  • Inverse Agonist: Binds to the receptor and induces an effect opposite to that of the normal agonist, reducing constitutive basal receptor activity (Intrinsic activity is negative).

Functional Antagonist

Also known as physiological antagonism. A functional antagonist is a drug that counters the effects of another drug by binding to a completely different receptor system, producing an opposite physiological effect.

Example: Histamine causes severe bronchoconstriction during anaphylaxis by binding to H1 receptors. Epinephrine is a functional antagonist; it binds to beta-2 adrenergic receptors to cause powerful bronchodilation, rescuing the airway.

Receptor Superfamilies

Proteins on cell membranes or within cells that interact with drugs:

  • Ligand-Gated Ion Channels: Extremely fast (milliseconds). Opens an ion pore upon drug binding (e.g., GABA receptors).
  • G-Protein Coupled Receptors (GPCRs): Medium speed (seconds). Uses secondary messengers like cAMP inside the cell (e.g., Adrenergic receptors).
  • Enzyme-Linked Receptors: Slow (minutes). Binding activates an intracellular enzyme cascade (e.g., Insulin receptors).
  • Intracellular/Nuclear Receptors: Very slow (hours to days). Lipophilic drugs enter the cell to alter DNA transcription (e.g., Steroid hormones).

Therapeutic Index & Drug-Response Curve

The Therapeutic Index (TI) is a measure of drug safety, calculated as the ratio between the toxic dose (TD50) and the effective therapeutic dose (ED50).

  • Narrow Therapeutic Index: The safe dose is very close to the toxic dose. Requires strict blood level monitoring (e.g., Warfarin, Lithium, Digoxin).
  • Potency vs. Efficacy: Potency is the amount of drug needed to produce a given effect (determined by receptor affinity). Efficacy is the maximum effect a drug can produce (determined by intrinsic activity). Efficacy is clinically more important than potency.

Clinical Significance in Nursing

Understanding these principles is non-negotiable for safe nursing practice. The nurse must assess renal and hepatic function before medication administration, predict how food will alter absorption, and recognize whether a patient's genetic profile or age puts them at risk for a narrowed therapeutic index.
