Pharmacokinetics vs. Pharmacodynamics: ADME, Receptors & Clinical Calculations

An intuitive, comprehensive breakdown tailored for nursing students, featuring simple explanations of ADME, live interactive simulators, receptor dynamics, therapeutic index, potency, and efficacy.

1. Pharmacokinetics: What the Body Does to the Drug

Pharmacokinetics (PK) is the study of how the body interacts with administered medications for the entire duration of exposure. In simple terms: What does your body do to the drug from the moment you take it until it leaves?

This field is governed by four core parameters summarized by the acronym ADME: Absorption, Distribution, Metabolism, and Excretion.

💡 Nursing Memory Mnemonic: ADME

Absorption (Getting into blood) → Distribution (Getting to tissues) → Metabolism (Processing in liver) → Excretion (Clearing via kidneys).

Absorption & Bioavailability

Absorption brings a drug from its administration site (e.g., tablet, capsule, or injection) into the systemic circulation. Before oral medications can be absorbed, they must undergo liberation (disintegration and dissolution in the digestive tract). Low stomach pH can chemically alter some drugs before they even reach circulation.

Bioavailability is the exact fraction of the administered drug that successfully reaches systemic circulation:

  • Intravenous (IV) Administration: 100% of the drug enters circulation instantly. Therefore, IV is the gold standard for bioavailability (100%).
  • Oral (PO) Administration: Must navigate stomach acid and digestive enzymes. Once absorbed by gut transporters, oral drugs pass through the portal system into the liver, experiencing first-pass metabolism, which substantially lowers the final amount reaching circulation.

For medications with delayed dissemination (such as oral, intramuscular, or transdermal routes), clinicians use the Area Under the Curve (AUC) on a plasma concentration graph over time to calculate total bioavailability.

Distribution & Volume of Distribution (Vd)

Distribution describes how a substance spreads throughout the body's fluid compartments, influenced by diffusion, convection, lipid solubility, and molecular size.

  • Volume of Distribution (Vd): A theoretical metric calculated as the total amount of drug in the body divided by its plasma drug concentration. Large, charged, or strongly protein-bound drugs (like cetrorelix) stay in the blood (low Vd). Small, lipophilic drugs (like chloroquine) diffuse extensively into tissues and adipose cells (very large Vd).
  • Loading Doses: Knowing Vd allows clinicians to calculate a loading dose (e.g., Vancomycin in severe infections) so drug concentrations reach therapeutic levels immediately rather than accumulating slowly over time.

Protein Binding

In the bloodstream, drugs can exist as either protein-bound or free. Only free (unbound) drug can cross membranes, bind to receptors, and exert pharmacological action or be eliminated.

Albumin and alpha-acid glycoprotein are the primary binding proteins. Conditions like renal failure or severe liver disease decrease plasma proteins (hypoalbuminemia), leaving more drug unbound in circulation. This drastically increases drug effects and toxicity risks (e.g., enhanced sedation or respiratory depression from standard doses of diazepam).

Metabolism & Excretion

Metabolism (biotransformation) converts drugs into more water-soluble compounds for elimination, primarily in the liver via Phase I (CYP450 oxidation) and Phase II (conjugation) reactions. Some inactive drugs called prodrugs (e.g., codeine) require metabolism to become active.

Excretion is the permanent removal of drugs from the body, chiefly via the kidneys through glomerular filtration and tubular secretion. Clearance measures how efficiently an organ removes a drug from the plasma. The half-life ($t_{1/2}$) is the time required for serum drug concentration to drop by 50%.

Zero-Order vs. First-Order Kinetics

  • First-Order Kinetics: Most medications follow this model, where a constant fraction of the drug is eliminated per unit time, resulting in a constant half-life.
  • Zero-Order Kinetics: Elimination occurs at a constant rate regardless of drug concentration (e.g., alcohol, high-dose aspirin, phenytoin). Half-life decreases as overall serum concentration drops.

Under continuous infusion, a drug reaches a steady state (input equals clearance) after 4 to 5 half-lives. Similarly, 94% to 97% of a drug is eliminated from the body after 4 to 5 half-lives.

2. Pharmacodynamics: What the Drug Does to the Body

Pharmacodynamics (PD) explores the biochemical and physiological mechanisms of action (MOA) by which drugs produce their therapeutic effects or side effects at cellular receptors.

Ligands & Receptors Dynamics

Any molecule that binds to a cellular receptor is called a ligand.

Receptor Ligand Type Intrinsic Activity Clinical Description & Action
Agonist High (1.0) Enhances receptor activity by mimicking endogenous messengers (e.g., Benzodiazepines facilitating GABA).
Antagonist Zero (0.0) Blocks agonists without triggering activation (e.g., Flumazenil reversing benzodiazepines).
Inverse Agonist Negative Reverses intrinsic activity, producing the opposite effect of an agonist.
Partial Agonist Intermediate Acts as an agonist when natural ligands are low, and as a blocker when natural ligands are in excess (e.g., Buspirone).

Signal Transduction & Gene Regulation

Neurotransmitters interact with two main receptor types:

  • Ionotropic Receptors (Ligand-Gated Channels): Rapidly swinging doors governing ion flux. Inhibitory transmitters (GABA, glycine) allow $Cl^-$ influx to hyperpolarize cells. Excitatory transmitters (glutamate, aspartate) allow $Na^+$ or $Ca^{2+}$ influx to depolarize cells and fire action potentials.
  • G-Protein-Coupled Receptors (GPCRs): Slower relay stations using secondary messengers like cAMP and protein kinases to trigger phosphorylation. This leads to long-lasting intracellular adaptations, genetic transcription, and receptor up- or down-regulation.

Potency, Efficacy & Therapeutic Index (TI)

• Potency: The dose required to produce 50% of a drug's maximal effect ($ED_{50}$). A highly potent drug requires a very small milligram dose.

• Efficacy ($E_{max}$): The maximum physiological response a drug can produce, regardless of how much the dose is increased.

• Therapeutic Index (TI): A crucial indicator of drug safety comparing the toxic dose to the effective dose:

Therapeutic Index (TI) = $TD_{50}$ / $ED_{50}$    (Toxic Dose 50 / Effective Dose 50)

Drugs with a narrow therapeutic index (e.g., Digoxin, Lithium, Warfarin) have a very small margin of safety between healing doses and lethal toxicity, requiring rigorous lab monitoring.
