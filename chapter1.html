Pharmacokinetics vs. Pharmacodynamics: ADME, Detailed Factors, Receptors & Unit I Syllabus

An intuitive, comprehensive breakdown tailored for BSN nursing students, featuring in-depth explanations of ADME factors, clinical exam pearls, and interactive visual simulators.

1. Pharmacokinetics: What the Body Does to the Drug & Detailed Factors

In clinical nursing, Pharmacokinetics (PK) tracks the quantitative movement of medications through the body. It is governed by four key processes summarized by ADME: Absorption, Distribution, Metabolism, and Excretion/Elimination.

💡 Nursing Memory Mnemonic: ADME

Absorption (Getting into blood) → Distribution (Getting to tissues) → Metabolism (Processing in liver) → Excretion (Clearing via kidneys).

A. Absorption & Factors Affecting Absorption

Absorption is the transfer of an unchanged drug from its site of administration into the systemic circulation. For oral drugs, liberation must occur first (tablet disintegration and dissolution).

  • Route of Administration: IV route provides 100% bioavailability instantly. Oral (PO) drugs must traverse mucosal barriers and face degradation.
  • Surface Area & Blood Supply: The small intestine has massive surface area and rich blood flow, making it the primary site of drug absorption compared to the stomach.
  • Lipid Solubility (Lipophilicity): Uncharged, non-ionized, fat-loving drugs cross cell membranes rapidly via passive diffusion.
  • Food and Drug Interactions: Food can delay gastric emptying, bind drugs (e.g., dairy calcium binding with tetracyclines), or alter pH.
  • First-Pass Effect: Oral drugs pass through the portal venous system directly to the liver, where a substantial percentage is metabolized before reaching general circulation.

B. Distribution & Factors Affecting Distribution

Distribution is the reversible transfer of a drug from the bloodstream into interstitial and intracellular tissue fluids.

  • Plasma Protein Binding (Albumin): Many drugs bind reversibly to plasma proteins. Only the free (unbound) drug fraction is pharmacologically active and able to diffuse to target receptors. Hypoalbuminemia (liver/kidney disease) increases free drug levels, predisposing patients to toxicity.
  • Blood Flow to Tissues: Highly perfused organs (brain, heart, liver, kidneys) receive drugs rapidly; fat, skin, and bone receive drugs much slower.
  • Membrane Permeability: Highly lipophilic drugs easily cross the Blood-Brain Barrier (BBB) or placenta, affecting the central nervous system or fetus.

C. Metabolism (Biotransformation) & Factors Affecting It

Metabolism chemically alters parent drugs into more polar, water-soluble metabolites for easier elimination. Primarily executed in the liver via Phase I (oxidation/reduction via Cytochrome P450 enzymes) and Phase II (conjugation) reactions.

  • Enzyme Induction & Inhibition: Certain drugs (e.g., rifampin, barbiturates) induce CYP450 enzymes, accelerating the breakdown of other drugs. Inhibitors (e.g., grapefruit juice, erythromycin) slow metabolism, risking drug toxicity.
  • Age & Organ Function: Neonates have immature liver enzyme systems, and older adults experience reduced hepatic blood flow and enzyme mass, prolonging drug half-lives.
  • First-Pass Metabolism: High hepatic extraction ratios significantly lower the systemic availability of drugs like nitroglycerin or propranolol when given orally.

D. Elimination/Excretion & Factors Affecting It

Excretion is the permanent removal of drugs or metabolites from the body, principally through renal filtration and urine output, though biliary, pulmonary, and sweat routes also contribute.

  • Renal Function (GFR & Creatinine Clearance): Renal impairment drastically delays excretion, leading to drug accumulation and toxicity unless doses are adjusted based on renal function tests.
  • Urine pH: Altering urine pH can trap ionized drug molecules in the renal tubules, enhancing elimination (e.g., alkalinizing urine with sodium bicarbonate clears weak acid drugs like aspirin).
  • Half-Life ($t_{1/2}$): The time required for serum drug concentration to decrease by 50%. It determines dosing frequency and time to reach Steady State (typically achieved in 4 to 5 half-lives of regular dosing).

2. Pharmacodynamics: What the Drug Does to the Body

Pharmacodynamics (PD) explores the biochemical and physiological effects of drugs and their mechanisms of action (MOA) at cellular receptors.

A. Ligands & Receptor Binding Types

Receptor Ligand Type Intrinsic Activity (α) Clinical Description & Action
Full Agonist 1.0 (Maximum) Binds the receptor and triggers maximum biological response (e.g., Morphine).
Partial Agonist Intermediate (>0 & <1) Binds the receptor but produces sub-maximal response, even if all receptors are occupied.
Antagonist 0.0 (Zero) Occupies the receptor without triggering activation, blocking agonists from binding (e.g., Naloxone).
Inverse Agonist Negative (<0) Forces the receptor into an inactive state, suppressing basal resting activity.

B. Potency, Efficacy & Therapeutic Index (TI)

• Potency: The amount of drug (dose) required to produce 50% of its maximal effect ($ED_{50}$). A more potent drug requires a smaller milligram dose.

• Efficacy ($E_{max}$): The absolute maximum therapeutic response a drug can elicit, independent of dose size.

• Therapeutic Index (TI): A critical clinical safety margin indicator:

Therapeutic Index (TI) = $TD_{50}$ / $ED_{50}$    (Toxic Dose 50 / Effective Dose 50)

A narrow therapeutic index (e.g., Digoxin, Warfarin, Theophylline) means toxicity is dangerously close to therapeutic levels, mandating strict routine serum blood level monitoring and nurse vigilance.

3. Unit I: Complete Introduction to Pharmacology Syllabus Topics (1 to 9)

1. Terminologies in Pharmacology

Key terms: Pharmacology (science of drugs and their interactions), Pharmacokinetics (ADME), Pharmacodynamics (drug mechanism and cellular effects), Bioavailability (fraction reaching systemic circulation unchanged), Half-life ($t_{1/2}$), Therapeutic Index (safety margin), and Steady State (rate of drug input equals rate of elimination).

2. Brief History of Pharmacology

Evolution from herbal folklore and ancient plant extracts (opium for pain, foxglove/digitalis for heart failure, cinchona bark/quinine for malaria) to 19th-century chemical isolation of active compounds and 20th-century synthetic/recombinant drug discovery.

3. Purposes of Medication

Classified into Diagnostic (e.g., radiopaque contrast dyes), Prophylactic (e.g., vaccines or pre-surgical antibiotics), Therapeutic (e.g., antihypertensives treating active hypertension), and Replacement (e.g., insulin for diabetes or thyroid hormone replacement).

4. Sources of Medication

Derived from Plants (morphine, digoxin, atropine), Animals (porcine/bovine heparin, insulin historically), Minerals (ferrous sulfate, magnesium sulfate), and Synthetic/Recombinant Laboratories (synthesized antibiotics, monoclonal antibodies).

5. Classification of Drugs

Categorized by pharmacological action, chemical structure, or clinical therapeutic use (e.g., antihypertensives, broad-spectrum antibiotics, loop diuretics, analgesics).

6. Three Types of Drug Supply Systems

1. Stock Supply System: Bulk quantities stored on hospital wards (higher risk of medication errors and diversion).
2. Individual Prescription System: Pharmacy dispenses specific medication amounts per individual patient.
3. Unit-Dose Distribution System: The gold-standard single-dose secure patient drawer system managed by pharmacy to maximize patient safety.

7. Drug Standards and Legislation

Statutory laws, regulatory bodies (e.g., FDA/DRAP), and official pharmacopeia standards ensuring pharmaceutical purity, strength, safety, and efficacy while strictly regulating controlled narcotics.

8. Resources for Drug Information

Authorized, evidence-based references: Nursing Drug Handbooks, Physicians' Desk Reference (PDR), and online clinical databases like Lexicomp, Micromedex, and UpToDate.
