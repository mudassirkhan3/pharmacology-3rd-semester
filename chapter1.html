Core Definitions & The Modern Paradigm Shift

To master psychopharmacology, one must first master the fundamental dichotomy:

  • Pharmacodynamics (PD) = What a drug does to your body (mechanisms of action, receptor binding, and physiological response).
  • Pharmacokinetics (PK) = What your body does to a drug (absorption, distribution, metabolism, and elimination).

Historically, antidepressants and antipsychotics were understood solely through superficial receptor binding or biogenic amine levels (e.g., serotonin, dopamine, norepinephrine). Modern neuroscience reveals a much deeper reality: many psychotropics alter neuronal function directly at the level of the gene. Specific gene products and neurotrophic growth factors represent the true "business end" of clinical efficacy.

1. Pharmacodynamics (PD): Mechanism of Action

Pharmacodynamics examines how drugs trigger therapeutic or adverse effects (such as dry mouth, sedation, or orthostatic hypotension). While receptor binding initiates the cascade, the ultimate effects run much deeper.

Ligands and Receptor Dynamics

Any molecule binding to a cellular receptor is a ligand. Ligands are categorized into four functional classes:

  • Agonist: Enhances receptor activity by mimicking endogenous ligands (e.g., Benzodiazepines act as agonists at GABA receptors, increasing $Cl^-$ ion influx and neural inhibition).
  • Antagonist (Neutral): Possesses zero intrinsic activity but blocks agonists, restoring baseline receptor resting state (e.g., Flumazenil reversing benzodiazepine overdose).
  • Inverse Agonist: Produces the opposite pharmacological effect of an agonist, diminishing baseline activity (e.g., beta-carbolines reducing chloride channel flow to provoke anxiety).
  • Partial Agonist: Acts as an antagonist when endogenous ligands are excessive, and as an agonist when endogenous ligands are scarce (e.g., Buspirone at 5-$HT_{1A}$ receptors).

Autoreceptors vs. Heteroreceptors: Autoreceptors reside on neurons producing their corresponding endogenous ligand (e.g., serotonin autoreceptors on serotonergic neurons). Heteroreceptors reside on neurons producing a different neurotransmitter (e.g., serotonin heteroreceptors on dopaminergic neurons), allowing complex cross-transmitter feedback loops.

Neurotransmitters & Signal Transduction

Neurotransmitters interact via two primary receptor architectures:

  • Ionotropic Receptors (Ligand-Gated): Rapidly swinging doors governing instant ion flux. Inhibitory transmitters (GABA, glycine) allow $Cl^-$ influx to hyperpolarize cells. Excitatory transmitters (glutamate, aspartate) allow $Na^+$ or $Ca^{2+}$ influx to depolarize cells.
  • G-Protein–Coupled Receptors (GPCRs): Slower relay stations utilizing secondary messengers (cAMP, inositol trisphosphate) and protein kinases to trigger protein phosphorylation and long-term genetic transcription (via tertiary messengers like c-fos and mRNA, leading to growth factors like BDNF).

2. Pharmacokinetics (PK): ADME Principles

Pharmacokinetics encompasses all bodily processes governing drug movement through the system: Absorption, Distribution, Metabolism, and Elimination (ADME).

Figure 1.2: Systemic drug absorption and pharmacokinetic profile across body compartments.

Absorption & Presystemic Elimination

Oral drug absorption is primarily a passive process occurring in the small intestine. Before reaching general circulation, oral drugs undergo presystemic elimination via three major physiological filters:

  • Intestinal CYP Enzymes: Localized oxidative metabolism in the gut epithelium.
  • P-glycoprotein (P-gp): An efflux transmembrane pump that extrudes drugs back into the intestinal lumen.
  • First-Pass Hepatic Extraction: Portal blood delivers absorbed drug straight to the liver, where enzymes metabolize a significant fraction before systemic entry.

Distribution & Protein Binding

CNS distribution speed depends heavily on lipophilicity (fat solubility). Highly lipophilic drugs (like diazepam) enter the brain rapidly but quickly redistribute into peripheral adipose tissue. Only free (unbound) drug can cross membranes and act at target receptors, though protein-binding shifts have minimal clinical impact because the body is an open system that continuously clears unbound drug.

Elimination & Steady State

Elimination half-life ($t_{1/2}\beta$) is the time required for plasma concentration to drop by half. Steady state (SS)—where drug elimination equals administration rate—is achieved in 4 to 5 half-lives.

To fully understand patient variability in drug response, clinicians must evaluate the specific physiological, pathological, and biochemical factors influencing each stage of ADME:

3. Drug Metabolism: Cytochrome P450 & Glucuronidation

Metabolism relies on two principal biochemical pathways:

  • Phase I Reactions: Oxidative, reductive, and hydrolytic processes (primarily hepatic via the Cytochrome P450 (CYP) heme-containing enzyme system) yielding water-soluble polar molecules.
  • Phase II Reactions (Conjugation): Glucuronidation occurring across the liver, kidneys, intestines, lungs, and brain. Crucially, glucuronidation is far less impacted by aging than Phase I CYP systems, making drugs eliminated strictly via glucuronidation (e.g., lorazepam, oxazepam) much safer in geriatric patients.

Major CYP Families & Clinical Substrates

  • CYP 3A4/5: The largest family (~50% of oxidative drug metabolism). Metabolizes tricyclics, triazolo-benzodiazepines, sertraline, quetiapine, and risperidone. Strongly inhibited by grapefruit juice.
  • CYP 2D6: Accounts for ~30% of oxidative metabolism. Responsible for hydroxylating all tricyclic antidepressants (TCAs), select SSRIs, and antipsychotics.
  • CYP 1A2: Metabolizes TCAs, mirtazapine, clozapine, olanzapine, and caffeine.
  • CYP 2C (2C9/2C19): Metabolizes citalopram, diazepam, barbiturates, and phenytoin.

4. Clinical Considerations & Drug–Drug Interactions

Clinicians must maintain constant vigilance regarding pharmacokinetic interactions, especially in high-risk patient contexts:

  • Elderly or medically compromised patients with diminished hepatic or renal function.
  • Patients maintained on high-dose regimens approaching toxic thresholds.
  • Co-prescription of two or more inhibitors sharing the same CYP metabolic pathway.
  • Abrupt cessation of a CYP inhibitor (leading to rapid substrate level drops and loss of efficacy).
  • Abrupt cessation of a CYP inducer (leading to dangerous substrate accumulation and toxicity).

💡 High-Yield Clinical Rule of Thumb

Enzyme Induction takes time (requires new protein synthesis over days to weeks). Enzyme Inhibition is rapid (often manifesting within days of introducing the inhibiting agent).
