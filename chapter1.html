Pharmacokinetics (PK): What Your Body Does to Medication

Based on the foundational work by Sean Grogan and Charles V. Preuss. Explore how drugs travel, adapt, get metabolized, and exit the human body through simple explanations, real-life examples, and interactive ADME animations.

Definition & Introduction

Pharmacokinetics (PK) is the study of how the human body interacts with administered substances throughout their entire duration of exposure. In simple terms: PK is what your body does to the drug.

This is closely related to, but distinct from, pharmacodynamics (PD), which examines what the drug does to your body (its biological effects and receptor interactions).

🎯 Why This Matters for Clinicians and Nurses

Understanding PK allows healthcare practitioners to prescribe and administer medications that provide the greatest benefit at the lowest risk, making precise dosage adjustments based on individual patient physiology and lifestyle.

Pharmacokinetics generally examines these four main parameters, famously known as ADME:

  • Absorption
  • Distribution
  • Metabolism
  • Excretion

1. Absorption & Liberation

Absorption is the process that brings a drug from its administration point (such as a tablet or capsule) into your systemic blood circulation. It dictates how fast and in what concentration a drug arrives at its target destination.

Methods of Drug Administration

Drugs can enter the body through many routes, each with unique advantages and absorption speeds:

  • Oral, intravenous (IV), intramuscular, intrathecal, subcutaneous
  • Buccal, rectal, vaginal, ocular, otic
  • Inhaled, nebulized, and transdermal (skin patches)

💡 The Role of Liberation in Oral Medications

Before an oral pill can be absorbed, it undergoes liberation—the process where the drug is released from its solid dosage form. If an oral pill gets stuck in the esophagus for hours, it can delay healing or even cause local mucosal damage. Once in the stomach, acidic fluids can chemically react with the drug before it ever reaches the bloodstream.

Bioavailability & The Area Under the Curve (AUC)

Bioavailability (F) is the fraction of the originally administered drug dose that successfully arrives in systemic circulation in an active form. It is a direct reflection of absorption efficiency.

Route Bioavailability Clinical Reason
Intravenous (IV) 100% (Gold Standard) Injected directly into the vein, bypassing digestive breakdown and liver filtering entirely.
Oral Tablets Varies (often lower) Must survive stomach acid, digestive enzymes, and hepatic first-pass metabolism.

What is First-Pass Metabolism?

When you swallow an oral medication, it is absorbed by gut transporters and routed straight to the liver via the portal vein. The liver and gut wall process large quantities of the drug before it reaches the rest of the body. This first-pass metabolism significantly lowers the final amount of active drug entering general circulation.

Understanding AUC (Area Under the Curve)

For drugs given orally, intramuscularly, or transdermally, absorption happens over time rather than all at once. Clinicians use the Area Under the Curve (AUC) on a graph measuring plasma concentration over time to calculate total drug bioavailability compared to a 100% IV baseline.

2. Distribution & Volume of Distribution (Vd)

Distribution describes how a substance spreads throughout the body's tissues. In the simplest sense, distribution is driven by two main physical forces: diffusion and convection.

Distribution is heavily influenced by drug polarity, molecular size, tissue binding abilities, patient hydration status, protein levels, and total body fat (habitus).

The ultimate goal of distribution is achieving the effective drug concentration—the exact concentration needed at the receptor site to create a therapeutic response.

Volume of Distribution (Vd)

The Volume of Distribution (Vd) is a mathematical metric describing how widely a drug disperses throughout the body's theoretical fluid compartments:

Vd = Total Amount of Drug in Body / Plasma Drug Concentration

  • Low Vd (Intravascular): Large, electrically charged, or heavily protein-bound molecules (like cetrorelix, Vd = 0.39 L/kg) stay trapped inside blood vessels.
  • Medium Vd (Extracellular): Small, hydrophilic molecules distribute through blood and extracellular fluids.
  • Very High Vd (Tissues/Fat): Small, lipophilic (fat-soluble) molecules (like chloroquine, Vd = 140 L/kg) diffuse deep into body cells and adipose tissue.

💡 Why Vd Matters: Loading Doses

Knowing Vd is vital for calculating correct loading doses. For example, a patient with a severe infection may need an initial loading dose of vancomycin so drug concentrations rapidly reach therapeutic levels without waiting for slow accumulation. Loading dose is calculated as:
Vd × Desired Plasma Concentration / Bioavailability.

Protein Binding & The Free Drug Rule

Inside the bloodstream, drugs can exist in two states: protein-bound or free (unbound).

Only free drug can act at pharmacological receptor sites, cross fluid barriers, or be eliminated by organs. The primary blood proteins responsible for binding medications are albumin and alpha-1-acid glycoprotein.

💡 Clinical Example: Renal Failure & Toxicity

In patients with kidney failure, uremia decreases the ability of acidic drugs (like diazepam) to bind to serum proteins. Even though the standard dose is given, there is far more free drug circulating in the active space. This sharply increases medication potency and the risk of severe toxicity, such as respiratory depression.

3. Metabolism & The Liver

Metabolism is the biochemical processing of a drug by the body into subsequent compounds. Its primary goal is converting fat-soluble (lipophilic) drugs into water-soluble (hydrophilic) substances so the kidneys can filter and clear them.

For prodrugs (like codeine), metabolism is required to convert an inactive substance into its active metabolite.

Phase I and Phase II Liver Reactions

While metabolism can occur in the gut, skin, lungs, and kidneys, the vast majority happens in the liver through two key phases:

  • Phase I Reactions (CYP450 enzymes): Transform substances into polar metabolites via oxidation, preparing them for Phase II.
  • Phase II Reactions (UGT enzymes): Conjugate large molecules onto the drug to make it fully water-soluble for excretion in urine or bile.

4. Excretion & Clearance

Excretion is the process by which drug waste is permanently eliminated from the body. While the kidneys handle the vast majority of excretion via glomerular filtration and tubular secretion, some drugs leave via the lungs, skin, or gastrointestinal tract.

Clearance and Maintenance Dosing

Clearance is the ratio of a drug's elimination rate to its plasma drug concentration. It depends heavily on organ blood flow and kidney status.

Understanding clearance allows practitioners to calculate accurate maintenance doses—doses designed to steadily replace the exact amount of drug eliminated since the last administration.
Maintenance Dose = Clearance × Desired Plasma Concentration / Bioavailability.

5. Drug Kinetics, Half-Life, and Steady State

Drug kinetics tracks how plasma concentrations change over time as metabolism and elimination take place. The two major kinetic models are:

  • First-Order Kinetics: The body eliminates a constant proportion (percentage) of the drug per unit of time. The half-life is constant, and clearance decreases as concentration drops. This applies to most medications.
  • Zero-Order Kinetics: The body eliminates a constant fixed amount per hour because metabolic enzymes are fully saturated.
    Example: Alcohol and high-dose salicylates or phenytoin.

Half-Life ($t_{1/2}$) and Steady State

The half-life is the time required for serum drug concentrations to decrease by 50%. It is directly proportional to Vd and inversely proportional to clearance:
$$t_{1/2} = \frac{0.693 \times Vd}{Clearance}$$

  • Reaching Steady State: When continuous drug infusion equals clearance, a stable steady-state plasma concentration is reached after 4 to 5 half-lives.
  • Complete Elimination: A drug is considered completely eliminated from the system after 4 to 5 half-lives (94% to 97% cleared).

6. Clinical Significance & Interprofessional Teamwork

When a healthcare provider prescribes medication, the ultimate goal is achieving a positive therapeutic outcome while minimizing adverse reactions. A thorough understanding of pharmacokinetics is essential for building safe, effective treatment plans.

Nursing, Allied Health, and Interprofessional Roles

  • Physicians, Nurses, and Pharmacists: Educate patients on correct self-administration, storage, and adherence to minimize toxicity.
  • Pharmacists: Verify dosing calculations, perform drug-interaction checks, and track plasma concentrations for narrow therapeutic index drugs (e.g., gentamicin).
  • Nurses: Monitor adverse events, assess treatment effectiveness on follow-up visits, verify medication adherence, and maintain open communication with the interprofessional care team.
