Introduction To Pharmacodynamics And Pharmacokinetics

A streamlined, beautifully formatted breakdown of how psychotropic medications interact with cellular receptors, alter genetic transcription, and undergo complex metabolic pathways in the human body.

Core Definitions & Paradigm Shift

In essence, pharmacodynamics is what a drug does to your body; pharmacokinetics is what your body does to a drug.

Understanding how psychotropic medications work is far more complex than once believed. In the past, antidepressants and antipsychotics were described solely by their superficial effects on specific neuronal receptors or biogenic amines. Present evidence shows that fundamental mechanisms operate at the level of the gene—specific gene products, rather than surface receptor changes, represent the true "business end" of a medication's clinical efficacy.

1. Pharmacodynamics (PD): Mechanism of Action

Pharmacodynamics governs a drug's mechanism of action (MOA): how it triggers biochemical changes in the brain or peripheral systems to produce clinical effects or side effects (such as dry mouth, orthostatic hypotension, or daytime somnolence).

Ligands and Receptors

Any molecule that binds to a cellular receptor is called a ligand:

  • Agonist: Enhances intrinsic activity by mimicking or facilitating an endogenous ligand (e.g., Benzodiazepines act at benzodiazepine receptors, facilitating GABA action and chloride ion flow).
  • Antagonist (Neutral): Possesses zero intrinsic activity but blocks agonists, restoring resting receptor status or preventing activation (e.g., Flumazenil reversing benzodiazepine overdose).
  • Inverse Agonist: Produces the opposite effect of an agonist, counteracting receptor activity (e.g., beta-carbolines reducing chloride flow and provoking anxiety).
  • Partial Agonist: Acts as an antagonist when endogenous ligands are in excess, and as an agonist when endogenous ligands are scarce (e.g., Buspirone at 5-HT1A receptors).

Autoreceptors vs. Heteroreceptors: Autoreceptors reside on neurons that produce their matching endogenous ligand (e.g., serotonergic autoreceptor on a serotonin-producing neuron). Heteroreceptors reside on neurons producing a different neurotransmitter (e.g., serotonin heteroreceptor on a dopaminergic neuron), enabling cross-transmitter regulation (crucial for drugs like mirtazapine).

Neurotransmitters & Signal Transduction

Neurotransmitters comprise amino acids, biogenic amines (norepinephrine, serotonin, dopamine, acetylcholine), and peptides. They communicate through two main receptor architectures:

  • Ionotropic Receptors (Ligand-Gated Channels): Rapidly swinging doors governing instant ion flux. Inhibitory amino acids (GABA, glycine) allow $Cl^-$ influx to hyperpolarize cells. Excitatory amino acids (glutamate, aspartate) allow $Na^+$ or $Ca^{2+}$ influx to depolarize cells and trigger firing.
  • G-Protein–Coupled Receptors (GPCRs): Slower relay stations utilizing secondary messengers like cAMP and protein kinases. These trigger phosphorylation, altering ion channel function and long-term genetic transcription (via tertiary messengers like c-fos and mRNA, leading to growth factors like BDNF).

2. Pharmacokinetics (PK): ADME Principles

Pharmacokinetics encompasses all bodily processes governing drug movement: Absorption, Distribution, Metabolism, and Elimination (ADME).

Absorption & Presystemic Elimination

Drug absorption is predominantly a passive process occurring in the small intestine. Key factors influencing systemic entry include minimal effective concentration (MEC), slow-release formulations, food intake (which generally slows absorption), and gastric acidity.

Before reaching general circulation, oral drugs undergo presystemic elimination via three major barriers:

  • Intestinal Epithelium CYP Enzymes: Localized oxidative metabolism.
  • P-glycoprotein (P-gp): An efflux transmembrane pump on intestinal cells that extrudes drugs back into the gut lumen.
  • First-Pass Hepatic Extraction: Portal circulation delivers absorbed drug directly to the liver, where hepatic enzymes metabolize a significant fraction before systemic entry.

Distribution & Protein Binding

Distribution speed into the central nervous system (CNS) depends heavily on lipophilicity (fat solubility). Highly lipophilic drugs (like diazepam) enter the CNS rapidly but also redistribute quickly into peripheral adipose tissue (thighs, buttocks), governing early onset and offset.

Only free (unbound) drug can cross membranes and act at target receptors. However, protein binding shifts have minimal clinical impact because the body is an open system that continuously clears unbound drug. Steady-state free drug concentration is determined strictly by dose/frequency and clearance.

Elimination & Steady State

Elimination half-life ($t_{1/2}\beta$) is the time required for plasma concentration to drop by 50%, determined by clearance (CL) and volume of distribution ($V_d$). Steady state (SS)—where elimination equals administration rate—is achieved in 4 to 5 half-lives.

3. Drug Metabolism: Cytochrome P450 & Glucuronidation

Metabolism involves two primary phases:

  • Phase I Reactions: Oxidative, reductive, and hydrolytic processes (largely hepatic via the Cytochrome P450 (CYP) heme-containing enzyme system) yielding polar, water-soluble molecules.
  • Phase II Reactions (Conjugation): Glucuronidation occurring in the liver, kidneys, intestine, lungs, and brain. Glucuronidation is far less affected by aging than Phase I CYP systems, making drugs eliminated solely via glucuronidation (e.g., lorazepam, oxazepam) safer in elderly patients.

Major CYP Families

  • CYP 3A4/5: The largest component (~50% of oxidative metabolism). Metabolizes tertiary tricyclics, triazolo-benzodiazepines, sertraline, quetiapine, and risperidone. Inhibited by grapefruit juice.
  • CYP 2D6: Accounts for ~30% of oxidative metabolism. Responsible for hydroxylating all tricyclic antidepressants (TCAs) along with several SSRIs and antipsychotics.
  • CYP 1A2: Metabolizes TCAs, mirtazapine, clozapine, olanzapine, and caffeine.
  • CYP 2C (2C9/2C19): Metabolizes citalopram, diazepam, barbiturates, and phenytoin.

4. Clinical Considerations & Drug–Drug Interactions

Clinicians must remain vigilant regarding pharmacokinetic drug-drug interactions, particularly in high-risk scenarios:

  • Elderly or medically ill patients with reduced hepatic/renal function.
  • Patients taking agents near toxic therapeutic ranges.
  • Co-prescription of two or more inhibitors of the same CYP pathway (risking exaggerated side effects).
  • Stopping a CYP inhibitor (leads to drop in substrate drug levels and potential loss of efficacy).
  • Stopping a CYP inducer (leads to rising substrate drug levels and new-onset toxicity).

💡 Clinical Rule of Thumb

Induction takes time (requires protein synthesis over days/weeks). Inhibition is rapid (often occurring within days of adding an inhibitor drug).
