BS Nursing Unit I & Advanced Pharmacology

UNIT 1: INTRODUCTION TO PHARMACOLOGY

Comprehensive coverage of Unit I core nursing pharmacology foundations, advanced pharmacokinetics, intrinsic activity, agonist types, therapeutic index, and clinical practice standards.

UNIT 1: INTRODUCTION TO PHARMACOLOGY

Welcome to foundational nursing pharmacology. This module covers core definitions, historical milestones, medication purposes, classifications, supply systems, legal standards, and practical toolkits for safe nursing administration.

1. Discuss the terminologies related to pharmacology

  • Pharmacology: The comprehensive science of drugs, covering their origin, chemical composition, pharmacokinetics, pharmacodynamics, therapeutic uses, and toxicology.
  • Drug: Any chemical substance that alters biological function through molecular interactions when introduced into a living organism.
  • Medication: A substance administered for the diagnosis, cure, mitigation, treatment, or prevention of disease.
  • Generic Name: The official, non-proprietary international name of a drug assigned by the USAN (e.g., paracetamol).
  • Trade / Brand Name: The proprietary trademarked name given by the pharmaceutical manufacturer (e.g., Tylenol, Panadol).
  • Adverse Drug Reaction (ADR) / Side Effect: An unintended, noxious, or hazardous response occurring at normal therapeutic doses.

2. Discuss the history of pharmacology briefly

Pharmacology transitioned from ancient folklore and herbal remedies into an empirical molecular science:

  • Ancient Era: Reliance on plants, minerals, and animal products documented in historical texts like the Ebers Papyrus (Egypt) and ancient Ayurvedic treatises.
  • 19th Century Emergence: The isolation of active chemical compounds from crude drugs (e.g., isolation of morphine from opium by Friedrich Sertürner in 1805, digitalis from foxglove).
  • Modern Era: Breakthroughs in synthetic chemistry, molecular biology, genomics, and monoclonal antibodies, establishing rigorous clinical trials and regulatory frameworks.

3. Identify the purposes of medication

Medications serve several core healthcare functions:

  • Diagnostic: Agents used to identify diseases (e.g., radiopaque contrast media for CT scans, barium sulfate).
  • Curative: Agents that eliminate pathogens or cure disease states (e.g., antibiotics curing bacterial pneumonia).
  • Palliative: Agents that relieve symptoms and improve quality of life without curing the underlying disease (e.g., analgesics in terminal cancer care).
  • Prophylactic (Preventive): Agents given to prevent illnesses before they occur (e.g., vaccines, prophylactic antibiotics prior to surgical incision).
  • Replacement: Agents that supplement missing physiological body chemicals (e.g., insulin for type 1 diabetes mellitus, levothyroxine for hypothyroidism).

4. Identify the source of medication

Source Category Description & Clinical Examples
Plant Sources Derived from roots, leaves, barks, or seeds (e.g., digoxin from foxglove, morphine from opium poppy, atropine).
Animal Sources Extracted from animal tissues or organs (e.g., animal insulin, heparin extracted from porcine intestinal mucosa).
Mineral Sources Inorganic elements and minerals from the earth (e.g., magnesium sulfate, ferrous sulfate, potassium chloride).
Synthetic & Biosynthetic Created in laboratories via organic chemistry or recombinant DNA technology (e.g., synthetic human insulin, sulfonamides, aspirin).

5. Discuss the classification of drugs

Drugs are categorized based on multiple standardized systems:

  • Therapeutic Classification: Categorized by what clinical condition they treat (e.g., antihypertensives, antidepressants, antihyperglycemics).
  • Pharmacological Classification: Categorized by how they act at the molecular or systemic level (e.g., beta-blockers, ACE inhibitors, calcium channel blockers).
  • Body System Classification: Grouped according to the physiological system they affect (e.g., cardiovascular drugs, respiratory agents, central nervous system drugs).

6. Describe the three types of drug supply system

Hospitals and healthcare facilities utilize structured systems to store, manage, and distribute medications safely:

  • Floor Stock System: Bulk supplies of common medications are kept on nursing units. Nurses dispense directly from floor stock; highly vulnerable to medication errors and inventory discrepancies.
  • Individual Prescription Order System: Medications are dispensed from the pharmacy specifically for an individual patient in a designated supply drawer for a 24-to-72-hour period.
  • Unit-Dose System: The gold standard safety system. Medications are dispensed by the pharmacy in single-unit, pre-packaged, pre-labeled doses ready to administer directly to the specific patient, reducing preparation errors.

7. Discuss the drugs standards and legislation

National and international laws protect public health by establishing strict standards for drug purity, strength, quality, safety, and efficacy:

  • Pharmacopeias & Formularies: Official public standards books (e.g., United States Pharmacopeia - USP, British Pharmacopoeia) defining medication composition and purity tests.
  • Regulatory Agencies: Government bodies (e.g., FDA in the United States, DRAP in Pakistan) responsible for approving new drugs, monitoring adverse events, and enforcing manufacturing compliance.
  • Controlled Substance Acts: Legislation regulating narcotics and habit-forming drugs, requiring secure double-locked storage, exact inventory counts, and witnessed waste disposal.

8. Identify resource to collect and utilize drug information

Nurses must consult verified, evidence-based literature before administering medications:

  • Standard Nursing Drug Handbooks: (e.g., Nursing Central, Mosby's Drug Guide).
  • Pharmacist Consultations & Hospital Formularies: Institutional references outlining approved drug lists, dosages, and IV compatibility.
  • Electronic Databases & Apps: UpToDate, Lexicomp, Micromedex, and official manufacturer prescribing information sheets.

9. Learn to prepare drugs cards

Drug cards are essential clinical learning tools used by nursing students to master pharmacology safely. A standard clinical drug card includes:

  • Generic & Brand Name: Proper spelling and pronunciation.
  • Pharmacological Classification & Mechanism of Action: Drug category and how it works.
  • Indication: Approved clinical uses and reasons the patient is taking it.
  • Dosage, Route & Safe Parameters: Standard clinical doses, routes, and baseline vital sign cutoffs (e.g., hold if heart rate under 60 bpm for digoxin).
  • Adverse Effects & Nursing Interventions: Common side effects, toxicities, lab monitoring, and patient education.

PHARMACOKINETICS (What the Body Does to the Drug)

Pharmacokinetics is the quantitative study of how a drug moves through the body over time. It encompasses four core stages remembered by the acronym ADME, along with liberation kinetics and comprehensive factors affecting each phase.

Absorption & Factors Affecting Absorption

Liberation is the release of a drug from its pharmaceutical dosage form. Absorption is the net movement of active drug molecules from the site of administration across biological membranes into systemic circulation.

Factors Affecting Drug Absorption:

  • Route of Administration: Intravenous (IV) bypasses absorption entirely (100% bioavailability). Oral (PO) drugs must traverse gastrointestinal mucosal barriers.
  • Surface Area & Blood Flow: Larger absorbing surfaces (e.g., small intestinal microvilli) and high regional blood perfusion accelerate absorption rates.
  • Lipid Solubility & Ionization (pH): Un-ionized, lipophilic drugs diffuse across cell membranes easily. Gastric pH affects weak acids/bases ionization states.
  • First-Pass Metabolism: High hepatic clearance of orally administered drugs significantly reduces systemic bioavailability before reaching general circulation.
  • Food and Drug Interactions: Presence of food, gastric emptying rate, and concurrent administration of binding agents (e.g., antacids) can alter drug absorption.

Distribution & Factors Affecting Distribution

Distribution refers to the reversible transfer of drug from systemic circulation into interstitial and intracellular tissue fluids.

The Volume of Distribution (Vd) is a theoretical pharmacokinetic parameter relating the total amount of drug in the body to the plasma drug concentration.

Factors Affecting Drug Distribution:

  • Plasma Protein Binding: Drugs bound to plasma proteins (like albumin) remain intravascular and pharmacologically inactive. Only the free, unbound fraction distributes into tissues.
  • Tissue Blood Flow: Well-perfused organs (brain, liver, kidneys) receive drugs much faster than poorly perfused tissues (adipose tissue, bone, resting skeletal muscle).
  • Lipophilicity & Molecular Size: Highly lipid-soluble drugs easily cross endothelial barriers, including the blood-brain barrier (BBB) and placental barrier.
  • Capillary Permeability: Specialized capillary structures (such as sinusoidal gaps in the liver or tight junctions in the brain) dictate regional tissue access.

💡 Protein Binding & Albumin Clinical Significance

Administering two highly protein-bound drugs simultaneously can cause competitive displacement from albumin binding sites, dangerously spiking free drug concentrations and triggering acute toxicity.

Metabolism (Biotransformation) & Factors Affecting Metabolism

Metabolism is the biochemical conversion of lipophilic parent drug molecules into more hydrophilic, water-soluble metabolites ready for renal excretion.

  • Phase I Reactions (Non-synthetic): Oxidation, reduction, or hydrolysis catalyzed predominantly by the hepatic Cytochrome P450 (CYP450) enzyme superfamily (e.g., CYP3A4, CYP2D6).
  • Phase II Reactions (Synthetic): Conjugation reactions (glucuronidation, sulfation, acetylation) attaching polar endogenous molecules to create inactive, easily excreted compounds.

Factors Affecting Drug Metabolism:

  • Enzyme Induction & Inhibition: Certain drugs induce CYP450 enzymes (accelerating clearance of co-administered drugs), while others inhibit them (causing drug accumulation and toxicity).
  • Age & Genetics: Neonates and elderly patients have reduced metabolic enzyme capacity. Genetic polymorphisms (e.g., slow vs. extensive CYP metabolizers) alter individual drug response.
  • Liver Health: Hepatic diseases (cirrhosis, hepatitis) compromise enzymatic activity, markedly prolonging drug half-life and elevating systemic toxicity risks.

Excretion & Factors Affecting Elimination

Excretion is the permanent removal of drugs and their metabolites from the body, primarily via the kidneys (urine) and hepatobiliary system (bile/feces).

Factors Affecting Renal Elimination:

  • Glomerular Filtration Rate (GFR): Renal function status directly impacts clearance. Conditions reducing GFR (e.g., acute kidney injury, chronic renal failure) cause drug accumulation.
  • Urinary pH & Ionization: Altering urine pH ionizes weak drugs, preventing tubular reabsorption and speeding up renal excretion (e.g., sodium bicarbonate to alkalinize urine for weak acid toxicity).
  • Competition for Tubular Secretion: Active transport systems in renal tubules can be saturated or competitively blocked by concurrent drug therapies (e.g., probenecid and penicillin).

PHARMACODYNAMICS (What the Drug Does to the Body)

Pharmacodynamics examines the biochemical and physiological effects of drugs and their mechanisms of action at the cellular and molecular level.

Intrinsic Activity & Agonist Types (Reversible & Irreversible)

Intrinsic Activity (Efficacy): The ability of a drug bound to a receptor to generate a physiological stimulus and trigger a cellular response. Denoted numerically from 0 to 1.

Classification of Agonists & Antagonists:

  • Full Agonist: Possesses maximal intrinsic activity (efficacy = 1.0). Fully activates receptors to produce the maximum biological response (e.g., morphine at mu-opioid receptors).
  • Partial Agonist: Possesses submaximal intrinsic activity (efficacy between 0 and 1). Produces a submaximal response even when all receptors are occupied, and can act as a competitive antagonist in the presence of a full agonist.
  • Inverse Agonist: Binds to constitutively active receptors and suppresses baseline basal activity below zero.
  • Competitive (Reversible) Antagonist: Binds reversibly to the same receptor site as the agonist. Can be overcome by increasing the concentration of the agonist (shifts dose-response curve to the right).
  • Non-Competitive (Irreversible) Antagonist: Binds covalently or with extremely high affinity to the receptor site or an allosteric site, permanently disabling receptor function. Increasing agonist concentration cannot overcome the blockade (suppresses maximum response).
  • Functional (Physiological) Antagonist: Two drugs acting through completely different receptors on different physiological systems produce opposing effects (e.g., histamine causing bronchospasm via H1 receptors vs. epinephrine causing bronchodilation via beta-2 adrenergic receptors).

Receptor Superfamilies & Signal Transduction

Cellular receptors mediate drug actions across four primary molecular superfamilies:

  • Type I: Ligand-Gated Ion Channels (Ionotropic): Millisecond response time (e.g., nicotinic acetylcholine receptors).
  • Type II: G Protein-Coupled Receptors (GPCRs): Seconds response time via intracellular second messengers like cAMP or IP3 (e.g., beta-adrenergic receptors).
  • Type III: Enzyme-Linked Receptors: Minutes to hours response time (e.g., insulin receptors with intrinsic tyrosine kinase activity).
  • Type IV: Intracellular (Nuclear) Receptors: Hours to days response time regulating gene transcription (e.g., corticosteroid and thyroid hormone receptors).

Therapeutic Index (TI) & Drug Response Curve (DRC)

Evaluating drug safety and potency requires quantitative dose-response analysis and safety margins.

1. Drug Response Curve (DRC) & Quantal Dose-Response

The Dose-Response Curve plots the magnitude of a drug's pharmacological effect against its concentration or dose.

  • Potency: The amount of drug needed to produce a given biological effect (measured by $\text{ED}_{50}$ - effective dose for 50% of population). Higher potency means a lower dose is required.
  • Efficacy: The maximum intensity of response produced by a drug regardless of dose ($\text{E}_{\max}$).

2. Therapeutic Index (TI) & Safety Margin

The Therapeutic Index is a quantitative measurement of relative drug safety, calculated as the ratio of the toxic dose to the therapeutic dose:

$$\text{TI} = \frac{\text{TD}_{50}}{\text{ED}_{50}}$$

Where $\text{TD}_{50}$ is the dose causing toxic effects in 50% of subjects, and $\text{ED}_{50}$ is the effective dose in 50% of subjects.

📊 Clinical Significance of Therapeutic Index

Narrow Therapeutic Index (NTI) drugs (e.g., digoxin, warfarin, lithium, gentamicin) have a very small margin of safety. Small variances in dosage or plasma concentration can shift rapidly from effective therapy to severe toxicity or lethality, requiring rigorous therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM).

Clinical Significance for Nursing Practice

Understanding pharmacokinetics, pharmacodynamics, intrinsic activity, and drug safety metrics empowers nursing professionals to:

  • Anticipate drug onset, peak action, and duration based on patient physiological status and organ function.
  • Perform baseline assessments and monitor vital signs to catch adverse drug reactions early.
  • Manage combination therapies safely, mitigating protein-binding displacements and CYP-mediated drug interactions.
  • Execute safe medication administration adhering to the Rights of Medication Administration.

Distribution refers to the reversible transfer of drug from systemic circulation into interstitial and intracellular tissue fluids.

The Volume of Distribution (Vd) is a theoretical pharmacokinetic parameter relating the total amount of drug in the body to the plasma drug concentration.

Factors Affecting Drug Distribution:

  • Plasma Protein Binding: Drugs bound to plasma proteins (like albumin) remain intravascular and pharmacologically inactive. Only the free, unbound fraction distributes into tissues.
  • Tissue Blood Flow: Well-perfused organs (brain, liver, kidneys) receive drugs much faster than poorly perfused tissues (adipose tissue, bone, resting skeletal muscle).
  • Capillary Permeability: Endothelial slit junctions in most capillaries allow easy drug egress. In contrast, tight junctions in the brain form the Blood-Brain Barrier (BBB), restricting polar or ionized drugs.
  • Lipid Solubility: Highly lipophilic drugs easily cross lipid cell membranes and accumulate in adipose tissue.

Metabolism & Factors Affecting Metabolism

Metabolism (Biotransformation) is the chemical alteration of drug molecules by the body, predominantly occurring in the liver, to facilitate excretion and terminate biological activity.

  • Phase I Reactions (Non-synthetic): Oxidation, reduction, or hydrolysis mediated primarily by the Cytochrome P450 (CYP450) enzyme system, converting lipophilic drugs into more polar metabolites.
  • Phase II Reactions (Synthetic / Conjugation): Coupling of the drug or Phase I metabolite with an endogenous polar substance (e.g., glucuronic acid, sulfate, glutathione) to form highly water-soluble inactive compounds.

Factors Affecting Metabolism:

  • Enzyme Induction & Inhibition: Inducers (e.g., rifampin, carbamazepine) accelerate metabolism of co-administered drugs, decreasing efficacy. Inhibitors (e.g., ketoconazole, grapefruit juice) decrease metabolism, predisposing to drug toxicity.
  • Age & Genetics: Neonates have immature metabolic enzymes, while elderly individuals exhibit reduced hepatic blood flow and enzymatic reserve. Genetic polymorphisms in CYP enzymes alter metabolic rates (poor vs. extensive metabolizers).
  • Liver Disease: Cirrhosis or hepatitis significantly impairs hepatic enzyme synthesis and clearance capacity.

Excretion & Factors Affecting Excretion

Excretion is the permanent elimination of drugs and their metabolites from the body. The primary organs of excretion are the kidneys (renal excretion) and the biliary system (fecal excretion).

  • Renal Excretion Processes: Includes glomerular filtration, active tubular secretion, and passive tubular reabsorption. Lipophilic drugs are reabsorbed into blood unless metabolized into hydrophilic polar compounds.
  • Biliary & Enterohepatic Circulation: Drugs excreted into bile enter the duodenum; some conjugates are hydrolyzed by gut flora and reabsorbed back into circulation.

Factors Affecting Excretion:

  • Renal Function (GFR): Creatinine clearance and glomerular filtration rate directly dictate elimination half-life and clearance of renally excreted drugs.
  • Urine pH: Altering urine pH can ionize weak acids or bases, trapping them in renal tubules and enhancing elimination (ion trapping).

PHARMACODYNAMICS (What the Drug Does to the Body)

Pharmacodynamics examines the biochemical and physiological effects of drugs on living organisms and their mechanisms of action at the cellular and molecular level.

Intrinsic Activity & Agonist Types

Intrinsic Activity refers to the ability of a drug-receptor complex to initiate a maximal pharmacological response once bound to a receptor.

  • Full Agonists: Possess high affinity and maximal intrinsic activity (efficacy = 1.0), reproducing the full physiological response of endogenous ligands (e.g., morphine at mu-opioid receptors).
  • Partial Agonists: Bind to receptors but possess sub-maximal intrinsic activity (efficacy between 0 and 1.0). They can act as agonists in the absence of full agonists, or as competitive antagonists in their presence.
  • Competitive Antagonists: Bind reversibly to the same receptor site as agonists without producing activation (intrinsic activity = 0), shifting the agonist dose-response curve to the right.
  • Non-Competitive Antagonists: Bind irreversibly or to an allosteric site, depressing the maximal attainable response regardless of agonist concentration.
  • Inverse Agonists: Bind to receptors to stabilize them in an inactive conformation, reducing constitutive baseline receptor activity below zero.

Receptor Superfamilies

Receptors are specialized macromolecular proteins located on cell membranes or intracellularly. They are divided into four primary superfamilies:

  • Type 1: Ligand-Gated Ion Channels (Ionotropic): Millisecond response times. Binding opens or closes ion channels (e.g., Nicotinic acetylcholine receptors, GABA-A receptors).
  • Type 2: G-Protein Coupled Receptors (GPCR / Metabotropic): Seconds response times. Seven-transmembrane spanning proteins coupled to intracellular G-proteins activating second messengers like cAMP or IP3 (e.g., Beta-adrenoceptors, muscarinic receptors).
  • Type 3: Enzyme-Linked Receptors: Hours response times. Extracellular binding activates intrinsic intracellular enzymatic activity, typically tyrosine kinase (e.g., Insulin receptors, growth factor receptors).
  • Type 4: Intracellular / Nuclear Receptors: Hours to days response times. Lipophilic drugs cross the cell membrane to bind intracellular receptors, regulating gene transcription and protein synthesis (e.g., Corticosteroid receptors, thyroid hormone receptors).

Therapeutic Index & Dose-Response Curve

Therapeutic Index (TI) is a quantitative measurement of relative drug safety, calculated as the ratio of the toxic dose to the therapeutic dose:

Therapeutic Index (TI) = TD50 / ED50

Where TD50 is the dose causing toxicity in 50% of subjects, and ED50 is the median effective dose in 50% of subjects. A narrow therapeutic index (e.g., warfarin, digoxin, lithium) requires routine therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM) to prevent severe toxicity.

Dose-Response Curves (DRC) graphically illustrate the relationship between drug concentration and the magnitude of the pharmacological effect. Graded dose-response curves demonstrate potency and efficacy, while quantal dose-response curves evaluate population frequency distributions of therapeutic and toxic thresholds.

Clinical Significance & Nursing Implications

Applying pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic principles at the bedside ensures patient safety and optimizes therapeutic outcomes:

  • Individualized Dosing: Accounting for patient age, renal function, hepatic impairment, and weight to calculate safe therapeutic dosages.
  • Therapeutic Drug Monitoring (TDM): Drawing peak and trough serum levels for drugs with narrow therapeutic indices (e.g., vancomycin, aminoglycosides, digoxin).
  • Monitoring for Adverse Reactions: Remaining vigilant for exaggerated pharmacodynamic responses or toxicities during drug administration.
  • Patient Education: Instructing patients on correct administration routes, recognizing side effects, and reinforcing adherence to prescribed regimens.
