Core Definitions & The Two Main Branches

Pharmacology splits into two main halves so you can separately ask two questions:

  • Pharmacodynamics (PD): What the drug does to the body — receptors, responses and clinical effects.
  • Pharmacokinetics (PK): What the body does to the drug — ADME: absorption, distribution, metabolism, elimination.

💡 Everyday Life Example

Taking ibuprofen for a headache: PD = how it blocks pain pathways; PK = how it dissolves, reaches blood, and is cleared by liver/kidney.

Quick Summary: Why this matters

When you understand both PD and PK, you can choose the right dose, frequency, and route for each patient — and predict when interactions or toxicity might occur.

1. Pharmacodynamics (PD): Receptors and Drug Action

Receptors are the 'locks'. Drugs are 'keys'. The interaction determines intensity, onset and adverse effects.

Ligand Types & Clinical Examples

  • Full agonist: Produces maximal response — e.g., morphine at μ-opioid receptors.
  • Partial agonist: Moderate response, can block stronger agonists — e.g., buprenorphine.
  • Neutral antagonist: Blocks receptor without own activity — e.g., naloxone reverses opioids.
  • Inverse agonist: Reduces constitutive receptor activity — clinically used in specific contexts.

Important PD concepts

  • Potency vs. Efficacy: Potency = how much drug to produce an effect; efficacy = maximum effect achievable.
  • Therapeutic Index (TI): Ratio of toxic dose to effective dose. Narrow TI → require monitoring (e.g., digoxin).
  • Tolerance & tachyphylaxis: Repeated use reduces effect (up-regulation or down-regulation of receptors).

Mnemonics

“PET” — Potency (how much), Efficacy (how big), Time (onset/duration).

2. Pharmacokinetics: The ADME Journey

ADME controls how quickly and how much drug reaches the target site.

ADME — bite-sized

  • Absorption: Route, formulation, gut motility and food alter absorption speed and extent.
  • Distribution: Perfusion, tissue-binding, and plasma protein binding determine where the drug goes.
  • Metabolism: Mostly hepatic; Phase I (functionalization), Phase II (conjugation) → produce water-soluble metabolites.
  • Elimination: Renal (filtration, secretion, reabsorption), biliary excretion, or exhalation.

Useful PK formulas (clinical)

ConceptFormula / RuleClinical meaning
Half-life (t½)t½ = 0.693 / keTime for plasma concentration to fall by 50%. Guides dosing interval.
Clearance (CL)CL = ke × VdVolume of plasma cleared of drug per unit time. Used to adjust dose in renal/liver disease.
Loading doseLD = (Css × Vd) / FUsed to rapidly achieve a target concentration (Css = target steady-state concentration).
Time to steady state~4–5 × t½After repeated dosing, steady peaks/troughs are reached after several half-lives.

Factors That Alter ADME

Patient-specific and drug-specific factors change ADME and therefore dosing decisions.

Absorption

  • Gastric emptying, pH, formulation, surface area and interacting medications (e.g., antacids)
  • Transdermal and inhaled routes bypass first-pass hepatic metabolism

Distribution

  • Plasma protein binding: only free drug is active — low albumin increases free fraction
  • Vd (volume of distribution) informs whether drug remains intravascular or distributes widely

Metabolism & Elimination

  • Liver enzyme activity (CYPs), renal GFR, age, genetics, and drug-diet interactions (e.g., grapefruit)
  • Organ failure often requires dose reduction and monitoring

Protein Binding Example

Same dose of a highly protein-bound drug can produce higher free levels in a hypoalbuminemic patient → risk of toxicity.

Deep Dive: Bioavailability (F)

Bioavailability is the fraction of dose that reaches systemic circulation unchanged. It's determined by formulation and first-pass metabolism.

RouteF (typical)Notes
IV100%Immediate, predictable
OralVariable (e.g., 20–90%)Affected by gut, liver first-pass
IM / SCHigh to moderateSlower absorption than IV
TransdermalOften lower but steadyBypasses GI/hepatic first-pass

Clinical tip: When switching route (e.g., IV → oral), calculate required oral dose using F to keep equivalent systemic exposure.

First-Order vs. Zero-Order Kinetics

Understanding these patterns helps predict accumulation and overdose risk.

  • First-order: Constant proportion removed per time (e.g., most drugs). Graphically exponential decline.
  • Zero-order: Constant amount removed per time (e.g., ethanol, phenytoin at high doses). Risk of rapid toxicity with increased dose.

Clinical rule of thumb

Steady state is usually reached after 4–5 half-lives. If t½ is 8 hours, steady state ≈ 32–40 hours.

3. Biotransformation & the CYP450 System

The hepatic CYP family (CYP3A4, CYP2D6, CYP2C9, etc.) metabolizes many drugs. Inhibition or induction drastically changes exposure.

Interactive: tap to read each hepatic processing step.

Interactive Liver Steps

Phase I: Functionalization🔍
Oxidation/reduction/hydrolysis (often CYP enzymes) add or expose functional groups to increase polarity.
Phase II: Conjugation🔗
Transferases add glucuronide, sulfate or glutathione groups to dramatically increase water solubility.
Phase III: Excretion🚽
Transporters move metabolites into bile or urine for elimination.

Grapefruit juice & CYP3A4

Grapefruit blocks intestinal CYP3A4 → increases bioavailability of drugs metabolized by that enzyme (e.g., some statins) → risk of toxicity.

4. Drug Interactions & Patient Safety

Enzyme inhibition raises levels of affected drugs; enzyme induction lowers them. Always review organ function and diet.

Practical safety checklist

  • Check renal function (eGFR) and liver tests before starting narrow TI drugs.
  • Review patient's herbal supplements (St John's wort, grapefruit, etc.).
  • Consider therapeutic drug monitoring for digoxin, lithium, aminoglycosides, vancomycin, and some antiepileptics.

High-Yield Rule

Before prescribing: check GFR, LFTs, and dietary interactions — especially when the drug has a narrow therapeutic window.
