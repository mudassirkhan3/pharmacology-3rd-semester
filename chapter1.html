1. Unit I: Complete Introduction to Pharmacology Syllabus Topics (1 to 9)

1. Terminologies in Pharmacology

Essential clinical definitions: Pharmacology is the study of drugs and their interactions with living systems. Pharmacokinetics is what the body does to the drug. Pharmacodynamics is what the drug does to the body. Bioavailability is the fraction of active drug entering systemic circulation. Half-life is the time required for plasma drug concentration to decrease by half. Therapeutic Index measures drug safety margin, and Steady State occurs when drug intake equals clearance rate.

2. Brief History of Pharmacology

Pharmacology evolved from early herbal folklore and ancient plant remedies (such as opium poppy, foxglove, and cinchona bark) into a precise biomedical science. The 19th-century birth of modern chemistry allowed active pharmaceutical compounds to be isolated (e.g., morphine extraction in 1805), leading to modern synthetic drug discovery and biotechnology.

Historical evolution from crude herbal remedies to modern pharmaceutical sciences.

3. Purposes of Medication

Medications are administered in clinical practice for four primary objectives:

• Diagnostic: Used with imaging or clinical tests to detect disorders (e.g., radiopaque contrast dyes).

• Prophylactic: Administered to prevent disease before onset (e.g., pre-surgical antibiotics, immunizations).

• Therapeutic: To cure, manage, or reduce symptoms of active illness (e.g., antihypertensives, insulin).

• Replacement: To substitute missing natural body chemicals (e.g., levothyroxine for hypothyroidism).

4. Sources of Medication

Pharmaceutical agents are sourced from four major origins:

1. Plant Sources: Alkaloids and glycosides extracted from plants (e.g., Morphine, Digoxin).

2. Animal Sources: Biological extracts from animal tissues (e.g., Heparin).

3. Mineral Sources: Inorganic elements and mineral compounds (e.g., Ferrous sulfate, magnesium sulfate).

4. Synthetic & Recombinant Sources: Laboratory-synthesized chemical compounds and recombinant DNA technology (e.g., human insulin).

5. Classification of Drugs

Drugs are categorized by their chemical nature, pharmacological mechanism, or clinical therapeutic use (e.g., antibiotics, antihypertensives, diuretics, analgesics). Classification helps nurses anticipate primary therapeutic effects and monitor specific adverse reactions.

6. Three Types of Drug Supply Systems

Hospitals utilize three primary drug distribution models:

1. Stock Supply System: Bulk quantities of non-controlled medications stored on wards (higher risk of error).

2. Individual Prescription System: Pharmacy dispenses specific patient prescriptions labeled individually.

3. Unit-Dose Distribution System: The gold-standard hospital system featuring pre-packaged, single-dose medications organized in secure patient drawers.

Unit-dose distribution systems enhance patient safety in acute hospital care units.

7. Drug Standards and Legislation

Statutory laws and pharmacopeia standards ensure pharmaceutical products maintain rigorous criteria for purity, strength, safety, and efficacy. Regulatory authorities enforce strict scheduling of controlled substances to prevent drug diversion and protect public health.

8. Resources for Drug Information

Nurses rely on authorized, evidence-based references to verify dosages, indications, and interactions:

• Nursing Drug Handbooks (focused on nursing assessments and patient teaching).

• Physicians' Desk Reference (PDR) / manufacturer prescribing inserts.

• Verified clinical databases such as Lexicomp, UpToDate, and Micromedex.

2. Pharmacokinetics: What the Body Does to the Drug (ADME)

In simple terms, Pharmacokinetics (PK) tracks the journey of a medication from the moment it enters your body until it completely leaves. It is summarized by the acronym ADME: Absorption, Distribution, Metabolism, and Excretion.

💡 Nursing Memory Mnemonic: ADME

Absorption (Entering blood) → Distribution (Traveling to tissues) → Metabolism (Liver processing) → Excretion (Kidney removal).

A. Absorption & Bioavailability

Absorption is how a drug moves from the administration site into systemic circulation. Before oral drugs can absorb, they must undergo liberation. Low stomach acidity and digestive enzymes can break down some drugs before they enter circulation.

Bioavailability: The exact percentage of the administered drug that successfully reaches your bloodstream in an active form. When a medication is given intravenously (IV), 100% of the drug enters the blood instantly, making IV the gold standard for immediate systemic availability.

First-Pass Metabolism: When you swallow an oral pill, it travels through the gut wall and directly to the liver via the portal vein. The liver metabolizes a large portion of the drug *before* it reaches the rest of the body.

B. Distribution & Volume of Distribution

Distribution describes how the drug spreads from the bloodstream into body tissues. This is heavily influenced by Protein Binding. In the blood, drugs often bind to plasma proteins like albumin. Only free (unbound) drug can leave the blood vessels to bind to cell receptors and produce an effect. If a patient has low albumin, more free drug floats around, raising the risk of toxicity!

C. Metabolism & Excretion

Metabolism (Biotransformation): Mostly handled by the liver using CYP450 enzymes to turn active, fat-soluble drugs into water-soluble compounds so the body can eliminate them.

Excretion: The permanent removal of drug waste from the body, primarily performed by the kidneys via urine.

3. Pharmacodynamics: What the Drug Does to the Body

Pharmacodynamics (PD) explores the drug's mechanism of action at the cellular and receptor level. Drugs act as ligands that lock into specific cellular receptors.

A. Ligands & Receptor Binding Types

Receptor Ligand Type Intrinsic Activity Clinical Description & Action
Full Agonist 1.0 (Maximum) Binds the receptor and triggers maximum biological response (e.g., Morphine).
Partial Agonist Intermediate (>0 & <1) Binds the receptor but produces sub-maximal response, even if all receptors are occupied.
Antagonist 0.0 (Zero) Occupies the receptor without triggering activation, blocking agonists from binding (e.g., Flumazenil).
Inverse Agonist Negative (<0) Forces the receptor into an inactive state, suppressing basal resting activity.

B. Potency, Efficacy & Therapeutic Index (TI)

• Potency: The amount of drug required to produce 50% of its maximal effect. A more potent drug requires a smaller dose.

• Efficacy: The absolute maximum therapeutic response a drug can produce.

• Therapeutic Index (TI): A critical safety metric measuring the margin between therapeutic benefit and toxicity (Toxic Dose 50 / Effective Dose 50). Drugs with a narrow therapeutic index require strict laboratory monitoring.
