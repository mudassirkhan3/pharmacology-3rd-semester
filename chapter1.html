Pharmacokinetics & Pharmacodynamics: The Complete Science of Drugs

Based on foundational works by Sean Grogan, Charles V. Preuss, and John Locke. Master how drugs move through the body (PK) and what drugs do to the body at the molecular and physiological level (PD).

Definition & Introduction

"Did we but know the mechanical affections of the particles of rhubarb, hemlock, opium, and a man...we should be able to tell beforehand that rhubarb will purge, hemlock kill, and opium make a man sleepy..." — John Locke, Essay Concerning Human Understanding

Pharmacokinetics (PK) is the study of how the body interacts with administered substances throughout their entire duration of exposure (what the body does to the drug). Conversely, Pharmacodynamics (PD) examines the drug’s molecular, biochemical, and physiological effect on the body (what the drug does to the body).

🎯 Core Distinction

Pharmacodynamics studies drug action on the organism, while pharmacokinetics studies how the organism processes the drug. Both make up the two fundamental branches of pharmacology.

1. Absorption & Liberation

Absorption is the process that brings a drug from its administration point into your systemic blood circulation.

Methods of Drug Administration

  • Oral, intravenous (IV), intramuscular, intrathecal, subcutaneous
  • Buccal, rectal, vaginal, ocular, otic, inhaled, nebulized, and transdermal

💡 The Role of Liberation in Oral Medications

Before absorption, oral drugs undergo liberation (release from their dosage form). If delayed in the esophagus, it can cause mucosal irritation. Stomach acid can also chemically degrade active ingredients prematurely.

Bioavailability & The Area Under the Curve (AUC)

Bioavailability (F) is the fraction of administered drug that reaches systemic circulation intact. Intravenous administration is the gold standard with 100% bioavailability.

Route Bioavailability Mechanism
Intravenous (IV) 100% Bypasses gut and liver filtering instantly.
Oral Tablets Varies (Lower) Subject to stomach acid and hepatic first-pass metabolism.

First-Pass Metabolism & AUC

Orally absorbed drugs pass through gut wall enzymes and the liver via the portal vein before reaching general circulation (first-pass metabolism). The Area Under the Curve (AUC) calculates total systemic bioavailability over a given time frame.

2. Distribution & Volume of Distribution (Vd)

Distribution describes how a drug spreads through body fluids and tissues via diffusion and convection, striving to achieve effective drug concentration at receptor sites.

Volume of Distribution (Vd)

Vd relates the total amount of drug in the body to its plasma concentration:

Vd = Amount of Drug in Body / Plasma Drug Concentration

  • Low Vd: Intravascular confinement (large/protein-bound molecules like cetrorelix).
  • High Vd: Extensive tissue/adipose penetration (small lipophilic molecules like chloroquine).

💡 Clinical Application: Loading Doses

Loading doses rapidly achieve therapeutic plasma levels based directly on Vd:
Loading Dose = Vd × Desired Concentration / Bioavailability.

Protein Binding & The Free Drug Rule

Drugs circulate either protein-bound (to albumin or alpha-1-acid glycoprotein) or as free active drug. Only free drug can bind receptors or be eliminated.

💡 Clinical Example: Renal Failure

In renal failure, uremia decreases serum protein binding for acidic drugs like diazepam. This increases the active free fraction, dramatically heightening the risk of toxicity (e.g., respiratory depression).

3. Metabolism & The Liver

Metabolism converts lipophilic drugs into water-soluble metabolites for renal clearance, or activates prodrugs (e.g., codeine).

  • Phase I (CYP450): Oxidation reactions creating polar intermediates.
  • Phase II (UGT): Conjugation reactions ensuring complete water solubility for excretion.

4. Excretion & Clearance

Excretion eliminates drug waste primarily via the kidneys (glomerular filtration and tubular secretion), or via lungs, skin, and GI tract.

Clearance measures the organ's efficiency in removing drug from blood plasma, guiding maintenance dosing calculations:
Maintenance Dose = Clearance × Desired Concentration / Bioavailability.

5. Drug Kinetics, Half-Life, and Steady State

Drug elimination follows specific kinetic models:

  • First-Order Kinetics: Constant fraction eliminated per unit time (exponential half-life). Used by most drugs.
  • Zero-Order Kinetics: Constant amount eliminated per hour due to enzyme saturation (e.g., alcohol, high-dose phenytoin).

Half-life ($t_{1/2}$) formula: $$t_{1/2} = \frac{0.693 \times Vd}{Clearance}$$

Therapeutic steady state and complete elimination are both achieved after 4 to 5 half-lives.

6. Pharmacodynamics (PD): Molecular Mechanisms & Receptors

Pharmacodynamics (PD) studies a drug's molecular, biochemical, and physiological effects. Drugs interact with biological targets (receptors, enzymes, carrier proteins) via:

  • Receptor Binding & Post-Receptor Effects: Direct molecular engagement altering cellular function.
  • Enzyme Inhibition: Blockade of catalytic active sites.
  • Chemical Interactions: Neutralization or direct binding (e.g., tumor necrosis factor binders).

Types of Pharmacodynamic Actions

  • Stimulation: Directly increasing cellular activity via receptor agonism.
  • Depression: Decreasing cellular activity via receptor inhibition.
  • Antagonism / Blocking: Binding a receptor without activating it, preventing endogenous ligands from binding.
  • Stabilization: Maintaining resting membrane state without direct agonism or antagonism.
  • Direct Chemical Reactions: Neutralization or chelation in bodily fluids.

Key PD Concepts: Emax, EC50, & Hill Coefficient

  • Emax: The maximal possible therapeutic effect of a drug.
  • EC50: The drug concentration producing 50% of the maximum effect.
  • Hill Coefficient: Slope measuring concentration-effect steepness. Values >3 indicate an almost instantaneous "all-or-none" effect.

Direct vs. Indirect & Immediate vs. Delayed Effects

Direct effects stem from immediate receptor/enzyme interaction (e.g., beta-blockers modifying cAMP). Indirect effects occur via upstream or downstream biological signaling cascades (e.g., corticosteroids binding nuclear transcription factors to suppress inflammatory mRNA).

Immediate effects occur within seconds (e.g., succinylcholine depolarizing neuromuscular junctions), whereas delayed effects require biological turnover time (e.g., chemotherapy bone marrow suppression).

Receptor Pharmacology: Affinity, Intrinsic Activity, Agonists, & Therapeutic Index

Receptor interactions define drug potency, efficacy, and safety margins:

Pharmacologic Term Definition & Clinical Meaning
Affinity ($K_d$) How tightly a drug binds to its receptor ($K_d = k_{off}/k_{on}$). A lower $K_d$ value indicates higher binding affinity (e.g., albuterol).
Intrinsic Activity & Efficacy The measure of a drug's ability to activate a receptor and produce a physiological response once bound. Full agonists have high efficacy; antagonists have zero intrinsic activity.
Full Agonist Binds to receptors and produces maximum biological response (Intrinsic activity = 1).
Partial Agonist Binds to receptors but produces a submaximal response even at 100% occupancy (Intrinsic activity between 0 and 1).
Competitive Antagonist Binds reversibly to the active site, shifting the agonist dose-response curve to the right without lowering $E_{max}$.
Non-Competitive Antagonist Binds irreversibly (or to an allosteric site), lowering the total available $E_{max}$.
Potency vs. Efficacy Potency refers to the amount of drug needed for a given effect ($EC_{50}$). Efficacy refers to the maximum effect ($E_{max}$) a drug can produce. Clinically, efficacy is far more important than potency.
Therapeutic Index (TI) Ratio of the toxic dose to the effective dose ($TD_{50} / ED_{50}$). A narrow therapeutic index means small dosing changes can lead to severe toxicity (e.g., warfarin, digoxin).

Receptor Occupancy, Up- & Downregulation, and Tolerance

According to the law of mass action, greater receptor occupancy increases pharmacodynamic response, though many systems feature spare receptors yielding maximal effects at <100% occupancy.

  • Downregulation: Chronic agonist exposure decreases receptor density via internalization (e.g., insulin receptors).
  • Upregulation: Chronic antagonist exposure increases receptor density. An exception is nicotine receptors, which upregulate despite being agonists, driving addiction.
  • Tolerance: Diminishing response over time, often driven by intracellular proteins like arrestins that uncouple G-protein receptors and trigger endocytosis (e.g., opioid tolerance).

7. Clinical Significance & Interprofessional Teamwork

Healthcare practitioners must treat the patient, not just the lab value or symptom. For instance, drugs like aspirin and ACE inhibitors are administered not merely to lower platelets or blood pressure, but to prevent catastrophic long-term endpoints like myocardial infarction, stroke, and renal failure.

Interprofessional Care Team Roles

  • Physicians & Nurse Practitioners: Formulate evidence-based pharmacological treatment plans considering both PK and PD parameters.
  • Pharmacists: Monitor therapeutic indexes, calculate clearance adjustments, and check for narrow therapeutic window toxicities (e.g., gentamicin, digoxin).
  • Nurses: Monitor adverse drug events, assess therapeutic efficacy during clinical follow-ups, verify patient adherence, and maintain open communication with the care team.
