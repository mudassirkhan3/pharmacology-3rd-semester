Section 2.1 • Antibacterials

Cell Wall Inhibitors (Beta-Lactams)

Comprehensive clinical breakdown of core beta-lactam antibiotic classes, mechanisms, clinical applications, and essential nursing considerations.

1. Penicillins

Introduction

Penicillins are among the oldest and most reliable beta-lactam antibacterial drugs, derived from the Penicillium fungi. They contain a core beta-lactam ring responsible for their bactericidal antibacterial activity.

Classification

Sub-Category Examples Spectrum & Clinical Notes
Natural Penicillins Penicillin G, Penicillin V Narrow spectrum; primarily effective against Gram-positive organisms.
Aminopenicillins Amoxicillin, Ampicillin Broadened spectrum; effective against certain Gram-negative bacilli.
Penicillinase-Resistant Nafcillin, Oxacillin, Dicloxacillin Resistant to penicillinase enzyme produced by specific staphylococci.
Extended-Spectrum Piperacillin (often paired with tazobactam) Pseudomonal coverage; used in severe nosocomial infections.

Mechanism of Action

Penicillins target bacterial structural integrity by binding to specialized cellular structures.

1. Drug Administered
→
2. Binds Penicillin-Binding Proteins (PBPs)
→
3. Inhibits Peptidoglycan Cross-Linking
→
4. Osmotic Lysis (Bactericidal)
Mechanism Mnemonic: Penicillin Binds Proteins to Destroy Wall (PBPDW)

Uses

Indicated for respiratory tract infections, streptococcal pharyngitis, syphilis, and endocarditis prophylaxis.

Uses Mnemonic: S S G P → Strep throat, Syphilis, Gonorrhea, Pneumonia.

Adverse Effects

Common complications include hypersensitivity reactions, gastrointestinal distress, and superinfections like Clostridioides difficile or oral candidiasis.

Adverse Effects Mnemonic: H A G S → Hypersensitivity (anaphylaxis), Allergies, GI upset / diarrhea, Superinfections.

Contraindications

Absolute contraindication in patients with a history of severe, immediate-type anaphylactic hypersensitivity to penicillins or cephalosporins.

2. Cephalosporins

Introduction

Cephalosporins are broad-spectrum beta-lactam antibiotics structurally related to penicillins. They are organized into five distinct generations, progressing from narrow Gram-positive coverage to extensive Gram-negative and central nervous system penetration.

Classification

Generation Examples Key Coverage Profile
1st Generation Cefazolin, Cephalexin Excellent Gram-positive coverage; frequent surgical prophylaxis.
2nd Generation Cefaclor, Cefoxitin Enhanced Gram-negative coverage compared to 1st generation.
3rd Generation Ceftriaxone, Cefotaxime Crosses the blood-brain barrier; highly effective against severe Gram-negative bacilli and meningitis.
4th Generation Cefepime Broad spectrum; highly potent against Pseudomonas aeruginosa.
5th Generation Ceftaroline Unique ability to cover Methicillin-Resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA).

Mechanism of Action

Interferes directly with the structural scaffolding of active bacterial replication.

1. Enters Bacterial Matrix
→
2. Attaches to Specific PBPs
→
3. Blocks Cell Wall Synthesis
→
4. Cell Lysis & Bacterial Death
Mechanism Mnemonic: Ceph Blocks Wall (CBW)

Uses

Surgical prophylaxis, urinary tract infections, pelvic inflammatory disease, community-acquired pneumonia, and bacterial meningitis (3rd generation).

Uses Mnemonic: S U P E R → Surgical prophylaxis, UTIs, Pneumonia, Endocarditis, Respiratory infections.

Adverse Effects

Hypersensitivity reactions, bleeding tendencies (due to hypoprothrombinemia in specific agents), disulfiram-like ethanol reactions, and nephrotoxicity when combined with aminoglycosides.

Adverse Effects Mnemonic: B A D S → Bleeding risks, Allergies (cross-reactivity), Disulfiram-like alcohol reaction, Superinfections.

Contraindications

Hypersensitivity to cephalosporins and history of severe immediate anaphylaxis to penicillins (due to potential cross-allergenicity).

3. Carbapenems

Introduction

Carbapenems are synthetic, ultra-broad-spectrum beta-lactam antibiotics featuring exceptional resistance to beta-lactamase (penicillinase) enzymes. They are typically reserved for complicated, multi-drug resistant (MDR) nosocomial infections.

Classification

Agent Co-Formulation / Details Primary Clinical Application
Imipenem Formulated with cilastatin to prevent renal breakdown Severe intra-abdominal, respiratory, and urinary tract infections.
Meropenem Standalone agent; lower seizure risk than imipenem Meningitis, sepsis, and severe polymicrobial infections.
Ertapenem Once-daily dosing; lacks activity against Pseudomonas Community-acquired and complicated skin/pelvic infections.
Doripenem High potency against pseudomonal strains Complicated urinary tract and intra-abdominal infections.

Mechanism of Action

Penetrates bacterial cells rapidly and resists enzymatic breakdown.

1. Rapid Cell Penetration
→
2. Binds PBPs (Resists Beta-Lactamases)
→
3. Halts Peptidoglycan Assembly
→
4. Rapid Bactericidal Lysis
Mechanism Mnemonic: Carbapenems Kill Bugs Resistant (CKBR)

Uses

Severe polymicrobial infections, febrile neutropenia, complicated intra-abdominal infections, and multidrug-resistant hospital-acquired pneumonia.

Uses Mnemonic: S M I T → Sepsis, Meningitis, Intra-abdominal infections, Tough resistant bugs.

Adverse Effects

Gastrointestinal distress, infusion site reactions, hypersensitivity, and a risk of drug-induced seizures (particularly notable with high-dose Imipenem therapy).

Adverse Effects Mnemonic: S G I H → Seizures (imipenem risk), GI distress, Infusion reactions, Hypersensitivity.

Contraindications

Hypersensitivity to carbapenems and caution in patients with a history of central nervous system disorders or seizure histories.
