Pharmacokinetics: ADME Principles

Pharmacokinetics studies what the body does to a drug from the moment it is administered until it is completely cleared. The core framework is known as **ADME**: Absorption, Distribution, Metabolism, and Elimination.

1. Absorption

Definition: Absorption is the movement of a drug from its site of administration into the systemic bloodstream. (Note: Intravenous administration bypasses absorption entirely, achieving 100% bioavailability instantly).

🧬 Mechanisms of Absorption

Passive Diffusion: Most common; drugs move from high to low concentration across lipid cell membranes.
Active Transport: Requires carrier proteins and cellular energy (ATP).
Endocytosis: Cell engulfs large molecules.

📈 Factors Affecting Absorption

• Surface Area: Larger surface areas (like the small intestine) absorb drugs faster than small areas.
• Blood Flow: High blood supply at the site accelerates absorption.
• Lipid Solubility: Lipophilic (fat-soluble) drugs cross membranes easily.
• pH & Ionization: Weak acids are absorbed in the acidic stomach; weak bases in the alkaline intestine.
• First-Pass Effect: Oral drugs pass through the liver via the portal vein first, where enzymes can deactivate a large portion before reaching systemic circulation.

2. Distribution

Definition: Distribution is the reversible transfer of a drug from the bloodstream into the interstitial spaces and body tissues (such as fat, muscle, and brain).

🌐 Key Distribution Concepts

Plasma Protein Binding: Drugs can bind to proteins like albumin in the blood. Only the unbound (free) drug is active and able to reach target receptors.
Blood-Brain Barrier (BBB): Tight capillary endothelial junctions in the brain prevent polar or hydrophilic drugs from entering, requiring drugs to be highly lipophilic to affect the CNS.

📈 Factors Affecting Distribution

• Cardiac Output & Blood Flow: Well-perfused organs (brain, heart, liver) receive drugs much faster than poorly perfused tissues (fat, bone, skin).
• Capillary Permeability: Leaky capillary beds in liver/spleen allow easy drug entry, whereas tight junctions restrict movement.
• Tissue Affinity: Some drugs bind strongly to specific tissues (e.g., tetracyclines bind to bone and teeth).
• Volume of Distribution ($V_d$): Indicates how widely a drug disperses throughout body fluids.

3. Metabolism (Biotransformation)

Definition: Metabolism is the chemical alteration of a drug by the body, primarily taking place in the liver, converting active lipid-soluble drugs into inactive, water-soluble metabolites ready for excretion.

🧪 Phase I & Phase II Reactions

Phase I (Functionalization): Oxidation, reduction, or hydrolysis via the Cytochrome P450 (CYP450) enzyme system, introducing polar groups.
Phase II (Conjugation): Attachment of a large polar molecule (like glucuronic acid) to make the drug completely water-soluble for renal excretion.

📈 Factors Affecting Metabolism

• Enzyme Induction: Certain drugs (e.g., rifampin, carbamazepine) increase CYP450 activity, speeding up the breakdown of other drugs.
• Enzyme Inhibition: Drugs (e.g., ketoconazole, grapefruit juice) block CYP450 activity, causing toxic drug accumulation.
• Genetics: Genetic variations cause individuals to be fast or slow metabolizers.
• Liver Disease & Age: Cirrhosis or extremes of age reduce metabolic capacity.

4. Elimination (Excretion)

Definition: Elimination is the irreversible removal of a drug and its metabolites from the body. The primary organ of excretion is the kidney, though biliary excretion, lungs, and sweat also contribute.

🌊 Renal Excretion Processes

1. Glomerular Filtration: Free drugs pass through kidney glomeruli into the urine filtrate.
2. Active Tubular Secretion: Transporters actively push drugs into the nephron tubule.
3. Passive Tubular Reabsorption: Lipid-soluble drugs can diffuse back into the blood unless ionized in the urine.

📈 Factors Affecting Elimination & Half-Life

• Renal Function ($GFR$): Kidney impairment drastically slows elimination, prolonging drug half-life ($t_{1/2}$) and increasing toxicity risk.
• Urine pH: Ionized drugs stay trapped in urine and are excreted faster.
• Half-Life ($t_{1/2}$): The time required for the concentration of a drug in the body to decrease by 50%.

💡 Mnemonic for ADME Overview: "All Dogs Met Eleanor"

Absorption (into blood) ➔ Distribution (to tissues) ➔ Metabolism (liver breakdown) ➔ Elimination (renal excretion)

6. Key Nursing Takeaways & Clinical Application

  • Assess Renal and Hepatic Function: Always review serum creatinine, creatinine clearance, AST/ALT, and bilirubin levels before administering drugs heavily metabolized or excreted by these organs.
  • Understand Route Differences: Remember that intravenous (IV) administration yields 100% bioavailability instantly, whereas oral drugs are subject to GI absorption barriers and hepatic first-pass metabolism.
  • Monitor Drug-Drug Interactions: Be vigilant for CYP450 inducers or inhibitors that can trigger unexpected drug toxicities or treatment failures.
  • Respect Food-Drug Interactions: Educate patients on specific administration requirements (e.g., taking drugs on an empty stomach vs. with food) to optimize or avoid altered drug absorption.
