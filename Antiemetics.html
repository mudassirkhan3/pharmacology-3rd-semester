Antiemetics (Anti-Nausea & Anti-Vomiting Agents)

Targeted pharmacological agents designed to prevent and treat nausea and vomiting by blocking various neurotransmitter receptors within the emetic center, chemoreceptor trigger zone (CTZ), and peripheral pathways.

1. Introduction to the Emetic Pathway & Control

Vomiting is a complex reflex coordinated by the medullary vomiting center, which receives neural input from the chemoreceptor trigger zone (CTZ), vestibular system, cerebral cortex, and visceral afferents in the gut. Antiemetics target specific receptors (such as serotonin, dopamine, neurokinin-1, and histamine) along these pathways to suppress nausea.

2. Classification & Prototype Drugs

💊 Serotonin 5-HT3 Receptor Antagonists

Prototypes: Ondansetron, Granisetron, Palonosetron. IMP FOR EXAM

Characteristics: First-line agents for chemotherapy-induced and postoperative nausea, blocking peripheral and central 5-HT3 receptors.

🧠 Dopamine Receptor Antagonists

Prototypes: Prochlorperazine, Promethazine, Metoclopramide. IMP FOR EXAM

Characteristics: Block dopamine D2 receptors in the CTZ. Metoclopramide also enhances upper GI motility as a prokinetic.

🛡️ Neurokinin-1 (NK1) Receptor Antagonists

Prototypes: Aprepitant, Fosaprepitant. IMP FOR EXAM

Characteristics: Central-acting agents preventing acute and delayed chemotherapy-induced nausea by blocking substance P.

⚡ Antihistamines & Anticholinergics

Prototypes: Meclizine, Dimenhydrinate, Scopolamine.

Characteristics: Highly effective for motion sickness and vestibular disorders by blocking inner ear and vestibular inputs.

3. Mechanisms of Action

Antiemetic classes selectively disrupt neural signaling at distinct sites in the central and peripheral nervous systems:

⚡ 1. 5-HT3 Blockade IMP FOR EXAM

💡 Simple Concept: Blocks serotonin released by gut damage from alerting the brain's vomiting center.
1
Peripheral Vagal Blockade: Selectively blocks serotonin 5-HT3 receptors on vagal afferent nerve terminals in the GI tract.
2
Central CTZ Blockade: Blocks 5-HT3 receptors centrally in the CTZ and solitary tract nucleus to stop afferent emetic signals.

🎯 2. Dopamine & NK1 Antagonism IMP FOR EXAM

💡 Simple Concept: Disables chemical trigger triggers (D2 in CTZ) and blocks Substance P to stop delayed emesis.
1
D2 Receptors (CTZ): D2 blockers inhibit dopamine signaling in the CTZ (Metoclopramide also adds prokinetic gastric emptying).
2
NK1 Receptors (Substance P): NK1 blockers cross the blood-brain barrier to block substance P at neurokinin-1 receptors, controlling delayed emesis.

🌊 3. Vestibular Suppression

💡 Simple Concept: Calms motion-induced signals traveling from the inner ear to the brain.
1
H1 & M1 Receptor Inhibition: Antihistamines and anticholinergics (like scopolamine transdermal patches) suppress hyperactive neural pathways.
2
Vestibular Deactivation: Cuts off sensory mismatch signals connecting the inner ear vestibular apparatus to the medullary vomiting center.

4. Clinical Uses & Therapeutic Indications

  • Prevention and treatment of Chemotherapy-Induced Nausea and Vomiting (CINV).
  • Management of Postoperative Nausea and Vomiting (PONV).
  • Treatment of motion sickness and vertigo (Meclizine, Scopolamine).
  • Hyperemesis gravidarum during pregnancy (often combination doxylamine and pyridoxine).
  • Gastroenteritis, migraine-associated nausea, and delayed gastric emptying (Metoclopramide).

💡 Mnemonic for Clinical Indications: "C.P.M.H."

C Chemotherapy-induced nausea and vomiting (CINV) prevention IMP FOR EXAM
P Postoperative nausea & vomiting (PONV) management
M Motion sickness, inner ear vertigo, & travel prophylaxis
H Hyperemesis gravidarum & acute gastroenteritis relief

5. Adverse Drug Reactions (ADRs)

⚠️ Neurological & Extrapyramidal Effects

Dopamine antagonists (prochlorperazine, metoclopramide) can cause Extrapyramidal Symptoms (EPS) such as akathisia, dystonia, parkinsonism, and tardive dyskinesia with long-term use. IMP FOR EXAM

⚠️ Cardiac & Anticholinergic Risks

5-HT3 antagonists: QT interval prolongation and risk of arrhythmias. IMP FOR EXAM
Antihistamines/Scopolamine: Typical anticholinergic side effects including dry mouth, blurred vision, urinary retention, and sedation.

💡 Mnemonic for Antiemetic Risks: "Q.E.S.T."

Q QT prolongation & cardiac arrhythmia risk (Ondansetron) IMP FOR EXAM
E Extrapyramidal symptoms / EPS (dystonia, akathisia from D2 blockers) IMP FOR EXAM
S Sedation & anticholinergic dry effects (dry mouth, blurred vision, urinary retention)
T Tardive dyskinesia risk with chronic metoclopramide therapy

6. Pharmacokinetics & Administration Guidelines

  • Timing for Efficacy: Antiemetics are significantly more effective when administered prophylactically before emetogenic stimuli (e.g., prior to chemotherapy infusion or travel) rather than as rescue therapy after vomiting begins. IMP FOR EXAM
  • Route Flexibility: Available in diverse formulations including oral disintegrating tablets (ODT), intravenous (IV), intramuscular (IM), rectal suppositories, and transdermal patches (scopolamine).

7. Key Nursing Takeaways & Patient Safety

  • Monitor Cardiac Rhythm: Assess baseline ECG and monitor QT intervals when administering high doses of IV 5-HT3 antagonists, especially in patients with electrolyte imbalances or concurrent heart medications. IMP FOR EXAM
  • Watch for Extrapyramidal Signs: Monitor patients taking dopamine antagonists for involuntary muscle spasms, restlessness, or facial grimacing, and keep diphenhydramine available as an antidote for acute dystonic reactions. IMP FOR EXAM
  • Educate on Sedation & Safety: Warn patients receiving antiemetics with sedative or anticholinergic properties (such as promethazine or scopolamine) to avoid driving or operating machinery.
  • Assess Hydration and Electrolytes: Monitor fluid balance, electrolyte levels, and signs of dehydration in patients experiencing persistent nausea and vomiting.
