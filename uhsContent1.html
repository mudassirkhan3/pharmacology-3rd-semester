Unit I: Introduction to Pharmacology

Welcome to the foundational bedrock of pharmacology! This unit introduces essential drug terminologies, historical milestones, medication purposes, sources, classifications, supply systems, legislation, and reliable reference resources to ensure safe clinical nursing practice.

1. Terminologies Related to Pharmacology Core Concept

Understanding clinical terminology is critical for safe medication administration and effective multidisciplinary communication:

Pharmacology

The comprehensive study of drugs, including their origin, chemical structure, properties, physiological effects, and mechanisms of action within living systems.

Pharmacokinetics (PK)

What the body does to the drug. Examines the four key phases: Absorption, Distribution, Metabolism, and Excretion (ADME).

Pharmacodynamics (PD)

What the drug does to the body. Examines drug-receptor interactions, signal transduction, and physiological responses.

Pharmacotherapeutics

The clinical application of drugs to prevent, diagnose, treat, or manage diseases and symptoms.

Toxicology

The study of poisons, adverse drug reactions, and harmful toxic effects of chemical substances.

Pharmacognosy

The study of medicinal drugs derived from natural sources (plants, minerals, and animals).

Pharmaceutics

The science of formulating active chemical drug compounds into safe, stable, and effective clinical dosage forms (tablets, syrups, injections).

2. Brief History of Pharmacology Historical Context

Pharmacology evolved from ancient herbal traditions into an exact biochemical science through major eras:

Ancient & Traditional Era

Early civilizations relied on trial and error with medicinal plants, barks, roots, and minerals (e.g., willow bark for pain, morphine from opium poppies, cinchona bark for malaria).

Emergence of Modern Science (19th Century)

Advances in organic chemistry allowed scientists to isolate pure active compounds (like morphine extracted from opium in 1805) rather than using crude plant mixtures.

Twentieth Century & Synthetic Era

Discovery of sulfonamides and penicillin revolutionized medicine. Development of synthetic drugs, receptor theory, and rigorous clinical trials established modern pharmacology.

Contemporary Genomic Era

Focus on pharmacogenomics (tailoring drug therapy based on genetic makeup), biotechnology, monoclonal antibodies, and targeted molecular therapies.

3. Purposes of Medication Clinical Application

Medications are prescribed and administered in healthcare settings for several distinct clinical purposes:

  • Prophylaxis (Prevention): Administered to prevent diseases or infections before they occur (e.g., vaccines, prophylactic antibiotics prior to surgery).
  • Diagnosis: Utilized in clinical tests and diagnostic imaging to identify abnormalities (e.g., radiocontrast dyes, diagnostic radiopharmaceuticals).
  • Treatment (Cure): Administered to eliminate pathogens or cure an active disease process (e.g., antibiotics curing bacterial pneumonia).
  • Palliation (Symptom Relief): Designed to relieve symptoms, pain, and suffering without curing the underlying disease (e.g., analgesics for terminal cancer patients).
  • Restoration (Maintenance): Used to restore normal body function or maintain optimal physiological status (e.g., insulin for diabetes, thyroid hormone replacement).

4. Sources of Medication High Yield

Drugs are sourced from diverse natural and synthetic origins in modern pharmaceutical manufacturing:

Plant Sources

Derived from leaves, roots, or bark. Examples: Digoxin from digitalis (foxglove), Morphine from opium poppy, Atropine from belladonna.

Animal & Mineral Sources

Extracted from animal tissues or inorganic mineral elements. Examples: Porcine/Bovine insulin, heparin, and mineral supplements like iron and magnesium sulfate.

Synthetic & Biotechnology

Synthesized in chemical laboratories or produced via recombinant DNA technology. Examples: Synthetic antibiotics, human recombinant insulin, monoclonal antibodies.

5. Classification of Drugs Core Concept

Drugs are categorized into groups to simplify clinical study and safe administration. Classification is typically based on three primary systems:

Body System Classification

Grouped by the specific organ system they affect. Example: Cardiovascular drugs (antihypertensives, antiarrhythmics), Central Nervous System drugs.

Therapeutic Use

Grouped by what condition they treat. Example: Antihypertensives (lower blood pressure), Antipyretics (reduce fever), Antibiotics (treat infections).

Mechanism of Action (MOA)

Grouped by how they work at the cellular or biochemical level. Example: Beta-blockers, ACE inhibitors, Calcium channel blockers.

6. Three Types of Drug Supply System Clinical Practice

Hospital and clinical facilities utilize different medication distribution systems to ensure efficiency, safety, and inventory control:

1. Floor Stock System

Commonly used emergency or routine supplies are kept in bulk quantities directly on the nursing unit. Pros: Immediate availability. Cons: Higher risk of medication errors and diversion.

2. Unit Dose Distribution System

Pharmacy dispenses individual, patient-specific, single-dose packages labeled for 24-hour cycles. Pros: Safest system, reduces nursing prep time, pharmacist reviews each dose.

3. Automated Dispensing Cabinets (ADCs)

Computerized secure medication storage units located on nursing units (e.g., Pyxis, Omnicell). Pros: Biometric security, real-time inventory tracking, barcode safety integration.

7. Drug Standards and Legislation Must Know

Government regulations and official compendia ensure that all marketed medications meet strict criteria for purity, potency, safety, and efficacy:

Official Drug Standards & Compendia

Reference standards like the United States Pharmacopeia (USP) or British Pharmacopoeia (BP) set official benchmarks for drug purity, strength, quality, and labeling standards.

Regulatory Agencies & Legislation

Agencies like the FDA (USA) or DRAP (Drug Regulatory Authority of Pakistan) enforce legislation governing drug approvals, clinical trials, prescription laws, and controlled substance schedules.

8. Resources to Collect and Utilize Drug Information Clinical Skills

Nurses must rely on validated, up-to-date, and authoritative reference sources before administering any medication:

  • Standard Nursing Drug Handbooks: (e.g., Nursing 2026 Drug Handbook or Davis's Drug Guide) providing clear nursing implications, dosages, and side effects.
  • Pharmacist Consultation: The most accessible and expert clinical resource for drug compatibility, dosing adjustments, and interactions.
  • Official Formulary & British National Formulary (BNF): Authoritative guides detailing approved institutional medications and prescribing guidelines.
  • Evidence-Based Databases: Electronic clinical decision tools such as Micromedex, UpToDate, and Lexicomp.

Key Nursing Takeaways

  • Master PK & PD: Understand that pharmacokinetics is what the body does to the drug, while pharmacodynamics is what the drug does to the body.
  • Always Verify Sources: Never rely on memory; cross-check dosages and compatibility using trusted nursing drug handbooks or hospital pharmacists.
  • Prioritize Patient Safety: Strict adherence to unit-dose distribution systems and automated dispensing safety checks prevents catastrophic medication errors.
