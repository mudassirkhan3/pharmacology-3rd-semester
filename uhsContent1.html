Unit I: Introduction to Pharmacology

Welcome to the foundational bedrock of pharmacology! This unit introduces essential drug terminologies, historical milestones, medication purposes, sources, classifications, supply systems, legislation, routes of administration, and reliable reference resources to ensure safe clinical nursing practice.

1. Terminologies Related to Pharmacology ⭐ High-Yield Focus

Understanding clinical terminology is critical for safe medication administration, avoiding lethal clinical errors, and maintaining precise multidisciplinary communication:

Pharmacology

The comprehensive study of drugs, including their origin, chemical structure, physical properties, physiological actions, biochemical mechanisms, and therapeutic applications within living systems.

Pharmacokinetics (PK)

What the body does to the drug. Examines the rate and extent of four sequential phases: Absorption, Distribution, Metabolism, and Excretion (ADME).

Pharmacodynamics (PD)

What the drug does to the body. Investigates molecular drug-receptor interactions, enzymatic alterations, intracellular signal transduction cascades, and biological effects.

Pharmacotherapeutics

The practical, clinical application of drugs to prevent, diagnose, treat, or manage diseases and restore homeostasis.

Toxicology

The branch concerned with poisons, adverse drug reactions (ADRs), overdosage, cellular toxicity, and antidotal treatments.

Pharmacognosy

The study of physical, chemical, and biological properties of crude medicinal drugs derived from natural plant, animal, or mineral sources.

Pharmaceutics

The science of converting pure active pharmaceutical ingredients (APIs) into stable, safe, and bioavailable dosage forms (tablets, syrups, injections).

📌 Exam Focus: Key Terminology Distinctions

• Pro-drug: An inactive precursor that requires metabolic conversion (usually in the liver) to become pharmacologically active (e.g., Enalapril → Enalaprilat; Levodopa → Dopamine).
• Agonist vs Antagonist: Agonists bind and activate receptors (Intrinsic Activity > 0); Antagonists bind and block receptors without activation (Intrinsic Activity = 0).
• Efficacy vs Potency: Efficacy (Emax) is the maximum physiological effect a drug can produce; Potency (EC50) is the concentration required to reach 50% of its maximum effect.

2. Brief History of Pharmacology Historical Milestones

Pharmacology evolved from ancient herbal traditions and trial-and-error folk remedies into an exact biochemical and genomic science through distinct eras:

Ancient & Traditional Era

Early civilizations relied on crude botanical remedies (e.g., Willow bark containing salicin for pain, Opium poppy for pain relief, and Cinchona bark containing quinine for malaria).

Emergence of Modern Science (19th Century)

In 1805, Friedrich Sertürner isolated pure Morphine from opium, sparking the extraction of pure active chemical alkaloids. Rudolf Buchheim founded the first independent laboratory of pharmacology in 1847.

Twentieth Century & Synthetic Era

In 1928, Alexander Fleming discovered Penicillin. The development of synthetic sulfonamides, organ-specific receptor theory (Paul Ehrlich's "magic bullet"), and double-blind clinical trials transformed modern medicine.

Contemporary Genomic Era

Focuses on Pharmacogenomics (customizing drug selection based on individual DNA profiling), recombinant DNA monoclonal antibodies, and targeted molecular therapies.

3. Purposes of Medication ⭐ Clinical Applications

Medications are prescribed and administered in healthcare settings for several distinct, high-yield clinical objectives:

  • Prophylaxis (Prevention): Administered to prevent diseases or infections before exposure (e.g., vaccines, preoperative prophylactic antibiotics like Cefazolin, antimalarial tablets).
  • Diagnosis: Utilized in clinical diagnostic procedures to confirm or locate pathological changes (e.g., radiocontrast dyes for angiography, barium meals, tuberculin PPD skin tests).
  • Treatment (Cure / Therapeutic): Administered to eradicate pathogens or eliminate the root cause of a disease (e.g., penicillin curing bacterial endocarditis).
  • Palliation (Symptom Relief): Designed to reduce symptom severity, pain, and suffering without curing the underlying terminal disease (e.g., IV morphine in end-stage oncology care).
  • Restoration (Maintenance / Replacement): Used to restore normal body function or replenish deficient physiological hormones/electrolytes (e.g., levothyroxine for hypothyroidism, insulin for Type 1 diabetes).

4. Sources of Medication ⭐ Exam Favorites

Drugs are derived from diverse natural, chemical, and biological sources in modern pharmaceutical manufacturing:

Plant Sources

Active botanical substances extracted from leaves, seeds, roots, or bark.

Key Examples:

  • Digoxin (Foxglove / Digitalis purpurea)
  • Atropine (Deadly Nightshade / Belladonna)
  • Quinine (Cinchona tree bark)

Animal & Mineral Sources

Extracted from living animal tissues, fluids, or inorganic Earth elements.

Key Examples:

  • Porcine/Bovine Insulin & Heparin
  • Premarin (Pregnant mare urine estrogen)
  • Ferrous Sulfate (Iron), Magnesium Sulfate

Synthetic & Biotechnology

Engineered via chemical synthesis or recombinant DNA genetic technology.

Key Examples:

  • Humulin (Recombinant Human Insulin)
  • Synthetic Antibiotics (Fluoroquinolones)
  • Monoclonal Antibodies (Infliximab)

5. Classification of Drugs Core Categorization

Drugs are categorized into distinct groups to streamline clinical study, prescribing guidelines, and administration safety:

Body System Classification

Grouped according to the specific anatomical organ system they primarily target.

Examples: Cardiovascular agents, Respiratory drugs, Central Nervous System depressants, Gastrointestinal medications.

Therapeutic Use

Grouped according to the specific clinical disease state or symptom they treat.

Examples: Antihypertensives (lower blood pressure), Antipyretics (reduce fever), Analgesics (relieve pain), Antiemetics (prevent vomiting).

Mechanism of Action (MOA)

Grouped by their precise molecular action at the cellular, receptor, or enzymatic level.

Examples: Beta-1 adrenergic blockers, ACE inhibitors, Calcium channel blockers, Proton pump inhibitors.

6. Three Types of Drug Supply System ⭐ Clinical Safety

Healthcare institutions utilize standardized medication distribution systems to control inventory, minimize drug theft, and ensure patient safety:

1. Floor Stock System

Bulk containers of routine medications are stored directly in nursing unit cabinets.

Pros: Immediate availability for emergency needs.
Cons: Highest risk of medication errors, drug contamination, and inventory loss.

2. Unit Dose Distribution System

Pharmacy dispenses single, individually packaged, patient-specific doses labeled for a 24-hour window.

Pros: Safest system overall; double-checked by a pharmacist; drastically reduces nursing prep errors.

3. Automated Dispensing Cabinets (ADCs)

Computerized automated dispensing units (e.g., Pyxis, Omnicell) located on the nursing unit.

Pros: Biometric login, real-time electronic MAR integration, barcode scanning, controlled drug tracking.

7. Routes of Drug Administration ⭐ Exam Core Topic

The chosen route dictates the onset speed, duration of action, and bioavailability of the drug. Routes are classified into four main pathways:

1. Enteral Route (GI Tract)

Oral (PO): Swallowed via mouth. Most convenient and economical. Subject to Hepatic First-Pass Metabolism (portal vein carries drug to liver first, reducing active drug concentration).

Sublingual (SL) & Buccal: Dissolved under the tongue or against the inner cheek. Highly vascularized; bypasses liver first-pass effect for immediate rapid systemic entry (e.g., Nitroglycerin for angina).

Rectal (PR): Suppositories or enemas. Useful for unconscious patients, severe vomiting, or pediatric fever spikes (partial first-pass bypass).

2. Parenteral Route (Injections)

Bypasses the digestive tract entirely via direct needle injection:

Intravenous (IV): Injected directly into veins. 100% Bioavailability (F=1). Fastest onset (seconds to minutes). Ideal for emergencies but irreversible!

Intramuscular (IM): Injected into deep muscle tissue at 90° angle (e.g., Ventrogluteal, Deltoid). Rapid absorption via muscular blood vessels.

Subcutaneous (SC/SQ): Injected into adipose layer at 45° to 90° angle (e.g., Insulin, Heparin). Slow, steady absorption.

Intradermal (ID): Injected into dermis at 10° to 15° angle (e.g., TB Mantoux tests, allergy testing).

3. Topical Route (Skin & Mucous Membranes)

Applied directly to local body surfaces or mucous linings:

Transdermal Patches: Provides slow, continuous, multi-day systemic absorption through intact skin (e.g., Fentanyl, Nitroglycerin, Nicotine patches).

Ophthalmic, Otic, & Nasal: Local drops or sprays. Clinical Tip: Hold inner canthus pressure during eye drop administration to prevent systemic absorption!

4. Inhalation Route (Respiratory Tract)

Gas or fine aerosolized droplets inhaled into bronchial airways via Metered-Dose Inhalers (MDIs), dry powder inhalers (DPIs), or nebulizers.

Advantage: Massive alveolar surface area provides instantaneous local bronchial delivery with reduced systemic side effects (e.g., Albuterol, Ipratropium).

8. Drug Standards and Legislation Must-Know Regulations

Statutory laws, drug regulatory authorities, and official compendia enforce strict quality control standards for drug purity, potency, bioavailability, and safety:

Official Drug Compendia & Standards

Official legal publications setting mandatory benchmarks for chemical purity, assay strength, packaging, and storage conditions:

  • USP (United States Pharmacopeia) / BP (British Pharmacopoeia)
  • National Formulary (NF)

Regulatory Legislation & Schedules

Statutory regulatory bodies (e.g., FDA in the USA, DRAP in Pakistan) enforce prescription laws and controlled substance tracking:

  • Controlled Substance Schedules (I to V): Schedule I = High abuse potential / No medical use (Heroin); Schedule V = Lowest abuse potential (Cough syrups with codeine).
  • Pregnancy Risk Categories (A, B, C, D, X): Category A = Safe; Category X = Strictly contraindicated (Teratogenic!).

9. Resources to Collect and Utilize Drug Information ⭐ Clinical Practice

Nurses are legally held responsible for verifying drug mechanisms, safe dosage ranges, compatibility, and side effects prior to administration using validated resources:

  • Nursing Drug Handbooks: (e.g., Davis's Drug Guide for Nurses, Lippincott Nursing Drug Handbook) containing concise nursing implications, IV push rates, and black box warnings.
  • Clinical Pharmacists: The premier interprofessional resource for complex drug compatibility, renal clearance adjustments, and drug-drug interactions.
  • Official Hospital Formularies & BNF: Institutional guides listing approved medications, therapeutic substitution rules, and local protocol guidelines.
  • Validated Online Databases: Electronic clinical software including Micromedex, Lexicomp, UpToDate, and Medscape.

💡 Mnemonic for the 5 Primary Rights of Medication Administration: "T-DRAM"

Time | Dose | Route | Amount/Patient | Medication. Always perform 3 safety checks against the Medication Administration Record (MAR) before giving any dose!

Key Nursing Takeaways & Exam Practice Points

  • Master Routes & Bioavailability: Intravenous (IV) administration provides instant onset and 100% bioavailability ($F=1$), while oral medications undergo first-pass hepatic metabolism.
  • Check Apical Pulse & Lab Values: Always assess specific physiological parameters before giving high-alert drugs (e.g., check apical pulse for 1 full minute before Digoxin; check serum potassium before Furosemide).
  • Prioritize Patient Safety: Unit-dose distribution and double-checking controlled substance counts prevent catastrophic errors and drug diversion.
