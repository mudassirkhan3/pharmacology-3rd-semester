Steroid Hormones & Their Antagonists

Endocrine-modulating agents designed to treat hormone-responsive malignancies (such as breast, prostate, and endometrial cancers) by either mimicking, blocking, or suppressing specific sex hormones or corticosteroids.

1. Introduction

Many common cancers rely on normal endogenous hormones (estrogens, androgens, and corticosteroids) to stimulate growth and proliferation. Hormone therapy uses agonist or antagonist agents to block receptor stimulation, suppress hormone synthesis, or induce apoptosis in hormone-sensitive tumor cells.

2. Classification & Prototype Drugs

Hormonal oncology agents are categorized by their specific endocrine pathway and mechanism:

  • Corticosteroids: Prednisone, Dexamethasone. (Induce apoptosis in lymphoid malignancies and serve as anti-inflammatory adjuncts / antiemetics.)
  • Estrogen Receptor Antagonists / Modulators (SERMs & SERDs): Tamoxifen, Fulvestrant. (Block estrogen receptors in breast tissue; tamoxifen acts as a partial agonist in bone/uterus, while fulvestrant is a complete degrader.)
  • Aromatase Inhibitors (AIs): Anastrozole, Letrozole, Exemestane. (Inhibit peripheral conversion of androgens to estrogen in postmenopausal women.)
  • Anti-androgens (Androgen Receptor Blockers): Bicalutamide, Enzalutamide, Apalutamide. (Competitively bind to androgen receptors to block testosterone action in prostate cancer.)
  • Gonadotropin-Releasing Hormone (GnRH) Analogs: Leuprolide, Goserelin (agonists); Degarelix (antagonist). (Suppress pituitary LH/FSH release, leading to castrate levels of testosterone or estrogen.)

3. Mechanism of Action (Detailed & Simple Explanations)

Endocrine agents disrupt tumor growth pathways through targeted receptor interactions:

🧬 SERMs (Tamoxifen)

The Receptor Competitor: Tamoxifen binds competitively to estrogen receptors on breast cancer cells, preventing natural estrogen from stimulating tumor growth.

However, it retains estrogenic agonist effects in bone and uterine tissue.

⚙️ Aromatase Inhibitors

The Estrogen Starvers: In postmenopausal women, adrenal androgens are converted to estrogen by the aromatase enzyme.

AIs block this enzyme completely, depriving estrogen-receptor-positive breast tumors of their primary fuel source.

🛡️ Anti-Androgens / GnRH Analogs

The Hormone Shutdown: GnRH agonists initially surge then shut down pituitary signals, halting gonadal hormone production.

Anti-androgens block any remaining circulating androgens from activating prostate cancer cell receptors.

4. Clinical Uses & Therapeutic Indications

Hormone therapies are central pillars in the long-term management of hormone-driven cancers.

  • Estrogen Receptor-Positive (ER+) Breast Cancer (via Tamoxifen, AIs, and Fulvestrant)
  • Advanced Prostate Cancer (via GnRH analogs and Anti-androgens)
  • Hematological Malignancies: Lymphomas and Acute Lymphoblastic Leukemia (via Corticosteroids)
  • Endometrial and Ovarian Cancers

💡 Mnemonic for Clinical Uses: "B-P-L"

Breast Cancer (ER+) | Prostate Cancer | Lymphomas & Leukemias (Corticosteroids)

5. Adverse Drug Reactions (ADRs)

By manipulating normal hormone balance, these therapies produce predictable endocrine deficiency symptoms and specific organ toxicities:

  • Menopausal Symptoms & Sexual Dysfunction: Hot flashes, night sweats, vaginal dryness (Anti-estrogens/AIs), and erectile dysfunction/loss of libido (Anti-androgens).
  • Bone Mineral Density Loss: Accelerated osteoporosis, bone demineralization, and fracture risk (due to estrogen/androgen suppression).
  • Thromboembolic Events: Increased risk of deep vein thrombosis (DVT) and pulmonary embolism (classic for Tamoxifen).
  • Metabolic & Uterine Effects: Hyperglycemia, weight gain, Cushingoid features (Corticosteroids); endometrial hyperplasia and uterine cancer risk (Tamoxifen).

💡 Mnemonic for ADRs: "H-O-T"

Hot flashes & hormonal imbalances | Osteoporosis & bone loss | Thromboembolism (DVT/PE) & Hyperglycemia

6. Pharmacokinetics

  • Administration: Primarily administered via oral tablets (Tamoxifen, AIs, Anti-androgens, Corticosteroids) or subcutaneous/intramuscular depot injections (GnRH analogs like Leuprolide).
  • Metabolism: Extensively metabolized in the liver via cytochrome P450 enzymes (particularly CYP3A4 and CYP2D6), making drug-drug interactions clinically significant.
  • Excretion: Eliminated predominantly through biliary/fecal routes and renal excretion, requiring caution in hepatic impairment.

7. Agent-Specific Complications & Clinical Phenomena

⚠️ The "Flare Phenomenon"

When starting GnRH agonists (Leuprolide) for prostate cancer, a transient spike in testosterone occurs during the first 1–2 weeks, which can temporarily worsen bone pain or urinary obstruction. This is managed by co-administering an anti-androgen beforehand.

🛡️ Bone Protection Strategies

Because aromatase inhibitors and androgen deprivation therapy significantly reduce bone density, patients are routinely prescribed baseline bone mineral density (DEXA) scans along with prophylactic calcium, vitamin D, and bisphosphonates or denosumab.

8. Key Nursing Takeaways & Patient Safety

  • Screen for Thromboembolic Red Flags: Educate patients taking Tamoxifen to report sudden chest pain, shortness of breath, or calf swelling immediately (signs of DVT/PE).
  • Monitor Bone Health & Supplementation: Ensure patients on AIs or hormone ablation therapies receive regular DEXA scans and adequate calcium/vitamin D intake.
  • Educate on Menopausal / Androgen Deprivation Effects: Prepare patients for expected hot flashes, mood fluctuations, and sexual health changes with supportive coping strategies.
  • Track Blood Glucose Levels: Monitor blood glucose closely in patients receiving high-dose corticosteroids, as steroid-induced hyperglycemia is common.
  • Manage the GnRH Flare: Verify that patients starting prostate cancer therapy with GnRH agonists have been covered with an anti-androgen to prevent tumor flare complications.
