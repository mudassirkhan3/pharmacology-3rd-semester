What is Pharmacokinetics?

Pharmacokinetics (PK) is defined as what the body does to a drug. It encompasses the quantitative study of drug movement through four distinct sequential phases: Absorption, Distribution, Metabolism, and Excretion (ADME).

A

Absorption

Entry into blood stream

D

Distribution

Movement into tissues

M

Metabolism

Enzymatic breakdown

E

Excretion

Removal from body

💡 Mnemonics for Nursing Exams

Pharmacokinetics = PK = "What Patient Does to Key (Drug)"
Pharmacodynamics = PD = "What Drug Does to Patient"

🩸

1. Absorption & Bioavailability

Absorption is the process by which a drug travels from its site of administration into the systemic circulation. Intravenous (IV) administration bypasses absorption entirely, resulting in 100% immediate bioavailability.

Bioavailability Formula (F)
F (%) = (AUC oral / AUC IV) × 100

Defined as the rate and fraction of unchanged drug that reaches systemic circulation.

Factors Affecting Drug Absorption:

  • Absorptive Surface Area: Microvilli in the small intestine provide a massive surface area, maximizing uptake.
  • Blood Flow (Vascularity): High perfusion maintaining steep concentration gradients accelerates absorption.
  • Lipid Solubility & Ionization: Non-ionized (lipophilic) drug molecules easily pass across biological lipid membranes.
  • First-Pass Metabolism: Hepatic degradation of oral drugs before reaching circulation.
🔍 Clinical Deep Dive: First-Pass Hepatic Effect

When taken orally, drugs pass from the gastrointestinal tract through the portal vein into the liver. Enzymes (CYP450) metabolize a fraction of the active drug before it ever reaches systemic target organs.

Nursing Example: Nitroglycerin undergoes >90% first-pass metabolism when swallowed. Therefore, it is administered sublingually (under the tongue) to bypass the liver and treat angina instantly.

💧

2. Distribution & Volume ($V_d$)

Distribution is the movement of drug molecules from blood plasma into interstitial fluid and intracellular body tissues.

Apparent Volume of Distribution ($V_d$)
Vd = Total Amount of Drug in Body / Plasma Drug Concentration (C₀)

A high Vd indicates extensive tissue binding; a low Vd indicates confinement to plasma.

Key Physiological Barriers to Distribution:

Physiological Barrier Anatomical Feature Clinical Nursing Impact
Blood-Brain Barrier (BBB) Tight endothelial junctions & astrocytic feet Only highly lipophilic drugs cross (e.g., general anesthetics).
Placental Barrier Fetal-maternal membrane capillary lining Teratogenic risk; assume drugs cross unless proven safe.
Plasma Protein Binding Reversible binding primarily to Albumin Bound drugs are inactive; free drugs are active. Hypoalbuminemia leads to toxicity.
🧪

3. Metabolism (Biotransformation)

Metabolism converts lipophilic (fat-soluble) drugs into polar, hydrophilic (water-soluble) metabolites so they can be excreted by the kidneys. The primary site of biotransformation is the liver.

Two Phases of Hepatic Metabolism:

Phase Primary Reaction Types Key Enzymes / Substrates
Phase I (Functionalization) Oxidation, Reduction, Hydrolysis Cytochrome P450 (CYP3A4, CYP2D6)
Phase II (Conjugation) Glucuronidation, Acetylation, Sulfation Covalent attachment of endogenous hydrophilic groups (e.g., Acetaminophen).
⚠️ Enzyme Inducers vs. Inhibitors

Inducers (e.g., Rifampin, Phenytoin): Accelerate drug breakdown $\rightarrow$ Decreases drug plasma levels $\rightarrow$ Treatment failure.
Inhibitors (e.g., Grapefruit Juice, Cimetidine, Erythromycin): Slow down breakdown $\rightarrow$ Increases drug levels $\rightarrow$ Toxicity!

🚽

4. Excretion & Half-Life ($t_{1/2}$)

Excretion is the permanent elimination of drugs and their metabolites from the body. The primary excretory organ is the Kidney (urine), followed by biliary/fecal, pulmonary, and exocrine secretions.

Elimination Half-Life ($t_{1/2}$)
t1/2 = (0.693 × Vd) / Clearance

The time required for total plasma drug concentration to drop by 50%. Takes 4 to 5 half-lives to achieve Steady State Concentration ($C_{ss}$).

Renal Excretion Mechanisms:

  1. Glomerular Filtration: Unbound free drug molecules filter across glomerulus.
  2. Active Tubular Secretion: Energy-dependent carrier transport in proximal tubules.
  3. Passive Tubular Reabsorption: Lipophilic non-ionized drugs re-enter blood stream unless trapped by urinary pH alteration (Ion Trapping).
📈

First-Order vs. Zero-Order Kinetics

Feature First-Order Kinetics Zero-Order Kinetics
Elimination Rate Proportional to drug concentration (Constant % per hour) Constant amount eliminated per unit time (independent of concentration)
Half-Life ($t_{1/2}$) Constant half-life Variable half-life (increases with higher dose)
Enzyme Capacity Enzymes NOT saturated Metabolizing capacity IS saturated
Examples Most clinical drugs (e.g., Amoxicillin, Metoprolol) Ethanol, Phenytoin, High-dose Aspirin
