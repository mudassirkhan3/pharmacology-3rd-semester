Pharmacokinetics is the quantitative study of what the body does to a drug from the moment it is taken until it is fully removed. Imagine swallowing a pill: it acts like a microscopic traveler passing through biological checkpoints controlled by four major steps: Absorption, Distribution, Metabolism, and Excretion (ADME).

1. Absorption, GI Mechanisms & Bioavailability

Absorption is the movement of a drug from its site of administration into the bloodstream. Think of a sponge absorbing water: the faster and more complete the absorption, the quicker the medicine works. When given Intravenously (IV), absorption is completely bypassed because the drug goes directly into the vein with 100% immediate bioavailability.

🧬 Mechanisms of GI Absorption ⭐ High-Yield Focus

When you swallow a tablet, drug molecules must cross the wall of the stomach or intestine to reach the blood:

  • Transcellular Passive Diffusion: The most common route. Lipophilic (fat-soluble) drugs glide directly through cell membrane lipids without needing energy.
  • Paracellular Transport: Small, water-soluble molecules slip through microscopic gaps (tight junctions) between adjacent cells.
  • Carrier-Mediated Transport: Special membrane carrier proteins actively grab drug molecules and carry them across.
  • Endocytosis / Pinocytosis: Cells engulf very large drug complexes inside small fluid vesicles.
💡 Real-World Example: An oral Paracetamol tablet dissolves in gut fluids, and its lipid-soluble molecules cross intestinal membranes via passive diffusion to enter the bloodstream.

📈 Factors Affecting Absorption ⭐ Key Calculation

What Speeds Up or Slows Down Absorption?

  • Surface Area: The small intestine has millions of tiny villi creating a surface area as huge as a tennis court, making it the primary site for drug absorption compared to the stomach.
  • Blood Flow: Rich intestinal blood flow rapidly sweeps absorbed drug away, maintaining a steep concentration gradient for continuous entry.
  • pH & Ionization: Non-ionized (un-charged) drugs are fat-soluble and absorb easily. Weakly acidic drugs (like Aspirin) stay un-ionized and absorb well in the acidic stomach.

Bioavailability Concept (F):

Bioavailability (F) is the exact fraction or percentage of the administered drug that reaches the main bloodstream active and unchanged.

Bioavailability (F) =
AUC oral AUC IV
× 100

Factors Lowering Bioavailability:

  • First-Pass Hepatic Metabolism: Oral drugs travel straight through the portal vein to the liver first, where liver enzymes break down a portion of the drug before it ever reaches the rest of the body.
  • Chemical Instability: Stomach acids destroy sensitive drugs (e.g., oral Insulin).
  • Solubility Limitations: Extremely hydrophobic or hydrophilic states restrict transport.
  • Chelation & Food Interactions: Binding to calcium/food prevents absorption.
💡 Real-World Example: Nitroglycerin suffers over 90% first-pass destruction if swallowed. That is why it is given sublingually (under the tongue) so it absorbs directly into blood vessels, bypassing the liver!
⚡ QUICK REMEMBER: Bioavailability & First-Pass Effect

• IV Route = 100% Bioavailability (No first-pass loss).
• Oral Route = Subject to First-Pass Hepatic Metabolism in the liver.

2. Distribution & Volume of Distribution

Distribution is the delivery of a drug from the bloodstream into tissues and fluid compartments throughout the body. Imagine a delivery train dropping cargo off at different stations; highly blood-perfused organs get their cargo first!

🌐 Key Factors Affecting Distribution

  • Perfusion / Blood Flow: Organs with high blood supply (brain, heart, liver, kidneys) receive drugs in seconds. Less perfused organs (fat, bone, skin) fill up slowly.
  • Capillary Permeability: Brain capillaries have extra tight junctions called the Blood-Brain Barrier (BBB). Only highly lipid-soluble drugs can cross into brain tissue.
  • Plasma Protein Binding: Many drugs bind to Albumin in blood. Bound drug molecules act like passengers stuck on a bus—they cannot leave blood vessels. Only free (unbound) drug is active and able to produce therapeutic effects!
  • Tissue Reservoir Binding: Some drugs accumulate in specific tissue storage sites.
  • Lipophilicity: Highly fat-soluble drugs deposit in adipose tissue.
💡 Real-World Example: Warfarin is 99% bound to plasma albumin. If another drug knocks Warfarin off albumin, free Warfarin spikes, triggering severe bleeding risk!

📦 Volume of Distribution (Vd) ⭐ Key Calculation

Volume of Distribution (Vd) is a calculated theoretical volume that reflects how widely a drug spreads out into body tissues versus staying in blood plasma.

Vd =
Total Amount of Drug in Body Plasma Concentration

Clinical Interpretation:

  • Low Vd (e.g., Heparin, ~3-5L): The drug stays confined inside the blood vessels because it binds strongly to plasma proteins or is very large.
  • High Vd (e.g., Digoxin, >500L): The drug leaves the blood and spreads extensively into deep body tissues, muscle, or fat reservoirs.
💡 Simple Analogy: A drop of red dye in a small cup of water stays dark red (Low Vd). That same drop dissolved in a huge swimming pool becomes pale and spread out (High Vd).

3. Metabolism (Biotransformation) & The CYP450 System

Metabolism (Biotransformation) is the body's process of chemically changing a drug, usually transforming fat-soluble drugs into water-soluble forms so the kidneys can easily flush them out in urine. The liver is the primary chemical processing plant.

🧪 Phase 1 Reactions

Functionalization: Liver enzymes use Oxidation, Reduction, or Hydrolysis to attach a small polar handle (-OH or -NH2) onto the drug molecule, making it more reactive for Phase 2.

💡 Example: Converting inactive Codeine into active Morphine via Phase 1 oxidation.

⚙️ The CYP450 System ⭐ Core Unit

The Cytochrome P450 family of liver enzymes carries out most Phase 1 reactions.

  • CYP Inducers (e.g., Rifampin, St. John's Wort): Speed up enzymes → drugs break down too fast → therapy fails.
  • CYP Inhibitors (e.g., Ketoconazole, Grapefruit Juice): Block enzymes → drugs build up → toxic risk.
💡 Warning: Drinking grapefruit juice while taking statins blocks CYP3A4, causing drug levels to surge and risk muscle damage!

🛡️ Phase 2 Reactions

Conjugation: The liver glues a heavy, highly water-soluble sugar or acid group (like Glucuronic Acid) onto the drug molecule, neutralizing it completely so it washes out into urine or bile.

💡 Example: Bilirubin and Morphine undergo glucuronidation to become water-soluble.

4. Kinetics of Elimination: First-Order vs. Zero-Order

How fast does the body remove a drug? Drug elimination speed follows two main mathematical patterns that determine how often a patient needs a dose.

💡 MEMORY TRICK: Zero-Order Drugs = "PEA"

Drugs cleared at a fixed, constant amount per hour because liver enzymes get overworked and saturated:
P → Phenytoin (Anticonvulsant)
E → Ethanol (Alcohol)
A → Aspirin (at high/toxic doses)

📈 First-Order Kinetics (Constant Percentage)

The body clears a constant fraction or percentage (e.g., 50%) of the remaining drug per hour. Drug half-life stays constant no matter how much drug is in the body.

Rate of Elimination = Constant × Drug Concentration

Over 95% of therapeutic medications follow First-Order kinetics.

💡 Simple Example: If you start with 100 mg of a drug with a 1-hour half-life: hour 1 leaves 50 mg; hour 2 leaves 25 mg; hour 3 leaves 12.5 mg.

📊 Zero-Order Kinetics (Constant Amount) ⭐ Clinical Risk

The body clears a fixed, constant amount (e.g., 10 mg/hour) regardless of blood concentration because processing enzymes are completely saturated.

Rate of Elimination = Constant (Saturated Rate)

Danger: Even small dose increases can cause steep toxicity spikes!

💡 Simple Example: The liver metabolizes about 1 standard alcoholic drink (~10-15g alcohol) per hour. Drinking faster saturates enzymes, raising blood alcohol level rapidly.

5. Elimination, Clearance & Half-Life

Excretion is the final, permanent removal of drug molecules from the body. While bile, sweat, saliva, and exhalation play minor roles, the kidneys are the main excretion organs.

🌊 Renal Clearance Processes

  • 1. Glomerular Filtration (GFR): Free, unbound drug molecules filter through nephron glomeruli into urine tubules.
  • 2. PCT Active Secretion: Active transporter pumps in the proximal tubule pump drugs directly into tubular urine.
  • 3. Tubular Reabsorption: Lipophilic un-ionized drugs tend to diffuse back into blood vessels. Ion Trapping (adjusting urine pH with bicarbonate) keeps drugs ionized so they stay trapped in urine and exit the body!
💡 Real-World Example: Penicillin is cleared very fast because renal active tubular secretion pumps it into urine rapidly.

⏱️ Clearance & Half-Life ⭐ Key Calculation

Clearance (CL) is the volume of plasma completely cleared of drug per unit of time (mL/min).

Half-Life (t½) =
0.693 × Vd Clearance (CL)

Clinical Rule: It takes 4 to 5 half-lives for a drug to reach a stable level in blood (Steady State - Css) or to be completely cleared from the body.

💡 Example: If a drug has a half-life of 6 hours, it takes about 24–30 hours (4–5 × 6 hrs) to reach steady state or to be fully cleared.

6. Design & Optimization of Dosage Regimens

🚀 Loading Dose ⭐ Core Dosing Rule

A single larger initial dose given at the start of treatment to raise plasma drug concentration to therapeutic levels rapidly during emergencies.

Loading Dose =
Target Concentration × Vd Bioavailability (F)
💡 Real-World Example: Giving a patient a large loading dose of Digoxin for acute atrial fibrillation to get heart rate under control immediately!

🔄 Maintenance Dose ⭐ Core Dosing Rule

Smaller, repeated doses given regularly to replace the exact amount of drug eliminated, keeping drug levels stable within the therapeutic window (Steady State - Css).

Maintenance Dose =
Target Conc. × Clearance × Dosing Interval Bioavailability (F)
💡 Everyday Analogy: A loading dose fills up a leaky bucket to the target line; the maintenance dose drips in just enough water to replace what leaks out!

7. Summary Table: First-Order vs. Zero-Order Kinetics

Feature First-Order Kinetics Zero-Order Kinetics
Elimination Rate Constant percentage (e.g., 50% removed per hour) Constant amount (e.g., 10 mg removed per hour)
Half-Life (t½) Constant (independent of drug concentration) Variable (increases as dose/concentration rises)
Enzymes Not saturated (plenty of free enzymes available) Saturated (capacity-limited, enzymes overworked)
Examples Over 95% of clinical drugs (e.g., Amoxicillin, Paracetamol) Phenytoin, Ethanol (Alcohol), High-dose Aspirin

8. Key Nursing Takeaways & Quick Revision

  • Check Renal & Liver Labs: Always check Serum Creatinine, GFR, and ALT/AST before giving drugs with a narrow safety margin (like Gentamicin or Digoxin).
  • Watch Serum Albumin Levels: Malnourished or elderly patients with low albumin have fewer binding sites. This means more free active drug in blood, leading to sudden toxicity!
  • Respect Steady State Timing: Remind patients that most chronic medications take 4 to 5 half-lives to reach full therapeutic effectiveness.
