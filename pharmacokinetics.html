Pharmacokinetics is the quantitative study of what the body does to a drug from the instant of administration to complete elimination. Think of an oral medication as a microscopic traveler passing through biological checkpoints governed by four core milestones: Absorption, Distribution, Metabolism, and Excretion (ADME).

1. Absorption, GI Mechanisms & Bioavailability

Absorption is defined as the transfer of a drug from its administration site into the systemic bloodstream. When given Intravenously (IV), absorption is completely bypassed with 100% immediate efficiency.

🧬 Mechanisms of GI Absorption ⭐ High-Yield Focus

When swallowed, drug molecules must cross intestinal epithelial membranes via distinct mechanisms:

  • Transcellular Passive Diffusion: Most common. Lipophilic (fat-soluble) drugs dissolve directly into and cross cell membranes down concentration gradients.
  • Paracellular Transport: Small polar water-soluble molecules pass through tight junction gaps between adjacent intestinal enterocytes.
  • Carrier-Mediated Transport: Transporter proteins actively shuttle nutrient-like drugs across membranes.
  • Endocytosis / Pinocytosis: Vesicles engulf very large macromolecular structures.

📈 Factors Affecting Bioavailability ⭐ Key Calculation

Determinants of Absorption Speed:

  • Surface Area: The small intestine's massive villi surface absorbs drugs vastly faster than the stomach.
  • Blood Flow: Rich perfusion maintains concentration gradients.
  • pH & Ionization: Weak acids absorb best in acidic stomach pH; weak bases prefer alkaline small intestine.

Bioavailability Concept (F):

Bioavailability (F) is the percentage fraction of administered drug reaching systemic circulation unchanged.

Bioavailability (F) =
AUC oral AUC IV
× 100

Factors Lowering Bioavailability:

  • First-Pass Hepatic Metabolism: Portal vein blood carries oral drugs to the liver first, where hepatic enzymes metabolize a large percentage before blood systemic entry.
  • Chemical Instability: Gastric acid destroys sensitive drugs (e.g., insulin).
  • Solubility Limitations: Extremely hydrophobic or hydrophilic states restrict transport.
  • Chelation & Food Interactions: Binding to calcium/food prevents absorption.
⚡ QUICK REMEMBER: Bioavailability & First-Pass Effect

• IV Route = 100% Bioavailability (No first-pass loss).
• Oral Route = Subject to First-Pass Hepatic Metabolism in liver.

2. Distribution & Volume of Distribution

Distribution is the reversible movement of a drug between blood circulation, interstitial fluid, and body tissue compartments.

🌐 Key Factors Affecting Distribution

  • Perfusion / Blood Flow: Highly perfused organs (brain, liver, heart) receive drug rapidly; bone and fat take longer.
  • Capillary Permeability: Brain microvessels have tight junctions creating the Blood-Brain Barrier (BBB), preventing hydrophilic drug entry.
  • Plasma Protein Binding: Bound drugs cling to Albumin. Only free (unbound) drug is pharmacologically active!
  • Tissue Reservoir Binding: Drugs like tetracycline bind calcium in bone tissue.
  • Lipophilicity: Highly fat-soluble drugs deposit in adipose tissue.

📦 Volume of Distribution (Vd) ⭐ Key Calculation

Volume of Distribution (Vd) is a theoretical fluid volume required to contain the total drug amount at plasma concentration levels.

Vd =
Total Amount of Drug in Body Plasma Concentration

Clinical Interpretation:

  • Low Vd (e.g., Heparin, ~3-5L): Confined to vascular plasma due to high plasma protein binding.
  • High Vd (e.g., Digoxin, >500L): Extensively distributes outside blood into deep tissue reservoirs.

3. Metabolism (Biotransformation) & The CYP450 System

Metabolism (Biotransformation) is the enzymatic alteration converting lipophilic compounds into hydrophilic metabolites easily excreted by kidneys. The liver is the chief organ.

🧪 Phase 1 Reactions

Functionalization: Involves Oxidation, Reduction, or Hydrolysis to introduce polar groups (-OH, -NH2), preparing the drug for Phase 2.

⚙️ The CYP450 System ⭐ Core Unit

The Cytochrome P450 enzyme family drives Phase 1 oxidation.

  • CYP Inducers (e.g., Rifampin): Speed enzyme work → lower drug levels → therapeutic failure.
  • CYP Inhibitors (e.g., Ketoconazole, Grapefruit Juice): Block enzymes → raise drug levels → toxicity.

🛡️ Phase 2 Reactions

Conjugation: Attaches heavy polar groups like Glucuronic Acid or Glutathione to make drug inactive and water-soluble for urine excretion.

4. Kinetics of Elimination: First-Order vs. Zero-Order

Drug elimination rates follow specific mathematical models essential for dosing accuracy.

💡 MEMORY TRICK: Zero-Order Drugs = "PEA"

Drugs eliminated at a constant rate due to saturated enzymes:
P → Phenytoin
E → Ethanol (Alcohol)
A → Aspirin (at toxic doses)

📈 First-Order Kinetics (Constant Percentage)

Eliminates a constant fraction/percentage per unit time. Half-life remains constant regardless of concentration.

Rate of Elimination = Constant × Drug Concentration

Most clinical drugs obey First-Order elimination.

📊 Zero-Order Kinetics (Constant Amount) ⭐ Clinical Risk

Eliminates a fixed, constant amount (mg/hr) per unit time because metabolic enzymes are completely saturated.

Rate of Elimination = Constant (Saturated Rate)

Danger: Small dose increases trigger huge toxicity spikes!

5. Elimination, Clearance & Half-Life

Excretion is the permanent removal of drug molecules from the body, primarily performed by the kidneys.

🌊 Renal Clearance Processes

  • 1. Glomerular Filtration (GFR): Free, unbound drug filters across glomeruli into nephron tubules.
  • 2. PCT Active Secretion: Transporters actively push drugs (penicillins) into tubular filtrate.
  • 3. Tubular Reabsorption: Un-ionized, lipophilic drugs diffuse back into blood. Ion Trapping via urine pH alteration prevents reabsorption.

⏱️ Clearance & Half-Life ⭐ Key Calculation

Clearance (CL) is the volume of plasma cleared of drug per unit time.

Half-Life (t½) =
0.693 × Vd Clearance (CL)

Clinical Rule: It takes 4 to 5 half-lives for a drug to reach steady state (Css) or to be completely eliminated from the body.

6. Design & Optimization of Dosage Regimens

🚀 Loading Dose ⭐ Core Dosing Rule

A high initial dose given to achieve target therapeutic concentration rapidly.

Loading Dose =
Target Concentration × Vd Bioavailability (F)

🔄 Maintenance Dose ⭐ Core Dosing Rule

Repeated smaller doses designed to balance elimination rate and maintain Steady State (Css).

Maintenance Dose =
Target Conc. × Clearance × Dosing Interval Bioavailability (F)

Examples Most clinical drugs Phenytoin, Ethanol, High-dose Aspirin

8. Key Nursing Takeaways & Quick Revision

  • Check Renal & Liver Labs: Always evaluate GFR, Creatinine, AST/ALT before administering drugs with narrow therapeutic indices.
  • Watch Albumin Levels: Low albumin (malnutrition) leads to higher free active drug levels and unexpected toxicity!
  • Respect Steady State Rules: Remember that full drug accumulation or clearance takes 4 to 5 half-lives.
