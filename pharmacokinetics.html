1 What is Pharmacokinetics?

Core Definition

Pharmacokinetics (PK) is the quantitative study of what the body does to a drug from the moment it enters the body until it is completely eliminated. While Pharmacodynamics deals with what the drug does to the body (receptor binding and therapeutic effects), Pharmacokinetics measures the movement, concentration, and lifespan of drug molecules inside body fluids and organs.

💡
EXAM FOCUS: PK vs. PD Pharmacokinetics = Body → Drug (Absorption, Distribution, Metabolism, Excretion).
Pharmacodynamics = Drug → Body (Mechanism of action, Receptors, Physiological effects).

3 Absorption

ADME Stage 1

Absorption is the process by which a drug transitions from its site of administration (e.g., gut mucosal membrane, muscle tissue, skin) into the systemic blood circulation.

Drug Dose Administered → Mucosal / Tissue Cross → Systemic Circulation

Mechanisms of Absorption

  • • Passive Diffusion: Uncharged, lipophilic (fat-soluble) drugs glide directly through cell lipid bilayers down a concentration gradient without using energy.
  • • Paracellular Transport: Small hydrophilic drugs pass through intercellular gaps between adjacent mucosal cells.
  • • Carrier-Mediated Transport: Transporter proteins actively pump drug molecules across cell membranes.
  • • Endocytosis: Cells engulf very large drug complexes inside fluid vesicles.

Factors Affecting Absorption

  • • Blood Flow: Rich intestinal blood flow rapidly sweeps absorbed drug away, maintaining a steep concentration gradient.
  • • Surface Area: The small intestine microvilli provide a massive surface area, making it the primary site of oral drug absorption.
  • • Lipid Solubility & pH: Non-ionized (un-charged) drugs stay lipid-soluble and absorb easily. Weak acids (e.g., Aspirin) remain un-ionized in acidic stomach fluid.
  • • Formulation: Liquids absorb faster than tablets; enteric coatings delay release until reaching the intestine.

4 Distribution

ADME Stage 2

Distribution is the reversible process by which a drug leaves the systemic blood stream and distributes into interstitial fluids, target tissues, and organ cells.

Plasma Protein Binding: Free vs. Bound Drug

Bound Drug (Inactive) Drug molecules attached to Albumin act like passengers locked inside a bus—they cannot leave blood vessels, cross membranes, or interact with receptors.
Free Unbound Drug (Active) Only free unbound drug is pharmacologically active, able to diffuse across capillaries into target tissues, interact with receptors, and undergo metabolism/excretion!

Physiological Barriers

  • • Blood-Brain Barrier (BBB): Endothelial cells in brain capillaries have extra tight junctions. Only highly lipid-soluble drugs cross into brain tissue.
  • • Placental Barrier: Lipophilic drugs cross fetal capillaries easily; nurses must exercise extreme caution during pregnancy.

Factors Influencing Distribution

  • • Organ Blood Perfusion: High-flow organs (heart, liver, kidneys, brain) receive drug rapidly. Fat, bone, and skin equilibrate slowly.
  • • Tissue Reservoirs: Certain drugs bind heavily in tissues (e.g., Tetracycline binds bone and teeth).

5 Metabolism (Biotransformation)

ADME Stage 3

Metabolism is the enzymatic conversion of a drug into a more polar (water-soluble) metabolite to facilitate renal excretion. The liver is the primary chemical processing plant.

HEPATIC BIOTRANSFORMATION PATHWAY
Lipophilic Drug → Phase I (CYP450) → Phase II (Conjugation) → Water-Soluble Excretable Metabolite

Phase I Reactions

Functionalization reactions using Oxidation, Reduction, or Hydrolysis to attach polar groups (-OH, -NH2), making molecules more reactive for Phase II.

CYP450 Enzymes

The Cytochrome P450 enzyme family carries out Phase I oxidations. CYP Inducers (e.g., Rifampin) speed up metabolism; CYP Inhibitors (e.g., Grapefruit juice) block metabolism.

Phase II Reactions

Conjugation reactions attach an endogenous polar acid (e.g., Glucuronic Acid) to completely neutralize the drug and make it excretable in urine or bile.

6 Elimination / Excretion

ADME Stage 4

Excretion is the permanent removal of drug molecules and metabolites from the body. The kidneys represent the primary pathway of drug excretion.

Renal Excretion Processes

  • • Glomerular Filtration: Free, unbound drug molecules filter through nephron glomeruli into urine tubules.
  • • Active Tubular Secretion: Transporters in the proximal tubule pump organic acids/bases actively into urine.
  • • Passive Tubular Reabsorption: Lipophilic un-ionized drugs diffuse back into blood vessels.

Ion Trapping Clinical Concept

Adjusting urine pH with IV Sodium Bicarbonate alkalinizes urine, keeping weak acid drugs (like Aspirin) ionized. Ionized drugs cannot cross membranes back into blood, trapping them in urine for rapid excretion during overdoses!

7 Bioavailability (F)

Core Metric

Bioavailability (F) is the percentage or fraction of an administered drug dose that reaches systemic circulation in an active, unchanged form.

Bioavailability (F) = [ Area Under Curve (Oral) / Area Under Curve (IV) ] × 100
IV Administration = 100% Bioavailability Direct venous injection completely bypasses gut absorption barriers and first-pass hepatic metabolism (F = 1.0 or 100%).
Oral Administration < 100% Reduced by incomplete gut absorption, gastric acid destruction, and liver first-pass metabolism.

8 Elimination Half-Life ($t_{1/2}$)

Dosing Time

Elimination Half-Life ($t_{1/2}$) is the time required for the plasma concentration of a drug to decrease by 50% (one-half) of its initial level.

The 4–5 Half-Lives Rule:
• It takes 4 to 5 half-lives for repeated dosing of a drug to reach stable therapeutic blood levels (Steady State - $C_{ss}$).
• It takes 4 to 5 half-lives for a drug to be >96% completely cleared from the body once dosing stops.

9 Drug Clearance (CL)

Elimination Rate

Clearance (CL) is the volume of blood plasma completely cleared of drug per unit of time (measured in mL/min or L/hr).

Clearance (CL) = Elimination Rate / Plasma Concentration

10 Volume of Distribution ($V_d$)

Tissue Partitioning

Volume of Distribution ($V_d$) is a calculated theoretical volume that relates the total amount of drug in the body to its concentration in blood plasma.

Vd = Total Amount of Drug in Body (Dose) / Plasma Concentration
Low $V_d$ (~3–5 L, e.g., Heparin) The drug is confined inside blood plasma vessels due to high plasma protein binding or large molecular size.
High $V_d$ (>50 L, e.g., Digoxin) The drug leaves blood plasma and distributes extensively into deep body tissues, skeletal muscle, or fat.

11 First-Pass Effect (Hepatic Metabolism)

High-Yield Landmark

The First-Pass Effect occurs when an orally ingested drug is absorbed through the intestinal mucosa into the portal vein and delivered directly to the liver, where liver enzymes metabolize a significant portion before it ever reaches systemic blood circulation.

ORAL DRUG FIRST-PASS PATHWAY
Oral Tablet → GI Tract Absorption → Portal Vein → Liver Enzymes Breakdown → Systemic Blood (Reduced Dose)

12 Factors Affecting Pharmacokinetics

Clinical Variables

Patient Factors

  • • Age: Neonates & elderly have lower liver/renal capacity.
  • • Genetics: Fast vs. slow acetylators.
  • • Pregnancy: Elevated blood volume & GFR.

Drug Factors

  • • Lipid Solubility: High lipophilicity aids membrane crossing.
  • • Molecular Weight: Large drugs stay in blood.
  • • Protein Binding: Bound fraction is inactive.

Disease Factors

  • • Hepatic Impairment: Slows metabolism & clearance.
  • • Renal Failure: Causes drug accumulation & toxicity.
  • • Heart Failure: Reduces organ perfusion.

13 Routes of Administration Comparison

Route vs. Bioavailability
Intravenous (IV) 100% immediate bioavailability. Emergency choice. Bypasses absorption & first-pass.
Oral (PO) Convenient, safe. Variable bioavailability due to GI breakdown and first-pass loss.
Sublingual (SL) Rapid absorption directly into systemic blood under tongue; bypasses first-pass metabolism.
Intramuscular (IM) Rapid absorption from rich muscle blood supply; suitable for depot formulations.

PHARMACOKINETICS IN 60 SECONDS
Bioavailability (F): 100% for IV drugs; reduced for oral drugs by first-pass liver metabolism.
Half-Life ($t_{1/2}$): Takes 4 to 5 half-lives to reach Steady State ($C_{ss}$) or full elimination.

17 Practice Questions & MCQs

Exam Self-Test

Q1: Which route of drug administration provides 100% immediate bioavailability?

✓ Correct! Intravenous administration delivers the drug directly into systemic blood, completely bypassing gut absorption and first-pass hepatic metabolism.

Q2: How many half-lives ($t_{1/2}$) are required for a drug to reach Steady State ($C_{ss}$) during chronic dosing?

✓ Correct! It takes 4 to 5 half-lives to reach steady state equilibrium or to be >96% eliminated from the body.
