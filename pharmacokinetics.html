1 What is Pharmacokinetics?

Core Concept

Pharmacokinetics (PK) is the quantitative study of what the body does to a drug over time. From the moment a drug is administered, the body acts upon it through four continuous biological processes: Absorption, Distribution, Metabolism, and Excretion (ADME).

Pharmacokinetics vs. Pharmacodynamics

• Pharmacokinetics (PK): What the body does to the drug (Movement, Concentrations, Lifespan).
• Pharmacodynamics (PD): What the drug does to the body (Receptors, Binding, Biological effects).

3 Absorption

ADME Stage 1

Absorption is the movement of a drug from its administration site into systemic blood circulation. When given Intravenously (IV), absorption is completely bypassed because the dose enters veins directly with 100% bioavailability.

4 Mechanisms of Transport

  • • Passive Diffusion: Uncharged, fat-soluble (lipophilic) drugs glide directly through cell lipid membranes down a concentration gradient without consuming ATP energy.
  • • Paracellular Transport: Small, water-soluble drugs pass through microscopic gaps between adjacent mucosal cells.
  • • Active Transport: Transporter proteins actively pump drug molecules across cell membranes against gradients.
  • • Endocytosis: Cells engulf large drug complexes inside fluid vesicles.

Factors Affecting Absorption

  • • Blood Flow: Rich intestinal blood flow rapidly sweeps absorbed drug away, maintaining a steep concentration gradient.
  • • Surface Area: The small intestine microvilli provide a massive surface area, making it the primary site of oral drug absorption.
  • • Lipid Solubility & pH: Non-ionized (un-charged) drugs stay lipid-soluble and absorb easily. Weak acids (e.g., Aspirin) remain un-ionized in acidic stomach fluid.
  • • Formulation: Liquids absorb faster than tablets; enteric coatings delay release until reaching the intestine.

4 Distribution

ADME Stage 2

Distribution is the reversible process by which a drug leaves systemic blood plasma and moves into interstitial fluids, tissues, and organ cells.

Plasma Protein Binding: Free vs. Bound Drug

Bound Drug (Inactive Reservoir) Drug molecules attached to Albumin cannot cross capiliary walls, exit blood vessels, or bind to receptors. They act as an inactive storage reservoir.
Free Unbound Drug (Active Fraction) Only free unbound drug can diffuse across capillary membranes into target tissues, interact with cell receptors, and produce therapeutic effects!

Special Biological Barriers

  • • Blood-Brain Barrier (BBB): Endothelial cells in brain capillaries have tight junctions. Only highly lipid-soluble drugs cross into brain tissue.
  • • Placental Barrier: Lipophilic drugs cross fetal capillaries easily; careful drug selection is necessary during pregnancy.

Key Influencing Factors

  • • Organ Perfusion: High-flow organs (heart, liver, kidneys, brain) receive drugs in seconds. Fat, bone, and skin equilibrate slowly.
  • • Tissue Binding: Certain drugs bind heavily in specific tissues (e.g., Tetracycline binds bone and teeth).

5 Metabolism (Biotransformation)

ADME Stage 3

Metabolism (Biotransformation) is the enzymatic alteration of a drug's chemical structure. Its primary goal is to convert fat-soluble (lipophilic) drugs into water-soluble (hydrophilic) metabolites that the kidneys can easily filter out in urine. The liver is the primary metabolic organ.

Phase I: Functionalization

Phase I reactions attach or unmask a polar functional group (-OH, -NH2, -SH) on the drug molecule.

  • • Oxidation: Catalyzed primarily by Cytochrome P450 (CYP450) enzymes in liver microsomes.
  • • Reduction: Addition of hydrogen atoms or loss of oxygen.
  • • Hydrolysis: Cleavage of chemical bonds using water (e.g., Aspirin breakdown).

Phase II: Conjugation

Phase II enzymes attach an endogenous polar substrate to form a highly water-soluble, inactive compound for urine or bile excretion.

  • • Glucuronidation: The most common Phase II reaction (glues glucuronic acid onto drugs).
  • • Sulfation & Acetylation: Converts molecules into excretable conjugates.
  • • Glutathione Conjugation: Essential for neutralizing toxic reactive drug metabolites.

CYP450 Induction vs. Inhibition Dynamics

CYP450 Enzyme Inducers Agents like Rifampin, Phenytoin, Carbamazepine, and St. John's Wort increase enzyme production. This speeds up drug metabolism, lowering plasma levels and causing treatment failure.
CYP450 Enzyme Inhibitors Agents like Ketoconazole, Erythromycin, Cimetidine, and Grapefruit Juice block CYP enzymes. This slows drug clearance, causing drug accumulation and increased toxicity risk.

6 Excretion (Elimination)

ADME Stage 4

Excretion is the final, permanent removal of unchanged drug molecules or metabolites from the body. The kidneys represent the primary excretion organ.

3 Renal Excretion Processes

  • • Glomerular Filtration: Unbound free drug molecules filter across nephron glomeruli into renal tubular fluid.
  • • Active Tubular Secretion: Transporters in PCT tubules actively pump organic acids/bases from blood into urine.
  • • Passive Tubular Reabsorption: Lipophilic un-ionized drugs diffuse back into blood vessels.

Ion Trapping Concept

Adjusting urine pH with IV Sodium Bicarbonate alkalinizes urine, keeping weak acid drugs (like Aspirin) ionized. Ionized drugs cannot cross back into blood membranes, trapping them in urine for rapid elimination during toxic overdoses!

7 Bioavailability (F)

Core Metric

Bioavailability (F) is the percentage or fraction of an administered drug dose that reaches systemic blood circulation in an active, unchanged form.

Bioavailability (F) = [ Area Under Curve (Oral) / Area Under Curve (IV) ] × 100

6 Key Factors Affecting Bioavailability

1. First-Pass Hepatic Metabolism: Extensive liver breakdown drastically decreases oral bioavailability.
2. Drug Solubility & Permeability: Hydrophilic drugs cross lipid membranes poorly; hydrophobic drugs dissolve poorly in GI fluids.
3. Drug Formulation: Particle size, binder excipients, and tablet disintegration time dictate dissolution rate.
4. Gastric Motility & Food Effects: High-fat meals delay gastric emptying, altering peak plasma concentrations.
5. P-Glycoprotein Transporters: Efflux pumps in intestinal mucosa pump absorbed drug back into the GI lumen.
6. GI Health: Malabsorption syndromes or surgical intestinal resection impair absorption.

8 Elimination Half-Life (t½)

Dosing Interval

Elimination Half-Life (t½) is the time required for the plasma concentration of a drug to decrease by 50% (one-half) of its initial level.

The 4–5 Half-Lives Rule:
• It takes 4 to 5 half-lives of repeated dosing for a drug to reach stable therapeutic blood levels (Steady State - Css).
• It takes 4 to 5 half-lives for a drug to be >96% completely cleared from the body once dosing stops.

9 Drug Clearance (CL)

Elimination Rate

Clearance (CL) is the volume of blood plasma completely cleared of drug per unit of time (measured in mL/min or L/hr).

Clearance (CL) = Elimination Rate / Plasma Concentration

10 Volume of Distribution (Vd)

Tissue Partitioning

Volume of Distribution (Vd) is a calculated theoretical volume that relates the total amount of drug in the body to its concentration in blood plasma.

Vd = Total Amount of Drug in Body (Dose) / Plasma Concentration
Low Vd (~3–5 L, e.g., Heparin) The drug is confined inside blood plasma vessels due to high plasma protein binding or large molecular size.
High Vd (>50 L, e.g., Digoxin) The drug leaves blood plasma and distributes extensively into deep body tissues, skeletal muscle, or fat.

11 First-Pass Effect

High-Yield Topic

The First-Pass Effect occurs when an orally ingested drug is absorbed through the intestinal mucosa into the portal vein and delivered directly to the liver, where hepatic enzymes metabolize a significant fraction before it reaches systemic blood circulation.

12 Factors Affecting Pharmacokinetics

Clinical Variables

Patient Factors

  • • Age: Neonates and elderly have reduced liver/renal clearance.
  • • Genetics: Fast vs. slow acetylators.
  • • Pregnancy: Increased blood volume & GFR.

Drug Factors

  • • Lipid Solubility: High lipophilicity aids membrane crossing.
  • • Molecular Size: Large drugs remain in blood plasma.
  • • Protein Binding: Bound drug fraction is inactive.

Disease Factors

  • • Liver Failure: Slows hepatic metabolism.
  • • Kidney Failure: Causes drug accumulation and toxicity.
  • • Heart Failure: Reduces organ perfusion.

13 Routes of Administration Comparison

Route vs. Bioavailability
Intravenous (IV) 100% immediate bioavailability. Emergency route. Bypasses absorption & first-pass.
Oral (PO) Convenient, safe. Variable bioavailability due to GI breakdown and first-pass loss.
Sublingual (SL) Rapid absorption directly into venous blood under the tongue; bypasses first-pass loss.
Intramuscular (IM) Rapid absorption from muscle blood supply; suitable for depot formulations.

15 Easy Mnemonics

Memory Aids
MEMORY TRICK 1

"ADME" Sequence

A → Absorption (Bloodstream Entry)
D → Distribution (Tissue Perfusion)
M → Metabolism (Hepatic Enzymes)
E → Excretion (Renal Output)

MEMORY TRICK 2

Zero-Order Drugs = "PEA"

Drugs eliminated at a constant amount per hour due to saturated enzymes:
P → Phenytoin
E → Ethanol (Alcohol)
A → Aspirin (high dose)

PHARMACOKINETICS IN 60 SECONDS
Bioavailability (F): 100% for IV drugs; reduced for oral drugs by first-pass liver metabolism.
Half-Life (t½): Takes 4 to 5 half-lives to reach Steady State (Css) or full elimination.
Clearance (CL): Volume of blood plasma completely cleared of drug per unit of time.
Volume of Distribution (Vd): High Vd = drug in tissues; Low Vd = drug in blood plasma.

17 Practice Questions & MCQs

Self-Test

Q1: Which route of drug administration provides 100% immediate bioavailability?

✓ Correct! Intravenous administration delivers the drug directly into systemic blood vessels, completely bypassing gut absorption barriers and first-pass hepatic metabolism.

Q2: How many half-lives (t½) are required for a drug to reach Steady State (Css) during repeated dosing?

✓ Correct! It takes 4 to 5 half-lives to reach steady state equilibrium or to be >96% completely eliminated from the body.
