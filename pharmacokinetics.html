Pharmacokinetics: ADME Principles, Kinetics & Dosage Design

Pharmacokinetics explores how the living human body impacts a therapeutic drug from administration to complete clearance. Think of a medicine capsule as a traveler navigating complex biological systems. This journey follows four foundational pillars known as ADME: Absorption, Distribution, Metabolism, and Elimination.

1. Absorption, GI Mechanisms & Bioavailability

Absorption is the movement of a drug from its site of administration into the systemic bloodstream. Intravenous (IV) administration bypasses absorption entirely, entering systemic circulation instantly with 100 percent efficiency.

🧬 Mechanisms of Absorption from the GI Tract

When an oral tablet is ingested, drug molecules cross the gastrointestinal (GI) epithelial barrier into circulation through specific transport pathways:

  • Transcellular Passive Diffusion: The primary route. Lipophilic (fat-soluble) drug molecules dissolve directly across lipid cell membranes of enterocytes from high to low concentration.
  • Paracellular Transport: Extremely small, polar, water-soluble molecules squeeze through microscopic tight junctions between adjacent intestinal cells.
  • Carrier-Mediated Transport: Transporter proteins actively shuttle nutrient-like drug molecules across the intestinal epithelium.
  • Endocytosis / Pinocytosis: The cell membrane engulfs very large macromolecular drugs into internal vesicles.

📈 Factors Affecting Absorption & Bioavailability

Key Factors Affecting Absorption:

  • Surface Area: Intestinal villi and microvilli provide a massive surface area that accelerates drug uptake far beyond stomach capacity.
  • Blood Flow: Rich splanchnic circulation maintains a steep concentration gradient.
  • pH and Ionization: Weak acids absorb best in the acidic stomach, while weak bases prefer the alkaline small intestine.

The Concept of Bioavailability (F):

Bioavailability defines the fraction of administered drug reaching systemic circulation unchanged.

Bioavailability (F) = (AUC oral / AUC IV) * 100

Influencing Factors: First-pass hepatic metabolism, GI degradation, solubility, and food interactions.

2. Distribution & Volume of Distribution

Distribution is the reversible transfer of drugs from the bloodstream into interstitial spaces and target body tissues.

🌐 Key Factors Affecting Distribution

  • Blood Flow (Perfusion): Highly perfused organs (brain, heart, liver) receive drugs rapidly, while adipose and bone receive them slowly.
  • Capillary Permeability: Fenestrated capillaries permit large molecules to pass, whereas tight cerebral junctions form the Blood-Brain Barrier.
  • Plasma Protein Binding: Reversible binding to albumin restricts drugs; only the free (unbound) fraction remains active at target receptors.
  • Tissue Binding & Lipophilicity: Lipophilic drugs concentrate heavily in adipose tissue; others bind bone mineral matrices.

📦 Volume of Distribution (Vd)

Volume of Distribution is the theoretical fluid volume required to contain all drug in the body at plasma concentration.

Vd = Total Amount in Body / Plasma Concentration

Clinical Examples:

  • Low Vd (e.g., Heparin, 3-5 L): Confined strictly to plasma due to high protein binding and large molecular size.
  • High Vd (e.g., Digoxin, 100s of L): Extensively sequestered in extravascular and adipose tissues.

3. Metabolism (Biotransformation) & The CYP450 System

Metabolism converts lipophilic compounds into hydrophilic metabolites in the liver for efficient renal elimination.

🧪 Phase 1 Reactions

Functionalization: Oxidation, reduction, or hydrolysis introduces polar groups (e.g., -OH), preparing the molecule for Phase 2 conjugation.

⚙️ The CYP450 System

The hepatic Cytochrome P450 enzyme superfamily drives biotransformation:

  • Inducers: Accelerate breakdown, risking therapeutic failure.
  • Inhibitors: Suppress metabolism, risking drug toxicity.

🛡️ Phase 2 Reactions

Conjugation: Covalently attaches bulky polar groups (glucuronide, sulfate, glutathione) to produce water-soluble excretable compounds.

4. Kinetics of Metabolism: First-Order vs. Zero-Order

📈 First-Order Kinetics

Mechanism: Elimination removes a constant percentage of drug per unit time. Half-life remains constant regardless of plasma concentration.

Rate = Constant * Concentration

📊 Zero-Order Kinetics

Mechanism: Elimination removes a constant amount per unit time due to enzyme saturation. Small dose increases can trigger sudden toxicity spikes (e.g., Phenytoin, Alcohol).

Rate = Constant (Saturated)

6. Elimination, Renal Factors, Clearance & Half-Life

Elimination is the permanent removal of drugs from the body, primarily orchestrated by the kidneys.

🌊 Renal Elimination & Excretion Factors

  • 1. Glomerular Filtration Rate (GFR): Filtration of unbound free drug molecules through glomerular capillaries into the nephron filtrate.
  • 2. Proximal Convoluted Tubule (PCT) Secretion: Active transporter pumps move specific drugs (e.g., penicillin) from peritubular capillaries into the tubule lumen.
  • 3. Distal Tubular Reabsorption: Lipophilic, un-ionized molecules diffuse back into blood. Urine pH manipulation (ion trapping) prevents reabsorption.
  • 4. Excretion by Other Routes: Biliary excretion into feces, pulmonary expiration of gases, and minor elimination via sweat and breast milk.

⏱️ Total Clearance & Half-Life ($t_{1/2}$)

Total Body Clearance (CL): Plasma volume completely cleared of drug per unit time by all organs.

CL = Rate of Elimination / Plasma Concentration

Half-Life ($t_{1/2}$): Time required for plasma drug concentration to decline by 50 percent.

t1/2 = (0.693 * Vd) / CL

Clinical Rule: Drugs reach steady-state or clear completely within 4 to 5 half-lives.

7. Design and Optimization of Dosage Regimens

Clinicians calculate precise dosing schedules to maintain therapeutic concentrations within the efficacy window while avoiding toxicity.

🚀 Loading Dose

Definition: An initial high dose administered to achieve immediate therapeutic plasma concentration without waiting 4-5 half-lives.

Loading Dose = (Target Conc * Vd) / Bioavailability

Application: Used in emergency situations when immediate drug efficacy is critical.

🔄 Maintenance Dose

Definition: Subsequent smaller doses scheduled at regular intervals to replace exactly the amount of drug eliminated.

Maintenance Dose = (Target Conc * Clearance * Interval) / Bioavailability

Steady State: Rate of drug input exactly matches elimination rate.

💡 Mnemonic for ADME Review: "All Dogs Met Eleanor"

Absorption ➔ Distribution ➔ Metabolism ➔ Elimination

8. Key Nursing Takeaways & Clinical Application

  • Monitor Renal and Hepatic Labs: Always review GFR, creatinine clearance, AST, and ALT prior to dosing adjustments in impaired patients.
  • Watch Protein-Binding Displacements: Competition for albumin sites can cause unexpected spikes in free active drug levels and toxicity.
  • Respect Zero-Order Risks: Drugs like phenytoin require rigorous therapeutic drug monitoring due to non-linear saturation kinetics.
  • Optimize Dosing Schedules: Adhere strictly to loading and maintenance protocols to maintain patient safety and clinical efficacy.
