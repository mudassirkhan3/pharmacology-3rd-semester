PHARMACOKINETICS

The quantitative study of drug movement through the body: Absorption, Distribution, Metabolism, and Excretion (ADME).

1. Overview of Pharmacokinetics

Pharmacokinetics (PK) describes what the body does to a drug over time. It measures how a drug enters systemic circulation, distributes to target tissues, undergoes enzymatic transformation, and is ultimately eliminated from the body.

Absorption GI tract to bloodstream
Distribution Bloodstream to tissues
Metabolism Hepatic transformation
Excretion Renal elimination

2. Absorption & Bioavailability

Absorption is the process by which a drug transitions from its site of administration into mucosal membranes and blood vessels. Intravenous (IV) administration bypasses absorption entirely, achieving 100% immediate bioavailability.

Transport Mechanisms

  • • Passive Diffusion: Uncharged, lipophilic drugs pass directly through lipid membrane bilayers down a concentration gradient.
  • • Paracellular Transport: Hydrophilic drugs pass through intercellular gaps.
  • • Active Transport: Membrane proteins transport molecules against gradients using metabolic energy.

Bioavailability (F) & First-Pass Effect

Bioavailability refers to the fraction of active drug reaching systemic circulation unchanged.

F = [ Area Under Curve (Oral) / Area Under Curve (IV) ] × 100

First-Pass Hepatic Metabolism: Orally ingested drugs travel via the portal vein straight to the liver, where hepatic enzymes partially metabolize the dose before it reaches systemic circulation.

3. Distribution & Volume of Distribution (Vd)

Distribution is the reversible transfer of a drug between systemic blood plasma and extracellular/intracellular tissue spaces.

Governing Physiological Factors

  • • Organ Perfusion: High-flow organs (heart, liver, brain, kidneys) receive drugs first; skeletal muscle and fat equilibrate slowly.
  • • Capillary Permeability: The Blood-Brain Barrier (BBB) tight junctions restrict entry to highly lipophilic drugs.
  • • Plasma Protein Binding: Drugs bind reversibly to Albumin. Only unbound (free) drug molecules leave blood vessels to bind target receptors.

Volume of Distribution ($V_d$)

$V_d$ is a theoretical fluid volume required to contain the total amount of drug in the body at the same concentration as in blood plasma.

Vd = Total Dose Administered / Plasma Concentration
  • • Low $V_d$ (~3–5 L): Drug remains confined inside blood vessels (e.g., Heparin).
  • • High $V_d$ (>50 L): Drug leaves blood plasma and partitions into muscle or tissue stores (e.g., Digoxin).

4. Metabolism (Biotransformation)

Metabolism is the enzymatic alteration of drug structures to form water-soluble (polar) metabolites suitable for renal excretion. The liver is the primary organ of biotransformation.

Phase 1: Functionalization

Introduces polar groups (-OH, -NH2) via Oxidation, Reduction, or Hydrolysis. Converts lipophilic drugs into more polar molecules.

CYP450 Enzyme System

Microsomal liver enzymes that oxidize drugs. Inducers (e.g., Rifampin) increase metabolism; Inhibitors (e.g., Ketoconazole) decrease metabolism.

Phase 2: Conjugation

Attaches an endogenous polar substrate (e.g., Glucuronic Acid) to form an inactive, water-soluble compound excreted in urine or bile.

5. Elimination Kinetics

First-Order Kinetics (Constant %)

The rate of drug elimination is directly proportional to plasma concentration. A constant percentage of drug is cleared per hour (95% of clinical drugs).

Rate = Constant (k) × Concentration

Zero-Order Kinetics (Constant Amount)

The elimination rate is fixed because metabolic enzymes are fully saturated. A constant mg amount is cleared per hour (e.g., Phenytoin, Ethanol, Aspirin).

Rate = Constant (Saturated Rate)

6. Renal Excretion, Clearance & Half-Life

Renal Excretion Mechanisms

  • • Glomerular Filtration: Unbound free drug molecules filter across glomeruli into urine tubules.
  • • Active Tubular Secretion: Transporters in PCT pump organic acids/bases into urine.
  • • Ion Trapping: Urine alkalinization (via Sodium Bicarbonate) keeps weak acids (e.g., Aspirin) ionized, preventing reabsorption and accelerating clearance.

Clearance ($CL$) & Elimination Half-Life ($t_{1/2}$)

Clearance is the plasma volume cleared of drug per unit time. Half-life is the time required for plasma drug concentration to drop by 50%.

Half-Life (t1/2) = [ 0.693 × Vd ] / Clearance (CL)

Steady State ($C_{ss}$): Repeated dosing requires 4 to 5 half-lives to reach stable therapeutic plasma concentrations.

7. Dosage Regimen Design

Loading Dose

An initial higher dose administered to rapidly achieve therapeutic plasma levels during medical emergencies.

Loading Dose = [ Target Conc. × Vd ] / Bioavailability (F)

Maintenance Dose

Repeated smaller doses given to replace the exact amount of drug eliminated, maintaining steady-state levels.

Maintenance Dose = [ Target Conc. × Clearance × Interval ] / F
