Pharmacokinetics: ADME Principles & Kinetics

Pharmacokinetics is the study of what the human body does to a drug. Imagine a pill as a traveler going on an incredible journey through your organs. This journey follows four main milestones known as ADME: Absorption, Distribution, Metabolism, and Elimination.

1. Absorption & Bioavailability

Absorption is the process by which a drug enters the bloodstream from its site of administration (such as the stomach, skin, or muscle). If you take a drug intravenously (IV), absorption is bypassed completely because the medicine goes straight into your veins.

🧬 Factors Affecting Absorption

  • Surface Area: The small intestine has villi and microvilli, giving it a massive surface area that absorbs drugs much faster than the stomach.
  • Blood Flow: Organs with rich blood supplies (like muscles or intestines) absorb injected or ingested drugs much quicker than poorly perfused areas (like fatty tissue).
  • Lipophilicity (Fat Solubility): Cell membranes are made of lipid bilayers. Fat-loving drugs slip across these membranes effortlessly.
  • pH and Ionization: Weak acids (like aspirin) are absorbed best in the acidic environment of the stomach, whereas weak bases prefer the alkaline small intestine.

🌟 The Concept of Bioavailability (F)

Bioavailability measures the percentage of the administered drug that reaches the systemic circulation in an active, unchanged form.

Bioavailability (F) = (AUC oral / AUC IV) * 100

Factors Affecting Bioavailability:

  • First-Pass Metabolism: Oral drugs must travel through the portal vein to the liver first. Liver enzymes can destroy a huge portion of the drug before it ever reaches the rest of the body (e.g., Nitroglycerin has such high first-pass breakdown that it cannot be taken as a standard oral pill).
  • Solubility & Formulation: Poorly soluble drugs or hard tablets that do not dissolve properly fail to absorb.
  • Chemical Instability: Some drugs are destroyed by stomach acid or digestive enzymes before absorption.

2. Distribution & Volume of Distribution

Distribution is the process where a drug leaves the bloodstream and travels into the interstitial fluids and body tissues (such as fat, muscle, and brain).

🌐 Key Factors Affecting Distribution

  • Blood Flow (Perfusion): Highly perfused organs like the brain, heart, and liver receive drugs within seconds. Tissues with low blood flow (fat, bone, skin) take hours.
  • Capillary Permeability: The liver and spleen have wide gaps in their capillaries, allowing large drugs to enter easily. In contrast, the brain has tight junctions (the Blood-Brain Barrier) that block polar drugs.
  • Plasma Protein Binding: Drugs can latch onto blood proteins like albumin. Only the free (unbound) drug is pharmacologically active and able to cross cell membranes to reach receptors. If a drug is 99 percent protein-bound, only 1 percent is active!
  • Tissue Binding: Some drugs accumulate heavily in specific tissues (e.g., tetracycline antibiotics bind strongly to calcium in bones and teeth).
  • Lipophilicity: Highly lipid-soluble drugs easily cross cell membranes and concentrate heavily in adipose (fat) tissue.

📦 Volume of Distribution (Vd)

Volume of Distribution is a theoretical concept that tells us how widely a drug spreads throughout the body relative to the blood plasma concentration.

Vd = Total Amount of Drug in Body / Plasma Concentration

Simple Examples:

  • Low Vd (e.g., Heparin, ~3 to 5 Liters): Stays almost entirely inside the blood plasma because it binds heavily to blood proteins and is too large to cross into tissues.
  • High Vd (e.g., Digoxin or Morphine, hundreds of Liters): Leaves the blood completely and concentrates heavily inside body fat, muscles, and organs, leaving very little drug floating in the blood plasma.

3. Metabolism (Biotransformation) & The CYP450 System

Metabolism is the chemical alteration of a drug by the body, turning fat-soluble (lipophilic) molecules into water-soluble (hydrophilic) compounds so the kidneys can easily excrete them in urine. This happens primarily in the liver.

🧪 Phase 1 Reactions

Modification: Phase 1 reactions involve oxidation, reduction, or hydrolysis. They introduce or unmask a polar functional group (like an OH group) on the drug molecule. This slightly increases water solubility and prepares the drug for Phase 2.

⚙️ The CYP450 System in Detail

The Cytochrome P450 enzyme family in the liver acts like a massive biological factory line breaking down drugs.

  • Inducers: Drugs like rifampin or St. John's wort speed up the factory. They increase enzyme production, causing other drugs to break down too fast (leading to treatment failure).
  • Inhibitors: Drugs like ketoconazole or grapefruit juice shut down the factory lines. They block CYP450 enzymes, causing other drugs to accumulate and reach toxic levels.

🛡️ Phase 2 Reactions

Conjugation: If a Phase 1 product is still not water-soluble enough, Phase 2 attaches a large, highly polar chemical group (such as glucuronic acid, sulfate, or acetate). This creates a completely inactive, highly water-soluble compound ready for elimination.

4. Kinetics of Metabolism: First-Order vs. Zero-Order

How do our bodies eliminate or metabolize drugs over time? There are two primary mathematical rules (kinetics) that govern this process.

📈 First-Order Kinetics (Exponential Elimination)

How it works: The body eliminates a constant percentage of the drug per unit time. If you have 100mg of a drug and 50 percent is eliminated every hour, you will have 50mg left after hour one, 25mg after hour two, and so on.

Rate of Elimination = Constant * Drug Concentration

Key Feature: The half-life is constant regardless of how much drug is in the body. Most drugs in pharmacology follow first-order kinetics at therapeutic doses.

📊 Zero-Order Kinetics (Constant Rate Elimination)

How it works: The body eliminates a constant amount of the drug per unit time, regardless of how high the concentration is. This happens when metabolic enzymes become completely saturated.

Rate of Elimination = Constant (Independent of Concentration)

Key Feature: If a person takes too much, the liver enzymes cannot keep up. A small increase in dose can cause a massive, dangerous spike in blood levels and toxicity.

Classic Examples: Alcohol, Aspirin (at high toxic doses), and Phenytoin.

6. Elimination (Excretion)

Elimination is the final removal of the drug and its metabolites from the body. The kidneys are the primary organs responsible for excreting water-soluble metabolites via urine, though the liver/biliary tract, lungs, and sweat glands also participate.

💡 Mnemonic for ADME Review: "All Dogs Met Eleanor"

Absorption (into blood) ➔ Distribution (to tissues) ➔ Metabolism (liver breakdown) ➔ Elimination (renal excretion)

7. Key Nursing Takeaways & Clinical Application

  • Check Organ Function Tests: Always review serum creatinine, creatinine clearance (GFR), AST, ALT, and bilirubin before administering medications cleared by the liver and kidneys.
  • Watch for Protein-Binding Displacement: If two highly protein-bound drugs are given together, they can kick each other off albumin binding sites, suddenly increasing free drug levels and causing toxicity.
  • Monitor Drug Interactions: Be extremely alert when combining CYP450 inducers or inhibitors with narrow therapeutic index drugs like warfarin or digoxin.
  • Understand Zero-Order Dosing Risks: Recognize that drugs following zero-order elimination (like phenytoin) require careful dose adjustments because small increases can lead to disproportionately high, toxic blood levels.
