Pharmacokinetics is the quantitative study of what the body does to a drug from the moment it enters the body until it is completely eliminated. When a patient takes a medication, it undergoes four sequential biological processes: Absorption, Distribution, Metabolism, and Excretion (ADME).

1. Absorption, GI Mechanisms & Bioavailability

Absorption is the movement of a drug from its site of administration into the systemic blood circulation. When a drug is given Intravenously (IV), absorption is completely bypassed because the drug is delivered directly into the venous system with 100% immediate bioavailability.

🧬 Mechanisms of GI Absorption EXAM FOCUS

For orally ingested medications to reach the bloodstream, drug molecules must cross cellular epithelial membranes:

  • Transcellular Passive Diffusion: The primary route for most drugs. Lipophilic (fat-soluble), uncharged molecules dissolve directly in cell lipid bilayers without consuming metabolic energy.
  • Paracellular Transport: Small, hydrophilic (water-soluble) molecules pass through tight intercellular junctions between adjacent epithelial cells.
  • Carrier-Mediated Transport: Specialized membrane proteins actively transport specific drug molecules across membranes (e.g., amino acid analogs).
  • Endocytosis / Pinocytosis: The cell membrane engulfs exceptionally large macromolecular drugs inside membrane vesicles.
💡 Real-World Example: Oral Paracetamol dissolves in gut fluids, and its lipid-soluble molecules cross intestinal membranes via passive diffusion to enter the portal vein.

📈 Factors Affecting Absorption KEY CALCULATION

Physiologic Factors Influencing Rate & Extent:

  • Surface Area: The small intestine possesses millions of microvilli, providing a massive surface area that makes it the primary site of oral drug absorption compared to the stomach.
  • Blood Flow: High splanchnic blood flow sweeps absorbed drug away, maintaining a steep concentration gradient across intestinal membranes.
  • pH & Ionization State: Non-ionized (uncharged) drug molecules are lipophilic and absorb readily. Weakly acidic drugs (e.g., Aspirin) remain largely un-ionized in acidic stomach fluid, favoring passive absorption.

Bioavailability Concept (F):

Bioavailability (F) is the percentage of an administered drug dose that reaches the systemic blood circulation in an active, unchanged form.

Bioavailability (F) =
AUC oral AUC IV
× 100

Factors Lowering Bioavailability:

  • First-Pass Hepatic Metabolism: Drugs absorbed from the GI tract pass through the portal system to the liver, where hepatic enzymes metabolize a fraction before reaching systemic circulation.
  • Chemical Instability: Destruction of acid-sensitive drugs by gastric juices (e.g., Penicillin G, oral Insulin).
  • Solubility Limitations: Extremely hydrophobic or hydrophilic states restrict transport.
  • Chelation & Food Interactions: Drug binding to calcium or food complexes prevents absorption.
💡 Real-World Example: Sublingual Nitroglycerin is placed under the tongue to absorb directly into systemic veins, completely bypassing first-pass hepatic metabolism.
⚡ QUICK REVISION: Bioavailability & First-Pass Effect

• IV Route = 100% Bioavailability (bypasses absorption & first-pass loss).
• Oral Route = Subject to incomplete absorption and First-Pass Hepatic Metabolism in the liver.

2. Distribution & Volume of Distribution

Distribution is the reversible process by which a drug leaves the systemic blood stream and distributes into interstitial fluid and intracellular tissues.

🌐 Key Factors Affecting Distribution

  • Organ Perfusion: Highly perfused organs (heart, liver, kidneys, brain) receive drug rapidly. Less perfused tissues (fat, bone, skin) equilibrate much slower.
  • Capillary Permeability: Specialized biological barriers like the Blood-Brain Barrier (BBB) contain tight junctions and P-glycoprotein efflux pumps, allowing only highly lipid-soluble drugs to enter brain parenchyma.
  • Plasma Protein Binding: Circulating drugs bind reversibly to plasma proteins (primarily Albumin). Protein-bound drug molecules cannot exit blood vessels or act on receptors. Only free (unbound) drug is pharmacologically active!
  • Tissue Binding: Certain drugs accumulate selectively in specific tissues (e.g., Tetracycline binds to bone and teeth).
  • Lipophilicity: High lipid solubility accelerates drug entry into tissue cells and fat stores.
💡 Clinical Alert: Warfarin is 99% bound to plasma albumin. If a co-administered drug displaces Warfarin from albumin, free Warfarin levels rise sharply, increasing bleeding risk!

📦 Volume of Distribution (Vd) KEY CALCULATION

Volume of Distribution (Vd) is a theoretical volume that relates the total amount of drug in the body to its concentration in blood plasma.

Vd =
Total Amount of Drug in Body (Dose) Plasma Concentration (C0)

Clinical Interpretation:

  • Low Vd (~3–5 L, e.g., Heparin): Drug is largely confined to plasma because it is heavily protein-bound or has a large molecular weight.
  • High Vd (>50 L, e.g., Digoxin, Chloroquine): Drug extensively distributes out of plasma into peripheral tissue reservoirs (muscle, adipose tissue).
💡 Simple Analogy: Adding dye to a small glass of water yields a high concentration (Low Vd). Adding that same dye to a large bucket yields a very low concentration (High Vd).

3. Metabolism (Biotransformation) & The CYP450 System

Metabolism (Biotransformation) is the enzymatic conversion of a drug into a more polar (water-soluble) metabolite to facilitate renal or biliary excretion. The liver is the primary organ of drug metabolism.

🧪 Phase 1 Reactions

Functionalization: Converts non-polar drugs into more polar molecules by introducing or unmasking functional groups (-OH, -NH2, -SH) via Oxidation, Reduction, or Hydrolysis.

💡 Example: Conversion of inactive prodrugs into active drugs (e.g., Codeine metabolized into active Morphine).

⚙️ The CYP450 System EXAM FOCUS

The Cytochrome P450 isoenzyme family carries out most Phase 1 oxidative reactions in liver microsomes.

  • CYP Inducers (e.g., Rifampin, Phenytoin): Accelerate enzyme activity → increased drug metabolism → reduced therapeutic efficacy.
  • CYP Inhibitors (e.g., Ketoconazole, Erythromycin, Grapefruit Juice): Block enzyme activity → decreased metabolism → drug accumulation & toxicity.
💡 Warning: Grapefruit juice inhibits CYP3A4, causing elevated plasma statin levels and increased risk of rhabdomyolysis!

🛡️ Phase 2 Reactions

Conjugation: Attaches an endogenous polar substrate (such as Glucuronic Acid, sulfate, or glutathione) to the drug or Phase 1 metabolite, forming a highly water-soluble, inactive compound for renal excretion.

💡 Example: Morphine undergoes hepatic glucuronidation to yield excretable polar conjugates.

4. Kinetics of Elimination: First-Order vs. Zero-Order

Elimination kinetics describe the rate at which drugs are cleared from the body, following two fundamental mathematical models.

💡 MEMORY TRICK: Zero-Order Drugs = "PEA"

Drugs eliminated at a constant, fixed amount per unit of time because metabolic enzymes become fully saturated:
P → Phenytoin (Anticonvulsant)
E → Ethanol (Alcohol)
A → Aspirin (at toxic/high therapeutic doses)

📈 First-Order Kinetics (Constant Percentage)

The rate of drug elimination is directly proportional to plasma drug concentration. A constant fraction or percentage (e.g., 50%) of drug is cleared per unit time.

Rate of Elimination = Constant (k) × Plasma Concentration

Elimination half-life (t½) remains constant regardless of plasma concentration. Over 95% of clinical drugs follow first-order kinetics.

💡 Simple Example: Starting with 100 mg of a drug (t½ = 1 hour): 50 mg remains after 1 hr, 25 mg after 2 hrs, 12.5 mg after 3 hrs.

📊 Zero-Order Kinetics (Constant Amount) CLINICAL RISK

The rate of elimination is constant regardless of plasma drug concentration. A constant amount (e.g., 10 mg/hour) of drug is cleared per unit time because metabolic capacity is saturated.

Rate of Elimination = Constant (Vmax - Saturated Rate)

Half-life is variable. Small increases in dose can lead to disproportionately large, dangerous spikes in plasma concentration!

💡 Simple Example: The liver metabolizes approximately 10–15 g of Ethanol per hour. Consuming alcohol faster saturates enzymes, causing blood alcohol levels to rise linearly.

5. Elimination, Clearance & Half-Life

Excretion is the permanent removal of unchanged drug or metabolites from the body. While biliary, pulmonary, and sweat excretion contribute, the kidneys represent the primary pathway of drug excretion.

🌊 Renal Clearance Processes

  • 1. Glomerular Filtration: Free, unbound drugs pass through glomerular fenestrations into the renal tubular lumen. Bound drugs are retained in plasma.
  • 2. Active Tubular Secretion: Active transporter systems in the proximal convoluted tubule pump organic acids and bases from blood into urine against concentration gradients.
  • 3. Passive Tubular Reabsorption: Lipophilic un-ionized drugs diffuse back into systemic circulation. Ion Trapping (alkalinizing urine with Sodium Bicarbonate for weak acids like Aspirin) keeps drugs ionized, preventing reabsorption and accelerating renal elimination.
💡 Clinical Example: Penicillin undergoes rapid active tubular secretion, resulting in a short half-life that requires frequent dosing unless combined with Probenecid.

⏱️ Clearance & Half-Life KEY CALCULATION

Clearance (CL) is the volume of blood plasma cleared of drug per unit of time (mL/min or L/hr).

Half-Life (t½) =
0.693 × Vd Clearance (CL)

Clinical Rule: It requires 4 to 5 half-lives for a drug to reach stable blood levels (Steady State - Css) during repeated dosing, or to be >96% eliminated from the body following drug discontinuation.

💡 Example: If a drug has a half-life of 6 hours, steady state will be achieved in 24 to 30 hours (4–5 × 6 hours).

6. Design & Optimization of Dosage Regimens

🚀 Loading Dose CORE DOSING RULE

A single larger initial dose administered to rapidly achieve target therapeutic plasma concentration without waiting 4–5 half-lives during acute medical emergencies.

Loading Dose =
Target Concentration × Vd Bioavailability (F)
💡 Clinical Application: Administering an intravenous Loading Dose of Digoxin or Phenytoin in emergency settings to establish rapid therapeutic blood levels.

🔄 Maintenance Dose CORE DOSING RULE

Repeated smaller doses administered continuously or periodically to replace the exact amount of drug eliminated, maintaining steady-state concentration within the therapeutic window.

Maintenance Dose Rate =
Target Concentration × Clearance Bioavailability (F)
💡 Everyday Analogy: A loading dose fills up a leaky bucket to the target water mark; the maintenance dose drips in just enough water to replace what leaks out!

7. Summary Table: First-Order vs. Zero-Order Kinetics

Feature First-Order Kinetics Zero-Order Kinetics
Elimination Rate Constant percentage (e.g., 50% cleared per hour) Constant amount (e.g., 10 mg cleared per hour)
Half-Life (t½) Constant (independent of drug concentration) Variable (increases as dose/concentration rises)
Enzymatic Capacity Unsaturated (capacity available) Saturated (capacity-limited)
Clinical Predictability Linear kinetics; proportional concentration rise Non-linear; small dose increases can cause toxicity
Prototypic Examples >95% of therapeutic drugs (Amoxicillin, Paracetamol) Phenytoin, Ethanol (Alcohol), High-dose Aspirin (PEA)

8. Key Nursing Takeaways & Quick Revision

  • Check Renal & Hepatic Function: Monitor Serum Creatinine, eGFR, and Liver Enzymes (ALT/AST) before administering narrow therapeutic index drugs (e.g., Gentamicin, Digoxin, Lithium).
  • Evaluate Serum Albumin Levels: Malnourished, burn, or elderly patients with hypoalbuminemia have fewer protein-binding sites, leading to increased free active drug levels and potential toxicity.
  • Educate Patients on Steady State: Counsel patients that chronic daily medications require approximately 4 to 5 half-lives to reach full therapeutic effect.
  • Recognize Ion Trapping: Urine alkalinization (e.g., IV Sodium Bicarbonate) traps acidic drugs like Aspirin in urine to manage toxic overdoses.
