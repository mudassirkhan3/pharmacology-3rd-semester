Pharmacokinetics: ADME Principles, Kinetics & Dosage Design

Pharmacokinetics is the study of what the human body does to a drug from the moment it enters until it is completely cleared. Think of a medicine pill as a traveler embarking on an intricate biological journey through your organ systems. This journey follows four core milestones known as ADME: Absorption, Distribution, Metabolism, and Elimination.

1. Absorption, GI Mechanisms & Bioavailability

Absorption is the movement of a drug from its site of administration into the systemic bloodstream. When a drug is administered intravenously (IV), absorption is bypassed entirely (100% bioavailability).

🧬 GI Absorption Mechanisms ⭐ High-Yield Focus

When you swallow an oral tablet, drug molecules must cross the gastrointestinal (GI) epithelial barrier to enter circulation through several distinct mechanisms:

  • Transcellular Passive Diffusion: The most common route. Fat-soluble (lipophilic) drug molecules dissolve directly into and slip across the lipid cell membranes.
  • Paracellular Transport: Extremely small, water-soluble polar molecules squeeze through microscopic tight junction spaces between adjacent intestinal cells.
  • Carrier-Mediated Transport: Transporter proteins embedded in the cell membrane actively carry nutrient-like drug molecules across the intestinal wall.
  • Endocytosis / Pinocytosis: The cell membrane engulfs very large macromolecular drugs, pulling them inside in vesicles.

📈 Bioavailability & Factors ⭐ Key Calculation

Key Factors Affecting Absorption:

  • Surface Area: Small intestine microvilli provide a massive surface area, absorbing drugs vastly faster than the stomach.
  • Blood Flow: Rich blood supply maintains a steep concentration gradient, accelerating absorption.
  • pH and Ionization: Weak acids absorb best in acidic stomach pH; weak bases absorb in the alkaline small intestine.

The Concept of Bioavailability (F):

Bioavailability represents the fraction (percentage) of an administered drug that reaches systemic circulation unchanged.

Bioavailability (F) = (AUC Oral / AUC IV) × 100

Factors Affecting Bioavailability: First-pass hepatic metabolism, chemical instability in GI acid, drug solubility, and food-drug interactions.

2. Distribution & Volume of Distribution

Distribution is the reversible transfer of a drug from the bloodstream into interstitial spaces and body tissues, such as fat, muscle, and the brain.

🌐 Key Factors Affecting Distribution

  • Blood Flow (Perfusion): Highly perfused organs (brain, heart, liver) receive drugs instantly; low-perfusion tissues (fat, bone) take longer.
  • Capillary Permeability: Leaky liver capillaries allow large molecules through; tight brain capillary junctions form the Blood-Brain Barrier (BBB).
  • Plasma Protein Binding: Bound drugs act as reservoirs; only the free (unbound) fraction is pharmacologically active.
  • Tissue Binding: Specific drug accumulation in tissue reservoirs (e.g., tetracycline binding to bone calcium).
  • Lipophilicity: Highly lipid-soluble drugs cross cell membranes rapidly and concentrate in adipose tissue.

📦 Volume of Distribution (Vd) ⭐ Key Calculation

Volume of Distribution is a theoretical fluid volume required to contain all the drug in the body at the same concentration as plasma.

Vd = Total Drug Amount in Body / Plasma Concentration

Practical Examples:

  • Low Vd (e.g., Heparin, ~3 to 5 L): Confined to blood plasma due to high protein binding and large molecular size.
  • High Vd (e.g., Digoxin, hundreds of Liters): Leaves vascular space and concentrates heavily in tissue stores and fat.

3. Metabolism (Biotransformation) & The CYP450 System

Metabolism is the chemical conversion of lipophilic (fat-soluble) drugs into hydrophilic (water-soluble) metabolites for efficient renal excretion, occurring primarily in the liver.

🧪 Phase 1 Reactions

Functionalization: Involves oxidation, reduction, or hydrolysis to introduce or unmask polar functional groups, preparing the drug for Phase 2.

⚙️ The CYP450 System ⭐ Core Focus

Primary enzymatic engine in hepatic biotransformation:

  • Inducers: Accelerate enzyme breakdown, causing therapeutic failure.
  • Inhibitors: Suppress CYP450, triggering dangerous toxicity build-up.

🛡️ Phase 2 Reactions

Conjugation: Attaches polar groups (like glucuronic acid or sulfate) to create inactive, water-soluble compounds ready for excretion.

4. Kinetics of Metabolism: First-Order vs. Zero-Order

The rate at which drugs are eliminated is governed by mathematical kinetic models.

📈 First-Order Kinetics

How it works: The body eliminates a constant percentage of the drug per unit time (e.g., 50% clearance every hour).

Rate of Elimination = Elimination Constant × Concentration

Key Feature: Half-life remains constant regardless of plasma concentration. Followed by most clinical drugs.

📊 Zero-Order Kinetics ⭐ Toxicity Alert

How it works: The body eliminates a constant amount of drug per unit time due to enzyme saturation.

Rate of Elimination = Constant Rate (Saturated)

Key Feature: Dose increases can trigger sudden toxicity surges. Examples: Alcohol, Aspirin, Phenytoin.

6. Elimination, Renal Factors, Clearance & Half-Life

Elimination is the irreversible removal of drugs from the body, carried out primarily by the renal system.

🌊 Renal Elimination Processes

  • 1. Glomerular Filtration (GFR): Unbound free drug molecules pass through glomeruli into renal filtrate.
  • 2. Proximal Tubule Secretion: Active transporter pumps push drugs (like penicillins) into the nephron tubule.
  • 3. Distal Reabsorption: Lipophilic, un-ionized drugs diffuse back into blood; ion trapping prevents reabsorption.
  • 4. Other Excretion Routes: Biliary, pulmonary, sweat, tears, and breast milk.

⏱️ Clearance & Half-Life Concepts ⭐ Key Calculation

Total Body Clearance (CL): Volume of plasma cleared of drug per unit time.

Total Clearance (CL) = Rate of Elimination / Plasma Concentration

Half-Life (t1/2): Time required for drug concentration to fall by 50%.

Half-Life (t1/2) = (0.693 × Vd) / CL

Rule of Thumb: Takes 4 to 5 half-lives to reach steady state or achieve total elimination.

7. Design and Optimization of Dosage Regimens

To achieve therapeutic success without causing toxicity, clinicians calculate precise dosing schedules based on pharmacokinetic parameters.

🚀 Loading Dose ⭐ Dosing Formula

Definition: Large initial dose given to rapidly reach target therapeutic concentration without waiting 4-5 half-lives.

Loading Dose = (Target Conc × Vd) / Bioavailability

When to Use: Emergency clinical crises requiring immediate therapeutic onset.

🔄 Maintenance Dose ⭐ Dosing Formula

Definition: Repeated smaller doses replacing eliminated drug to maintain steady-state window.

Maintenance Dose = (Target Conc × CL × Interval) / Bioavailability

Steady State: Achieved when administration rate equals elimination rate.

💡 Mnemonic for ADME Review: "All Dogs Met Eleanor"

Absorption (into blood) ➔ Distribution (to tissues) ➔ Metabolism (liver breakdown) ➔ Elimination (renal excretion)

8. Key Nursing Takeaways & Clinical Application

  • Monitor Renal and Hepatic Labs: Review GFR, creatinine, and liver enzymes to adjust dosing safely in organ impairment.
  • Watch Protein-Binding Displacement: Highly bound drugs displaced from albumin trigger sudden free-drug surges and toxicity.
  • Understand Zero-Order Dosing Risks: Recognize that zero-order drugs (phenytoin) require close monitoring due to saturation risk.
  • Respect Loading vs Maintenance Schedules: Administer initial loading doses accurately during urgent interventions before transitioning to maintenance schedules.
