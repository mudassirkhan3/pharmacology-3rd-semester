1 What is Pharmacokinetics?

Core Definition

Pharmacokinetics (PK) is the quantitative study of what the body does to a drug from the moment it enters the body until it is completely eliminated. While Pharmacodynamics deals with what the drug does to the body (receptor binding and therapeutic effects), Pharmacokinetics measures the movement, concentration, and lifespan of drug molecules inside body fluids and organs.

💡
EXAM FOCUS: PK vs. PD Pharmacokinetics = Body → Drug (Absorption, Distribution, Metabolism, Excretion).
Pharmacodynamics = Drug → Body (Mechanism of action, Receptors, Physiological effects).

3 Absorption

ADME Stage 1

Absorption is the process by which a drug transitions from its site of administration into the systemic blood circulation.

Mechanisms of Absorption

  • • Passive Diffusion: Uncharged, lipophilic drugs glide directly through lipid bilayers down a concentration gradient.
  • • Paracellular Transport: Small hydrophilic drugs pass through intercellular gaps.
  • • Carrier Transport: Transporter proteins actively pump drug molecules across cell membranes.

Factors Affecting Absorption

  • • Blood Flow: Rich intestinal blood flow maintains a steep concentration gradient.
  • • Surface Area: Small intestine microvilli provide a massive absorption surface area.
  • • Lipid Solubility & pH: Un-ionized drugs remain lipid-soluble and absorb easily.

4 Distribution

ADME Stage 2

Distribution is the reversible movement of a drug from systemic blood plasma into tissue spaces and cell interiors.

Plasma Protein Binding: Free vs. Bound Drug

Bound Drug (Inactive) Drugs bound to Albumin stay locked inside blood vessels and cannot interact with receptors.
Free Unbound Drug (Active) Only free unbound drug diffuses into target tissues to exert therapeutic effects!

5 In-Depth Drug Metabolism (Biotransformation)

Core Focus

Metabolism converts lipophilic (fat-soluble) drugs into polar, water-soluble compounds so they can be excreted by the kidneys. Without metabolism, lipophilic drugs would be continually reabsorbed in renal tubules and remain in the body indefinitely.

Phase I: Functionalization

CYP450 Driven

Introduces or unmasks polar functional groups (-OH, -NH2, -SH) on the drug molecule via Oxidation, Reduction, or Hydrolysis.

Key Enzymes: Cytochrome P450 (CYP450)
• CYP3A4: Metabolizes over 50% of clinically prescribed drugs.
• Inducers (e.g., Rifampin, Phenytoin): Increase CYP synthesis → lowers drug levels.
• Inhibitors (e.g., Ketoconazole, Grapefruit Juice): Block CYP activity → risks drug toxicity!

Phase II: Conjugation

Water-Soluble Link

Attaches an endogenous, highly polar molecule to the drug or Phase I metabolite, rendering it completely inactive and water-soluble for urinary excretion.

Primary Conjugation Pathways:
• Glucuronidation: Most common pathway (via UDP-glucuronosyltransferase).
• Glutathione Conjugation: Essential for neutralizing toxic metabolites (e.g., NAPQI).
• Sulfation & Acetylation: Converts compounds into easily excreted ionized salts.

6 Bioavailability (F) & Determinant Factors

High-Yield Topic

Bioavailability (F) is the fraction of an administered drug dose that reaches systemic circulation intact and active.

Bioavailability (F) = [ Area Under Curve (Oral) / Area Under Curve (IV) ] × 100

Key Factors Affecting Bioavailability:

1. First-Pass Hepatic Metabolism: Orally ingested drugs travel directly through the portal vein to the liver. Highly extracted drugs (e.g., Nitroglycerin) undergo massive liver breakdown before reaching general circulation.
2. Drug Solubility & Dissolution Rate: Hydrophilic drugs struggle to cross cell lipid membranes, while highly lipophilic drugs dissolve poorly in water fluids. A balanced partition coefficient optimizes $F$.
3. Chemical Instability in GI Fluids: Gastric acid destroys penicillin G; digestive enzymes degrade peptide drugs like Insulin, requiring parenteral administration.
4. Formulation & Excipients: Particle size, tablet compression force, and salt forms determine how quickly a dosage form dissolves and absorbs.

Clinical Case: Paracetamol Toxicity & Metabolism

NCLEX / Exam Case

The Scenario: A patient takes an overdose of Acetaminophen (Paracetamol). At therapeutic doses, 90% is safely conjugated via Phase II (Glucuronidation/Sulfation). However, in overdose, Phase II pathways become saturated!

Biochemical Breakdown:

1. Excess paracetamol is shunted into Phase I CYP2E1 oxidation, producing a toxic reactive metabolite: NAPQI.
2. Glutathione stores deplete rapidly while trying to detoxify NAPQI.
3. Unbound NAPQI binds liver proteins, causing acute hepatic necrosis.

Nursing Intervention: Administer N-acetylcysteine (NAC). NAC replenishes hepatic glutathione stores, neutralizing NAPQI and protecting liver tissue!

8 Practice Questions & MCQs

Exam Self-Test

Q1: Which CYP enzyme is responsible for metabolizing over 50% of prescription drugs?

✓ Correct! CYP3A4 is the predominant hepatic microsomal enzyme responsible for the metabolism of more than half of all clinical pharmaceuticals.

Q2: Why does Grapefruit Juice increase the risk of drug toxicity for certain oral medications?

✓ Correct! Grapefruit juice inhibits intestinal CYP3A4, reducing first-pass metabolism and increasing systemic drug bioavailability to toxic levels.
