What the body does to the drug

Pharmacokinetics is the study of what the human body does to a drug from the moment it enters until it is completely cleared. Think of a medicine pill as a traveler embarking on an intricate biological journey through your organ systems. This journey follows four core milestones known as ADME: Absorption, Distribution, Metabolism, and Elimination.

1. Absorption, GI Mechanisms & Bioavailability

Absorption is the movement of a drug from its site of administration into the systemic bloodstream. When a drug is administered intravenously (IV), absorption is bypassed entirely because it enters the blood stream directly with 100 percent efficiency.

🧬 Mechanisms of Absorption from the GI Tract ⭐ High-Yield Focus

When you swallow an oral tablet, drug molecules must cross the gastrointestinal (GI) epithelial barrier to enter circulation through several distinct mechanisms:

  • Transcellular Passive Diffusion: The most common route. Fat-soluble (lipophilic) drug molecules dissolve directly into and slip across the lipid cell membranes of intestinal enterocytes from high to low concentration.
  • Paracellular Transport: Extremely small, water-soluble polar molecules squeeze through microscopic tight junction spaces between adjacent intestinal cells.
  • Carrier-Mediated Transport: Transporter proteins embedded in the cell membrane actively carry nutrient-like drug molecules across the intestinal wall.
  • Endocytosis / Pinocytosis: The cell membrane engulfs very large macromolecular drugs, pulling them inside in vesicles.

📈 Factors Affecting Absorption & Bioavailability ⭐ Key Calculation

Key Factors Affecting Absorption:

  • Surface Area: The small intestine features villi and microvilli, providing a massive surface area that absorbs drugs vastly faster than the stomach.
  • Blood Flow: Rich blood supply at the site maintains a steep concentration gradient, accelerating absorption.
  • pH and Ionization: Weakly acidic drugs are absorbed best in the acidic stomach, whereas weakly basic drugs prefer the alkaline small intestine.

The Concept of Bioavailability (F):

Bioavailability represents the fraction (percentage) of an administered drug that reaches the systemic circulation unchanged.

Bioavailability (F) =
AUC oral AUC IV
× 100

Factors Affecting Bioavailability:

  • First-Pass Hepatic Metabolism: Oral drugs pass directly from gut blood vessels into the liver, where hepatic enzymes metabolize a significant fraction before reaching systemic blood circulation.
  • Chemical Instability in GI Fluids: Highly acidic gastric juice in the stomach or enzymatic gut secretions can chemically destroy unstable drug molecules (such as insulin) before absorption occurs.
  • Drug Solubility & Dissolution Rate: Hydrophilic drugs struggle to cross lipophilic intestinal membranes, whereas excessively hydrophobic drugs fail to dissolve in aqueous gut fluids, drastically limiting overall absorption.
  • Food & Drug Binding Interactions: Consuming food or concurrent medications can bind drug molecules into insoluble chelate complexes in the gut lumen, directly reducing total systemic drug entry.
⚡ QUICK REMEMBER: Bioavailability & First-Pass Effect

• IV Route = 100% Bioavailability (bypasses absorption).
• Oral Route = Subject to First-Pass Hepatic Metabolism (reduces active drug before reaching blood).

2. Distribution & Volume of Distribution

Distribution is the reversible transfer of a drug from the bloodstream into the interstitial spaces and body tissues, such as fat, muscle, and the brain.

🌐 Key Factors Affecting Distribution

  • Blood Flow (Perfusion): Highly perfused organs like the brain, heart, and liver receive drugs almost instantly. Tissues with low perfusion (fat, bone, skin) take much longer.
  • Capillary Permeability: Leaky capillary beds in the liver and spleen allow large drug molecules to pass. Conversely, tight endothelial junctions in the brain form the Blood-Brain Barrier, restricting hydrophilic drugs.
  • Plasma Protein Binding: Drugs can bind reversibly to plasma proteins like albumin. Only the free (unbound) fraction is pharmacologically active and capable of reaching target tissue receptors.
  • Tissue Binding: Certain drugs accumulate avidly in specific body reservoirs (e.g., tetracycline antibiotics binding to calcium in bones and teeth).
  • Lipophilicity: Highly lipid-soluble drugs readily cross cell membranes and concentrate heavily in adipose (fat) tissue.

📦 Volume of Distribution (Vd) ⭐ Key Calculation

Volume of Distribution is a theoretical fluid volume that would be required to contain all the drug in the body at the same concentration measured in blood plasma.

Vd =
Total Amount of Drug in Body Plasma Concentration

Practical Examples:

  • Low Vd (e.g., Heparin, ~3 to 5 Liters): Remains confined almost entirely within the blood plasma compartment because it binds heavily to plasma proteins and is too large to cross tissue barriers.
  • High Vd (e.g., Digoxin or Morphine, hundreds of Liters): Leaves the vascular system entirely and accumulates extensively in extravascular body tissues and fat stores.

3. Metabolism (Biotransformation) & The CYP450 System

Metabolism is the chemical conversion of lipophilic (fat-soluble) drugs into hydrophilic (water-soluble) metabolites, allowing the kidneys to excrete them efficiently. This process takes place primarily in the liver.

🧪 Phase 1 Reactions

Functionalization: Phase 1 involves oxidation, reduction, or hydrolysis, which introduces or unmasks polar functional groups (such as hydroxyl groups). This slightly increases water solubility and prepares the drug for Phase 2.

⚙️ The CYP450 System in Detail ⭐ Core Unit

The Cytochrome P450 enzyme family in the liver acts as the primary enzymatic engine for drug biotransformation.

  • Inducers: Drugs like rifampin or St. John's wort accelerate enzyme activity, causing companion drugs to break down prematurely (leading to therapeutic failure).
  • Inhibitors: Drugs like ketoconazole or grapefruit juice suppress CYP450 activity, causing companion drugs to accumulate to toxic levels.

🛡️ Phase 2 Reactions

Conjugation: If a Phase 1 metabolite is still insufficiently water-soluble, Phase 2 covalently attaches a large, highly polar chemical moiety (such as glucuronic acid, sulfate, or glutathione) to form an inactive, water-soluble compound ready for excretion.

4. Kinetics of Metabolism: First-Order vs. Zero-Order

The rate at which drugs are metabolized and eliminated is governed by mathematical kinetic models.

💡 MEMORY TRICK: Zero-Order Drugs = "PEA"

Drugs eliminated at a constant rate (due to saturated enzymes):
P → Phenytoin
E → Ethanol (Alcohol)
A → Aspirin (at toxic/high doses)

📈 First-Order Kinetics (Exponential Elimination)

How it works: The body eliminates a constant percentage of the drug per unit time. If you have 100mg and 50 percent is eliminated every hour, you have 50mg left after hour one, 25mg after hour two, and so on.

Rate of Elimination = Constant × Drug Concentration

Key Feature: The half-life is constant regardless of plasma concentration. Most clinical drugs follow first-order kinetics at therapeutic dosages.

📊 Zero-Order Kinetics (Constant Rate Elimination) ⭐ Clinical Risk

How it works: The body eliminates a constant amount of drug per unit time, regardless of how high the concentration is. This occurs when metabolic enzymes become saturated.

Rate of Elimination = Constant (Saturated Rate)

Key Feature: If dosage exceeds enzyme capacity, small dose increases cause sudden, dangerous toxicity spikes.

Classic Examples: Alcohol, high-dose Aspirin, and Phenytoin.

5. Elimination, Renal Factors, Clearance & Half-Life

Elimination is the irreversible removal of drugs and their metabolites from the body. While the liver aids biliary excretion, the kidneys are the primary organs of elimination.

🌊 Renal Elimination Processes

  • 1. Glomerular Filtration Rate (GFR): Unbound free drug molecules suspended in blood plasma pass through kidney glomeruli into the renal filtrate. GFR is the primary driver of renal drug filtration.
  • 2. Proximal Convoluted Tubule (PCT) Secretion: Active transporter pumps in the PCT actively push drugs (such as penicillins) from peritubular capillaries directly into the nephron tubule.
  • 3. Distal Tubular Reabsorption: Lipid-soluble, un-ionized drug molecules in the filtrate can diffuse backward across tubular cells into the bloodstream. Altering urine pH (ion trapping) can keep drugs ionized so they are excreted in urine rather than reabsorbed.
  • 4. Excretion by Other Routes: Includes biliary excretion into feces, pulmonary elimination of volatile gases, and excretion via sweat, tears, and breast milk.

⏱️ Clearance and Half-Life Concepts ⭐ Key Calculation

Total Body Clearance (CL): The volume of blood plasma completely cleared of a drug per unit of time by all eliminating organs combined.

Total Clearance (CL) =
Rate of Elimination Plasma Concentration

Half-Life (t½): The time required for the concentration of a drug in the body to decrease by exactly 50 percent.

Half-Life (t½) =
0.693 × Vd Clearance (CL)

Clinical Rule of Thumb: It takes approximately 4 to 5 half-lives for a drug to be completely eliminated or to reach a stable steady-state concentration (Css) with regular dosing.

6. Design and Optimization of Dosage Regimens

To achieve therapeutic success without causing toxicity, clinicians calculate precise dosing schedules based on pharmacokinetic parameters.

🚀 Loading Dose ⭐ Core Dosing Rule

Definition: A large initial dose given immediately to achieve the target therapeutic concentration rapidly, without waiting through 4 to 5 half-lives of regular dosing.

Loading Dose =
Target Concentration × Vd Bioavailability (F)

When to use: Used when a drug has a long half-life and an immediate medical crisis requires rapid symptom control (e.g., emergency anti-arrhythmic or antibiotic loading).

🔄 Maintenance Dose ⭐ Core Dosing Rule

Definition: Smaller, repeated doses given at regular intervals to replace the exact amount of drug eliminated from the body since the previous dose, maintaining a steady therapeutic concentration window.

Maintenance Dose =
Target Conc. × Clearance × Dosing Interval Bioavailability (F)

Steady State: Achieved when the rate of drug administration equals the rate of elimination, keeping blood levels stable within the therapeutic window.

8. Key Nursing Takeaways & Quick Revision

  • Monitor Renal and Hepatic Labs: Review GFR, serum creatinine, AST, ALT, and bilirubin levels to adjust dosages safely for patients with organ impairment.
  • Watch for Protein-Binding Displacement: Competing highly protein-bound drugs can displace each other from albumin binding sites, triggering sudden surges in free active drug and toxicity.
  • Understand Zero-Order Dosing Risks: Recognize that drugs following zero-order elimination (like phenytoin) require meticulous monitoring because small dosage increments can cause dangerous toxicity.
  • Respect Loading vs Maintenance Schedules: Ensure loading doses are administered fully when rapid therapeutic onset is clinically required, followed by steady maintenance scheduling.
